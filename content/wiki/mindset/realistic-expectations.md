## Why do expectations matter?

A plan that works slower than you expected can feel like a plan that fails. If you expect a habit to feel easy after three weeks, or the scale to drop a kilo every week, a normal result starts to look like a failure. This article gives the numbers from research so you can plan with them.

:::takeaway
Plan on **two to five months** before a new habit feels automatic [1]. In weight loss trials, people who finished lost **5 to 9%** of their weight in the first six months on average, after which it levelled off [2]. In studies of untrained men aged 18 to 40, about ten weeks of lifting added about **1.5 kg** of fat-free mass on average [5]. Your weight is normally higher after the weekend than later in the week [4].
:::

## How long does it take for a habit to stick?

Longer than the 21 days you often hear. A systematic review of 20 studies advises planning for at least **two to five months** before a new health habit becomes automatic [1]. Automatic means you do it without having to decide each time. Until then, it takes effort, and that is normal.

People also differ a lot. In one healthy-eating study in the review, only 23% of the people reached the point the researchers counted as a habit, and for them the median time was 59 days [1][8]. Individual estimates in that study ran from 4 to 335 days [1][8]. The study lasted 84 days, so everything past day 84 was projected from a curve [8]. If it takes you longer than a friend, that does not mean you are doing it wrong. [[consistency]] goes into how habits form and what helps.

## How does weight loss usually go?

It goes on for months, then levels off. A review of 80 weight loss trials with at least a year of follow-up found that programs built on a reduced-calorie diet gave moderate weight loss, and that weight levelled off at about six months [2]. Among people who completed the trials, programs with a diet (diet alone, diet and exercise, or meal replacements) gave an average loss of 5 to 8.5 kg, or 5 to 9% of starting weight, in the first six months [2]. At two, three and four years, about 3 to 4 kg (3 to 4.3%) was still off, and no group of people who finished the trials went all the way back to its starting weight [2]. Advice alone and exercise alone gave little weight loss [2]. Keep in mind who these numbers describe: after one year, on average 29% of the participants had dropped out, and most studies left them out of the results [2].

These are averages from trials in people with overweight or obesity, so some people lose much more and some less. Why the loss stalls is not settled. In a model of how the body responds to eating less, weight keeps going down for years if you keep eating the same reduced amount; it takes about a year to lose half of the eventual loss [3]. The authors of that model think the stall at about six months in trials more likely comes from people slowly drifting back towards their old intake [3]. See [[calorie-deficit]] for a sensible weekly pace and [[plateaus]] for what to do when it stalls.

## Can you keep the weight off?

More than one in six people manage it. In a national US survey of 14,306 adults who had ever been overweight or obese, 17.3% said they had kept off at least 10% of their weight for at least a year [6]. In the National Weight Control Registry, a group of people who had lost at least 13.6 kg and kept it off for a year, more than 87% were estimated to still keep off at least 10% after five and ten years [7]. Those people signed up because they had succeeded and reported their own weight, so the number is not your chance at the start. In the registry, more regain went with less exercise, less dietary restraint and weighing less often [7].

## Why does the scale go up when you did everything right?

Because your weight moves from day to day for more reasons than fat. In a study of 80 adults who weighed themselves daily, weight was highest on Sunday and Monday and fell during the week. The authors call this weekly pattern normal, and not a sign of weight gain [4]. Weigh yourself at the same time each day, compare weekly averages, and look at the trend over several weeks. See [[progress-tracking]].

## How fast can you build muscle?

Slowly. A meta-analysis of 111 studies in healthy men aged 18 to 40 found that resistance training programs of 4 to 24 weeks, about ten weeks on average, increased fat-free mass by 1.54 kg in untrained men and 0.98 kg in men who already trained [5]. Fat-free mass is everything except fat, so it includes water and glycogen, not only muscle. The review did not include women or older adults, so these numbers do not tell them what to expect. For a target rate of weight gain in a bulk, see [[bulking]]; for how muscle grows, see [[hypertrophy]].

## How do you plan with these numbers?

- Think in months, not weeks. Give a habit two to five months [1], and remember that weight loss in trials went on for about six months [2].
- Expect the pace to change. A stall after a few months is common [2].
- Judge trends, not days. A higher weigh-in after the weekend is normal [4].
- Pick goals you control. You decide whether you train three times this week; you do not decide what the scale says on Monday. See [[goal-setting]].

:::checklist
- Habits | Give a new habit at least two to five months [1].
- Fat loss | Expect the loss to slow down after about six months [2].
- Muscle | In untrained young men, about 1.5 kg of fat-free mass in ten weeks was the average [5].
- Scale | Weigh at the same time of day and compare weekly averages.
- Long term | Keep the habits that got you there [7].
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Judging a habit after 21 days** - The review advises two to five months [1].
- **Calling the slowdown a failure** - Weight levelled off at about six months in the trials [2].
- **Reacting to one weigh-in** - Weight is normally higher after the weekend [4].
- **Comparing yourself with someone else** - In one study, estimates of how long a habit took ranged from 4 to 335 days between people [8].
- **Stopping what worked once you reach your goal** - Regain went with less exercise and less self-weighing in the registry [7].
