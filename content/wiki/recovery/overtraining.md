## What is overtraining?
Overtraining is a state of chronic performance decline and fatigue that does not resolve with normal rest. It follows prolonged imbalance between stress and recovery and differs from planned, reversible overreaching.

:::takeaway
Prevent by deloading every **4-10 weeks**, cutting volume **30-50%** when fatigue and performance dip.
:::

## How does it work?
Excessive [[volume]], high [[intensity]], inadequate [[sleep]], and low [[calories]] accumulate physiological and psychological stress. Hormonal shifts, elevated cortisol, and nervous system fatigue reduce performance and increase injury risk. Monitoring trends in mood, sleep, soreness, and bar speed helps catch issues early before they become long layoffs.
Because the condition develops over weeks, small flags—like rising resting heart rate, poor appetite, or persistent irritability—should trigger earlier intervention.

## Practical application
Log RPE, readiness, and bar speed to spot declines. Use planned deloads and extra rest days when signs appear. If symptoms persist—irritability, elevated resting heart rate, persistent soreness—slash sets, lower load, and increase food and sleep until performance rebounds. Address life stress where possible and reintroduce training stress gradually once recovery metrics normalize.
Coordinate nutrition with recovery: raise carbs on hard weeks, add protein to cover 1.6-2.2 g/kg, and ensure hydration matches higher workloads. When returning, rebuild with submaximal loads and shorter sessions before restoring full [[volume]].

:::checklist
- Monitor | Track RPE trends, bar speed, mood, and resting HR weekly.
- Deload | Drop volume **30-50%** every **4-10 weeks** or at first sustained dip in performance.
- Reset | If signs persist, cut load and sets further and prioritize sleep and calories until metrics improve.
:::

## Common mistakes
- Ignoring early signals and pushing harder, deepening the fatigue cycle.
- Adding stimulants like [[caffeine]] to mask poor recovery instead of adjusting training and nutrition.
- Dropping intensity but keeping high set counts, still driving fatigue while stalling skill practice.
