## What is DOMS?
Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness (DOMS) is the stiffness and soreness that peaks 24-72 hours after unaccustomed or high eccentric [[volume]]. It is a sign of muscle damage and inflammation, not a direct indicator of growth.

:::takeaway
Reduce DOMS by progressing **sets/reps/load gradually**, keeping most sets at **1-3 RIR**, and spreading work across **2-3** weekly sessions.
:::

## How does it work?
Novel stress and eccentrics cause microtrauma and inflammation, leading to soreness and temporary performance drops. As you adapt, soreness diminishes at the same workload. Persistent severe DOMS can impair movement quality, [[sleep]], and training frequency, slowing [[progressive-overload]].
DOMS is influenced by conditioning, tempo, and range of motion; large ROM and slow eccentrics raise soreness, while gradual exposure reduces it over time.

## Practical application
When starting new lifts or higher volumes, scale in over 1-2 weeks. Emphasize controlled eccentrics without excessive tempos. Keep weekly [[volume]] within 10-20 sets per muscle and distribute across the week. Use light movement, walking, or cycling to increase blood flow on sore days. Ensure protein (1.6-2.2 g/kg), carbs around training, hydration, and sufficient sleep to support recovery.
If soreness is severe, trim a few sets or reduce eccentric tempo temporarily, then rebuild as tolerance improves.
Brief modalities like warm showers, light stretching, or foam rolling can reduce discomfort temporarily, but progression and load management remain the primary fixes.

:::checklist
- Gradual load | Add sets/reps/load stepwise; avoid sudden spikes in eccentrics.
- Distribution | Split weekly work into **2-3** sessions to limit severe soreness.
- Active recovery | Use light movement and adequate nutrition/hydration to speed relief.
:::

## Common mistakes
- Treating soreness as the goal and chasing it with excessive eccentrics or volume.
- Jumping volumes or new exercises too quickly, disrupting technique and recovery.
- Letting severe DOMS reduce range of motion and bar speed, flattening training quality.
