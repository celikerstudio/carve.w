## What are fitness myths?
Fitness myths are common misconceptions—like spot reduction or “no pain, no gain” always— that misguide training and nutrition choices. Dispelling them improves results and safety.

:::takeaway
Use evidence-based principles: progressive overload, adequate [[protein]], matched [[calories]], and quality [[sleep]]—not fads or extreme rules.
:::

## How does it work?
Myths often ignore individual context and the fundamentals of adaptation. Evidence-based training uses measurable [[volume]] and [[intensity]] progression, plus recovery, to drive change. Understanding real drivers prevents wasted effort and injury from gimmicks or extreme practices.
Myths spread because they are simple, promise shortcuts, or sound intuitive (“sweating means fat loss”). Knowing the mechanisms—calories drive fat loss, tension plus recovery drives [[hypertrophy]]—helps filter noise and focus on actions that compound over time.

## Practical application
Build programs around core lifts and track load/reps. Set nutrition to goal-appropriate calories with sufficient protein and carbs. Question claims lacking mechanisms or data, and favor reputable sources. If a strategy is extreme or unsustainable, scale it to something you can maintain. Revisit basics first when progress stalls.
Use a short myth-check: What is the claim? What mechanism is proposed? Is there evidence? Does it fit your context and recovery? This keeps you from program-hopping and protects adherence.
Keep a small list of trusted references (coaches, research summaries) and default back to them when a new trend appears. Track your own results for 4-6 weeks before judging whether a method works for you.

:::checklist
- Fundamentals | Prioritize progressive training, protein, and sleep over gimmicks.
- Claims test | Ask for mechanism and evidence; avoid “magic” fixes.
- Sustainability | Choose approaches you can keep doing; extreme rules often backfire.
:::

## Common mistakes
- Chasing fads instead of applying basics consistently.
- Believing spot reduction or “soreness equals growth.”
- Overreacting to short-term fluctuations instead of following trend data.
