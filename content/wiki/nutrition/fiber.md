## What is fiber and where does it come from?

Fiber comes from plant foods: whole grains, vegetables, fruit, pulses and nuts [2]. It counts among the carbohydrates; see [[carbohydrates]]. The Voedingscentrum, the Dutch nutrition centre, says fiber contributes to good digestion [1].

:::takeaway
In the Netherlands, adults aged 18 to 49 should get at least **24 g (women) or 30 g (men)** of fiber a day, and more the more energy you use [1]. WHO and EFSA both say **25 g** for adults [3][4]. Get it from whole grains, vegetables, fruit, pulses and nuts, and build up slowly [1][2].
:::

## How much fiber do you need?

Four sources, four ways of saying it:

- **Voedingscentrum:** the amount depends on how much energy you need, so the Gezondheidsraad (the Dutch Health Council) states it per megajoule: 3.0 to 3.5 g. Anyone who needs more energy needs more fiber. For ages 18 to 49 the page says at least 24 g a day for women and at least 30 g for men [1]. The tables go up with your activity level: a man of 18 to 29 gets 31 to 37 g when lightly active and 40 to 47 g when active, a woman of the same age 25 to 30 g and 33 to 38 g [1].
- **Gezondheidsraad (July 2026):** 3.0 to 3.5 g per megajoule, which is 12.6 to 14.6 g per 1,000 kcal [2]. At 2,000 kcal that comes to about 25 to 29 g.
- **WHO:** at least 25 g of naturally occurring fiber a day for adults, together with at least 400 g of vegetables and fruit [3].
- **EFSA:** an adequate intake of 25 g a day for adults. EFSA uses that term when it cannot establish a recommended intake [4].

The Dutch figures follow your energy needs, which is why men get a higher number than women and why the advice rises if you train a lot [1][2]. WHO and EFSA use one figure for all adults [3][4].

The Voedingscentrum says many Dutch people get too little, and also that you do not need more than the recommended amount [1]. In the cohort studies below, the curves suggest the risk may keep falling at higher intakes [5]. The sources do not settle that. Reaching the recommended amount is the clear goal.

## What does fiber do for your health?

The Voedingscentrum says fiber lowers the risk of heart and vascular disease, type 2 diabetes and bowel cancer [1]. The numbers behind this come from studies that follow people over time, described below [5].

A large series of reviews looked at studies that followed people over time. Those who ate the most fiber had a 15 to 30% lower risk of dying from any cause or from cardiovascular disease, and of coronary heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and colorectal cancer, than those who ate the least. The biggest drop in risk was at 25 to 29 g a day, and higher intakes could give even more benefit [5]. These are observational findings, so on their own they show a link, not proof of cause. The reviewers rated the certainty of the evidence for fiber as moderate and say the relationships could be causal, because risk kept falling as intake rose and trials point the same way. The trials, in adults without chronic disease (weight-loss and supplement trials excluded), found lower bodyweight, systolic blood pressure and total cholesterol with higher fiber intakes [5]. The findings apply to the population at large, not to people with chronic disease [5].

There is a caveat from the Voedingscentrum: most studies look at fiber-rich foods, such as whole grains, vegetables, fruit and pulses. So the effect of fiber by itself is not always clear [1].

## Does fiber help you lose weight?

A little, and the sources are careful about it. The Voedingscentrum says people who eat enough fiber keep a somewhat lower bodyweight than people who eat much less. It adds that the exact role of fiber is hard to pin down: a diet full of fiber-rich foods may simply be lower in energy [1]. Some types of fiber add to feeling full after a meal, but it is not clear enough whether that leads to eating less over the whole day [1].

A systematic review of randomized trials points the same way. Viscous fibers (pectins, beta-glucans and guar gum) lowered appetite in 59% of the comparisons, against 14% for less viscous fibers. The effects on how much people ate and on bodyweight were small [6]. Fiber helps, but it is not a fix by itself. See [[hunger-and-satiety]].

## Which foods give you fiber?

Foods, not added fibers. The Gezondheidsraad names whole grain products, vegetables, fruit, pulses and nuts [2]. WHO names whole grains, vegetables, fruit and pulses [3]. Since most research is on these foods, the Voedingscentrum says it is important to eat fiber-rich products, not just the fiber [1]. The appetite review above tested specific fiber types such as guar gum and pectin. That does not show that a supplement does what fiber-rich foods do [1][6].

## How do you add fiber without stomach trouble?

Slowly. The Voedingscentrum says it is smart not to suddenly eat much more fiber. It can cause wind, cramps or constipation, and your gut needs time to adapt [1]. It also says fiber binds fluid, so drink enough [1]. A practical way is to swap one thing at a time, for example wholemeal bread for white, or adding a portion of beans or lentils, and give your gut time before the next change.

- Amount | At least **24 g (women) or 30 g (men)** at 18 to 49 in the Netherlands [1]. WHO and EFSA: **25 g** [3][4].

- Foods | Whole grains, vegetables, fruit, pulses and nuts [2].
- Pace | Add fiber step by step so your gut can adapt [1].

## Common mistakes

- **Doubling your fiber in a week.** It can cause wind, cramps or constipation [1].
- **Chasing fiber as a separate nutrient.** The research is on fiber-rich foods [1].
- **Expecting fiber to do the weight loss for you.** The sources find a link with lower bodyweight and small effects in trials [1][5][6].
- **Reading the observational numbers as proof.** The 15 to 30% comes from studies that follow people over time [5].
