## What is sleep?
Sleep is the primary recovery process where the body repairs tissue, consolidates learning, and regulates hormones. For lifters, quality sleep drives strength, muscle gain, and adherence. It amplifies the benefits of training, nutrition, and supplementation.

:::takeaway
Target **7-9 hours** nightly with consistent bed and wake times; avoid caffeine **6-8 hours** before bed.
:::

## How does it work?
During deep sleep, growth hormone and other anabolic processes repair muscle from [[volume]] and [[intensity]] work. REM sleep aids motor learning, improving technique. Adequate sleep maintains insulin sensitivity and appetite regulation, helping control [[calories]] and body composition. Chronic restriction elevates cortisol, raises injury risk, and slows progress even with good programming.
Sleep also stabilizes mood and pain perception, making hard sessions feel more manageable and improving adherence.

## Practical application
Create a wind-down routine: dim lights, cool the room, and keep screens and heavy meals out of the last hour. If needed, use a light carb snack to aid relaxation. For late training days, cap caffeine earlier and allow enough time to fall asleep. Short 20-30 minute naps can bridge poor nights without harming nighttime sleep. Track sleep duration and how you feel on heavy lifts to calibrate habits, and adjust training stress if sleep dips.
Consider consistent morning daylight exposure and a brief walk after waking to anchor circadian rhythm. If noise is an issue, earplugs or a quiet fan can reduce disturbances.

:::checklist
- Sleep dose | Keep **7-9 hours** nightly; guard the schedule like training.
- Environment | Cool, dark room; reduce screens and bright light before bed.
- Caffeine cut-off | Stop intake **6-8 hours** pre-bed to preserve depth and quality.
:::

## Common mistakes
- Trading sleep for extra training, which reduces adaptation and raises injury risk.
- Heavy caffeine use late in the day, blunting sleep depth and recovery.
- Irregular schedules across weekdays and weekends, disrupting circadian rhythm and adherence.
