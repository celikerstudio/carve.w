## What are carbohydrates?

Carbohydrates are sugars, starches and fibre. Your body breaks sugars and starches down to glucose and stores some of it as glycogen: about 80 to 100 g in the liver and 300 to 400 g in your muscles [5]. Glycogen is a main fuel for hard endurance exercise and for lifting [5]. Fibre is the part you cannot digest; see [[fiber]].

:::takeaway
Carbs are fuel, not the cause of fat gain. Low-carb and keto diets do not burn more fat when calories and protein are the same [1][2]. How many you eat is mostly a matter of preference and training: endurance athletes need a lot, and lifters may need less than the **3 to 7 g per kg** often advised [5][6].
:::

## Do carbs make you fat?

Not more than other calories do. Fat loss comes down to a calorie deficit; see [[calorie-deficit]]. The ISSN, a sports nutrition society, states that no controlled study in a research ward with equal calories and protein has found a meaningful fat loss or metabolic advantage for low-carb or keto diets [1].

The best test of this is a meta-analysis of 32 feeding studies in which all food was provided and carbohydrate was swapped for fat at the same calories. The lower-carb diets did not burn more fat. The lower-fat diets came out slightly ahead, by 26 kcal a day in energy burned and 16 g a day in fat lost, too small to matter in practice [1][2].

Outside the lab the result is similar. In the DIETFITS trial, 609 adults with overweight or obesity (BMI 28 to 40) and without diabetes followed a healthy low-fat or a healthy low-carb diet for a year. They lost 5.3 and 6.0 kg, a difference that was not significant [3].

## Why do people lose weight on keto, then?

Because they often eat less without trying. The ISSN explains the apparent advantage of low-carb and keto diets in two ways [1]. They tend to contain more protein, which fills you up more than carbs or fat. And keto diets can reduce appetite, so people eat fewer calories without counting them. If that works for you, it is a fine reason to choose it. It does not mean carbs themselves were the problem. See [[hunger-and-satiety]].

## Is keto a good idea if you lift?

It is not clearly better, and the picture for muscle is muddled. A meta-analysis of 13 small trials in people who did strength training found that keto diets lost more weight and fat than other diets, and also about 1.3 kg more fat-free mass, which lifting did not prevent [4]. In some of these trials the keto group ate fewer calories, and several did not report intake [7]. Fat-free mass also includes water and stored carbohydrate, which drop on a keto diet. A review of these studies concludes that once calories and glycogen are accounted for, low-carb diets do not seem to hold back muscle growth [7]. The ISSN adds that restricting carbs can hurt performance, especially in endurance sports; for strength it is less clear [1].

## How many carbs do you need?

It depends on how you train. The ISSN says daily advice of 5 to 12 g per kg is common, with the high end for athletes who train hard for many hours a week. It also notes that these numbers come mostly from research in male endurance athletes, and that strength athletes may need less [5].

For lifting, the evidence is thin. A review points out that the 3 to 7 g per kg often advised for strength training is largely borrowed from endurance research and may be more than needed [6]. In 17 longer-term studies of diet and strength training, the amount of carbohydrate made no difference to strength in 15 [7].

So for most lifters: get your protein first, then fill the rest of your calories with carbs and fat in a balance you like and can keep up. If your workouts feel flat on low carbs, eat more of them, especially around training. See [[macros]] and [[pre-workout-nutrition]].

## Which carbs are best?

Fibre-rich ones. A large series of reviews and meta-analyses found that people who ate the most fibre had a 15 to 30% lower risk of dying from any cause or from cardiovascular disease, and of coronary heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and colorectal cancer, than those who ate the least. The biggest benefit was seen from 25 to 29 g of fibre a day, and more may be better still. That part comes from observational studies, but trials also showed lower bodyweight, blood pressure and cholesterol when people ate more fibre. Whole grains showed the same pattern. The glycaemic index of foods mattered little [8].

In practice: whole grains, oats, potatoes, legumes, fruit and vegetables as the base, and sweets and white bread as the extra.

## Does it matter when you eat carbs?

Mostly not. According to the ISSN, timing matters mainly when you have to refill glycogen quickly, for example when you train twice in one day [5]. Otherwise the daily total counts. See [[post-workout-nutrition]].

## Your checklist

:::checklist
- Fat loss | Choose low-carb or higher-carb by what you can keep up; calories decide fat loss.
- Protein first | Set your protein, then fill the rest with carbs and fat.
- Training | Flat workouts on low carbs? Add carbs, especially before longer sessions.
- Quality | Build on whole grains, potatoes, legumes, fruit and vegetables, and aim for at least 25 g of fibre a day.
:::
