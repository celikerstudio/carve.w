## What is a plateau?

A plateau is a stretch in which the number you are chasing stops moving. Two situations get the same name. Your lifts stop going up, or the scale stops going down. They have different causes, so the fixes differ too.

:::takeaway
To keep progressing in the gym, the stimulus is likely to have to go up over time, even if only slightly [1]. In weight loss, the usual pattern in lifestyle programmes for adults with overweight or obesity is that most weight is lost by about **6 months**, followed by a plateau and gradual regain [2].
:::

## Why do your lifts stall?

Your muscles adapt to what you ask of them. The ACSM position stand of 2026, an overview of 137 systematic reviews in healthy adults, describes progressive overload as increasing the stimulus, such as load, volume, training frequency, exercise selection or duration, "even if only slightly", as the muscle continues to adapt [1].

The same position stand adds that progression is not necessary to get beneficial outcomes. Increasing the stimulus is likely a requirement only for people who want continued longer-term progress [1]. So when a lift stays the same, raising the demand slightly is the first thing to try. [[progressive-overload]] shows the ways to do that, from adding weight to adding reps or sets.

## Does changing your routine help?

The ACSM defines variation as the systematic modification of training variables over time to facilitate continued adaptation [1]. That is a planned change, not hopping between programmes.

For muscle growth, the position stand found no difference between linear, undulating and nonperiodized training. With appropriate progressive overload, periodization is not significantly superior to nonperiodized programs [1]. For muscle growth, then, a fancier rotation of rep ranges does not replace making sure the stimulus keeps going up. For strength the position stand could not determine the effect of periodization, although one review slightly favoured periodized programs when volume was equal [1]. The authors also note that much of the evidence they pooled comes from inexperienced trainees, although in earlier work they found that training experience made little difference [1].

## What should you check when your lifts stall?

Start with the simple things.

- **Is the stimulus still going up?** Compare the last month with the one before: weight, reps and sets. If none of them went up, that is the place to start. See [[progressive-overload]].
- **Do you have a record?** Without one you cannot tell a stall from a feeling. See [[progress-tracking]].
- **Is recovery in order?** Poor [[sleep]] or too little food make hard sessions harder.
- **Are you worn out?** [[deload]] covers planned easier weeks: what they are, how coaches use them, and that it is unclear whether you need one.

Change one thing at a time. If you change the programme, the food and the sleep in the same week, you will not know what worked.

## Why does weight loss slow down?

The 2013 AHA/ACC/TOS guideline for adults with overweight and obesity describes the usual pattern in a lifestyle intervention: maximum weight loss at 6 months, followed by a plateau and gradual regain over time [2]. That is a description of the typical course, not a rule for every person. It is about people in these programmes, not about people who are already at a healthy weight.

Part of the slowdown is the body. A lighter body burns less, so the same intake closes less of the gap. [[calories]] explains how this slows weight loss over time, and [[metabolism]] covers what is known about metabolism adapting during dieting. Hall and colleagues also find that bodyweight reacts slowly to a change in intake [3].

Their model already includes metabolic adaptation, and with a constant reduction in intake it has weight levelling off only after several years. The plateau seen in outpatient weight-loss programmes after 6 to 8 months comes much earlier, so they consider it more likely that adherence was not constant over that time. They support this with studies in which energy expenditure at the plateau was much higher than what people said they ate. They also leave open that the model does not capture long-term energy metabolism well [3]. So the body slows weight loss down over time, and in Hall's reading the early plateau in these programmes is more likely intake drifting up. The data cannot tell you which it is in your own case.

## What can you do when the scale stops?

- **Look at a few weeks, not a few days.** Weight moves around from day to day. See [[progress-tracking]].
- **Check what you actually eat.** Hall and colleagues think intake drifting up is a likely reason for the early plateau [3]. [[calorie-deficit]] shows how to set and check a deficit.
- **Decide what you want.** A plateau at a lower weight can be a good place to stay for a while. Pushing further means a bigger gap between intake and use again, and only you can say whether that is worth it now.
- **Keep following the plan while you check.** Switching plans at the first slow month makes it hard to see what works. See [[consistency]].

:::checklist
- Lifts | Compare the last month with the one before. Did weight, reps or sets go up?
- Step | Raise one thing slightly: a little weight, a rep or a set [1].
- Scale | Judge a few weeks of weights, not single days.
- Intake | Check what you really eat before changing the plan.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Changing the whole programme when one small step up in the stimulus would do.
- Swapping routines often and calling it variation. Variation in the ACSM sense is planned [1].
- Reading a plateau in weight loss as proof that your metabolism is broken. The sources describe a typical course, and they cannot tell you the cause in your case [2][3].
- Changing food, training and sleep all at once.
