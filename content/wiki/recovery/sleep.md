## How much sleep do adults need?

Adults need **7 or more hours** of sleep a night, on a regular basis. That is the recommendation of the two main US sleep societies (the AASM and the SRS), and it applies to adults aged 18 to 60 [1]. A panel of experts reached it after reviewing the evidence on sleep and health.

:::takeaway
Sleep **7 or more hours** a night, on a regular basis [1]. Short sleep can make you hungrier, may mean losing more lean mass while you diet, and can weaken your training. A large dose of caffeine 6 hours before bed still cut sleep in one study [7].
:::

## What does short sleep do to fat loss?

Sleep affects what you lose when you diet. In a small study, 10 overweight adults followed a moderate calorie restriction for 14 days, twice. Once they had 8.5 hours in bed each night (about 7 hours 25 minutes of measured sleep), once 5.5 hours (about 5 hours 14 minutes). With the shorter sleep they lost less of their weight as fat (0.6 kg against 1.4 kg) and more fat-free mass (2.4 kg against 1.5 kg). They also reported more hunger [2]. Fat-free mass includes muscle, but also water, glycogen and organs, so this is not a pure muscle number.

The study was tiny and short, and the authors say so. The people in it were not training. It fits the point of [[calorie-deficit]]: the deficit sets how much you lose, and sleep seems to influence what that loss is made of.

## Does short sleep make you hungrier?

In a study of 12 healthy young men, one night of about 4 hours of sleep was followed by about 559 kcal more eaten the next day than after a night of about 8 hours. They were also hungrier before breakfast and dinner [3]. They slept from 02:00 to 06:00 for that one night. One night in 12 men proves little about months of short sleep, and the amounts varied a lot from man to man.

A longer trial points the same way from the other side. In 80 adults with overweight who normally slept less than 6.5 hours, half got a counseling session aimed at 8.5 hours in bed. Over two weeks they slept about 1.2 hours more per night (measured with a wrist monitor) and ate about 270 kcal a day less than the control group, with no diet instructions [4]. See [[hunger-and-satiety]] for how appetite works.

## What does short sleep do to muscle and training?

In a study of 13 young adults, one night without any sleep lowered muscle protein synthesis after a meal by 18%, and cortisol was 21% higher over the next day [5]. Testosterone also looked lower, but that result was weaker, and the drops in testosterone and protein synthesis showed up in the seven men and not in the six women. The authors call the sex difference exploratory. This was one extreme night, not a normal short one. It suggests that a body without sleep may be less ready to build muscle, in men at least.

Performance can drop too. A review of 45 trials (670 people, mostly one night or a few hours less sleep) found that in athletes, sleep deprivation lowered endurance, power, maximal force and speed, and made the same effort feel harder [6]. Maximal force fell the least of these, and only in athletes. It dropped mainly after waking up hours too early; a fully sleepless night did not lower it significantly [6]. Most studies tested one night, so the review says little about weeks of short sleep. If your training feels flat for days in a row, sleep is one thing to check next to your [[volume]], your [[rest-days]] and the risk of [[overtraining]].

## Which habits improve sleep?

One habit has a direct test: the timing of caffeine. In a small home study, 400 mg of caffeine in a pill reduced sleep when taken 0, 3 or 6 hours before bed. Even at 6 hours, sleep was more than an hour shorter, and the participants did not notice it themselves [7]. That is a large dose, so a smaller one may do less. The sports nutrition position stand on caffeine says that caffeine can lengthen the time to fall asleep and reduce deep sleep, and that people differ in how strongly it hits them [8]. So it makes sense to keep late-day caffeine low, and to go earlier if you are sensitive. See [[caffeine]] for doses.

More habits are in [[sleep-hygiene]]. The 80-adult trial above used personal sleep hygiene counseling to get people to sleep longer, so such advice can work [4]. Which single tip did the work in that trial is not known.

:::checklist
- Time in bed | Plan about 8 hours or more in bed. In one study 8.5 hours in bed gave about 7 hours 25 minutes of sleep [2].
- Caffeine | Keep it early in the day. 400 mg at 6 hours before bed still cut sleep in one study.
:::

:::check
Common sleep advice, not tested against sleep length or fat loss in any source here: going to bed and waking at similar times, including weekends; a dark, cool room; no screens or hard training in the last hour before bed. The caffeine timing rests on one small study at 400 mg; a safe cut-off for smaller doses or for you is not established.
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Treating sleep as optional** - Short sleep can lower your performance in the next session [6].
- **Dieting on little sleep** - You may get hungrier, and in a small study more of the weight lost was lean mass [2].
- **Late coffee or a strong pre-workout** - A large dose of caffeine 6 hours before bed still cut sleep in one study [7]. See [[caffeine]].
- **Expecting one number to fit everyone** - The recommendation is 7 or more hours, not exactly 7, for adults aged 18 to 60 [1].
