## What is progress tracking?

Progress tracking means writing down what you eat, how you move and what the scale says, so you can see what is happening instead of guessing. It also covers your training log.

:::takeaway
Lifestyle programmes for adults with overweight or obesity usually include regular self-monitoring of **food**, **physical activity** and **weight**. To keep lost weight off, the 2013 AHA/ACC/TOS guideline adds weighing **weekly or more often** [1]. Frequent weighing goes with better maintenance [1], and self-monitoring in general goes with more weight loss [2]. Both are associations and do not show that tracking causes the result.
:::

## What do the guidelines say?

The 2013 AHA/ACC/TOS guideline for adults with overweight and obesity says that comprehensive lifestyle interventions usually provide a structured behaviour change programme with regular self-monitoring of food intake, physical activity and weight. The same behaviours are recommended to maintain lost weight, with weekly or more frequent monitoring of body weight added [1].

It also reports that strategies such as frequent self-weighing (at least weekly), eating a reduced-calorie diet and high levels of physical activity are associated with better weight maintenance over time [1]. The wording matters: associated with. People who weigh themselves often may also be the people who are more committed in other ways. This guideline is about adults with overweight or obesity who are losing weight or keeping it off, not about people at a healthy weight.

## How strong is the evidence?

The guideline itself calls it an association. A 2011 systematic review of 22 US studies from 1993 to 2009 looked at self-monitoring of diet, exercise and self-weighing in weight loss programmes. It consistently found an association between self-monitoring and weight loss, but rated the evidence as weak because of methodological limitations [2]. The studies mostly relied on self-report, and in all but two the samples were mainly white women [2]. The authors call for studies that establish how much self-monitoring is required [2].

So tracking is a reasonable tool, and the research cannot tell you how much of it you need or that it will work the same for everyone.

## Why does the number jump around?

Daily weight is noisy. In a study of 80 adults, weight was higher early in the week and fell during the week, and the authors say such weekly swings should be seen as normal and not as weight gain [3]. A single weigh-in therefore says little. One way to deal with it is to weigh on the same days and compare the average of a week with the one before. [[calorie-deficit]] shows what to expect from the scale on a deficit.

## Can tracking help you stick with it?

The 2024 review of habit formation suggests self-monitoring as one of the tools for building a habit: practitioners should counsel people on the long time habits take and give them tools like self-monitoring and accountability [4]. That is the authors' advice, and the review did not test it. See [[consistency]] for how long a habit takes.

## What should you track?

Keep it small enough that you will still do it in a month.

- **Weight.** Weigh at the same time of day. For adults with overweight who are keeping lost weight off, the guideline recommends at least weekly [1]. If you weigh more often, compare weekly averages. If you weigh once a week, do it on the same day.
- **Food.** Write down what you eat, or at least the things that matter for your goal. See [[calorie-deficit]].
- **Training.** Log the exercise, the weight, the sets and the reps. Without this record you cannot tell if you are doing more than last time, which is what [[progressive-overload]] depends on.
- **Sessions done.** A tick for each workout shows how consistent you were, even in a week when the scale did nothing.

If weighing or logging food makes you anxious, or you have had an eating disorder, talk to your doctor or dietitian before you start, and ask whether tracking suits you.

:::checklist
- Weigh | Weekly or more often when keeping weight off [1], at the same time of day, and compare averages if you weigh more than once a week.
- Eat | Write down what you eat for as long as it helps you.
- Lift | Log weight, sets and reps for each exercise.
- Review | Pick a fixed day to look at the week.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Reacting to a single weigh-in. Daily weight moves with the week [3].
- Tracking so much that it makes you anxious or you quit. A log you keep up beats a detailed one you drop.
- Treating the association as proof. The sources do not show that weighing itself is the cause [1][2].
- Making big changes without looking at a few weeks of data.
