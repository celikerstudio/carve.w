## What are fat burners?

Fat burners are supplements sold to speed up fat loss. This article covers four ingredients you will often see in them: caffeine, green tea extract, carnitine and CLA. It does not cover other ingredients. Caffeine is found in many supplements, typically as guarana, bissey nut or kola, so it can be in a product without the word "caffeine" on the front [1].

:::takeaway
Fat burners do little. Meta-analyses found about **1 kg** more weight loss than control with carnitine and with CLA, and green tea gave a small difference that was not statistically significant [4][5][7][8]. Caffeine limits for healthy adults who are not pregnant are **200 mg at once and 400 mg a day**, from all sources [2]. For green tea extract the European Food Safety Authority found **no dose that can be called safe** for the liver [3]. Fat loss comes from a [[calorie-deficit]].
:::

## Do fat burners work?

Mostly not. A systematic review in adults with overweight or obesity looked at 315 randomised trials of 14 supplements and alternative therapies for weight loss. Only 52 of those trials were of good enough quality to support a claim, and 16 of those found a significant difference in weight. The authors' conclusion: the high-quality evidence for these products is limited [6].

### Does carnitine burn fat?

Your body makes carnitine and uses it to handle fat, so supplements sound logical. The ISSN, a sports nutrition society, found that most data suggest carnitine does not markedly change fat metabolism, exercise performance or weight loss in overweight people, obese people or trained people [1].

Two meta-analyses of randomised trials in adults found a small difference. One, of 9 trials with 911 people, found 1.33 kg more weight loss on carnitine than in the control groups, and the effect got smaller the longer people took it [7]. The other, of 37 trials with 2,292 people, found 1.21 kg [8]. Both are well under the 2.5 kg that the CLA meta-analysis below treats as clinically meaningful.

### Does CLA burn fat?

CLA is a fatty acid. In animal studies it looked impressive. In humans, the ISSN says studies suggest at best a modest ability to stimulate fat loss [1]. A meta-analysis of placebo-controlled trials found people on CLA lost 1.08 kg more than people on placebo. The authors set the bar for a clinically meaningful difference at 2.5 kg, and CLA did not reach it [5].

### Does green tea extract burn fat?

A Cochrane review of trials of 12 to 13 weeks in overweight or obese adults found little. In six studies outside Japan (532 people), the green tea groups lost 0.04 kg more than the control groups. Eight studies in Japan (1,030 people) were too different to combine, and their differences ranged from 0.2 to 3.5 kg in favour of green tea. The authors concluded that green tea appears to give a small weight loss that was not statistically significant and is not likely to be clinically important [4]. The 2021 systematic review of weight-loss supplements agrees: of 16 higher-quality green tea trials, only 2 found a significant difference, 1.5 to 1.7 kg, and both lasted less than 8 weeks [6].

### Does caffeine burn fat?

The ISSN says caffeine has a documented ability to increase energy expenditure and promote weight loss. It does not say how much [1].

## How much caffeine is safe in a fat burner?

For healthy adults who are not pregnant, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) found no safety concerns with single doses of caffeine up to **200 mg**, or with habitual intake up to **400 mg a day** [2]. For pregnant or breastfeeding women the limit is 200 mg a day, and for children and adolescents EFSA proposes 3 mg per kg of bodyweight a day [2]. See [[caffeine]] for how this works out and what it does to your sleep.

This is where fat burners catch people out. The caffeine in the product adds to your coffee, tea and any pre-workout. Green tea extract can contain caffeine too [4]. Read the label for the milligrams of caffeine, look for guarana, bissey nut and kola, and add it all up.

## Can green tea extract hurt your liver?

Yes. EFSA concluded that it was not possible to identify an EGCG dose from green tea extracts that could be considered safe. EGCG is the main catechin in green tea [3].

In the clinical studies EFSA reviewed, there was no evidence of liver toxicity below **800 mg EGCG a day** for up to 12 months, but at that dose or higher, transaminases (liver values in the blood) rose significantly compared with control groups. Liver toxicity was reported with one specific product at a daily dose equal to 375 mg EGCG. Green tea extracts have been linked to liver toxicity, especially when used for weight control [3]. Taking the extract on an empty stomach and in one dose raised the EGCG level in the blood more than taking it with food and in split doses [3].

Food supplements with green tea catechins provide anywhere from 5 to 1,000 mg EGCG a day [3], so check the EGCG amount on the label. Drinking green tea is a different case. EFSA considers tea made in the traditional way safe at the usual intakes in Europe. Eight of the reported cases of liver injury did follow green tea, but most were linked to supplements [3].

## Are fat burners safe in the long run?

For many supplements nobody knows. The ISSN notes that many supplements lack long-term safety data, and that many have never had basic safety studies that match the length and the dosages people actually use [1]. A product that is on sale is not a product that has been shown to be safe.

## What works for fat loss instead?

A [[calorie-deficit]] is what drives fat loss. See also [[protein]] and [[sleep]].

:::checklist
- Caffeine | If you are a healthy adult and not pregnant, stay under **200 mg** at once and **400 mg** a day from the product, coffee, tea, pre-workout and green tea extract together.
- Under 18, pregnant or breastfeeding | The limits are lower. See [[caffeine]].
- Green tea extract | EFSA found no EGCG dose from extracts that can be called safe for the liver. Liver values rose at **800 mg or more** a day, and liver toxicity was reported with one product at 375 mg.
- Doubts | If you have a liver condition, take medication or are pregnant, ask your doctor first.
- Money | Put it into food and training first.
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Expecting the product to do the work.** The meta-analyses above found differences of about 1 kg or less, and the effect of carnitine got smaller over time [5][7][8].
- **Stacking caffeine.** A fat burner, a pre-workout and a few coffees can add up to more than the **400 mg** a day that EFSA considers safe for healthy adults who are not pregnant [2].
- **Reading 800 mg as a safe line.** EFSA found no dose of green tea extract that can be called safe, and liver toxicity was reported with one product at 375 mg [3].
- **Not reading the label.** Caffeine can hide as guarana, bissey nut or kola, and the EGCG amount in green tea extract varies a lot [1][3].
