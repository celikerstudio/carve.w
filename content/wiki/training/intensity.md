## What is intensity?

Intensity has two meanings in the gym, and people mix them up.

The first is how heavy the weight is. This is usually given as a percentage of your one-rep max (1RM), the heaviest weight you can lift once with good form. A set at 80% of your 1RM is a heavy set. See [[1rm]].

The second is how hard the set is for you, which is effort. That is about how close you end the set to failure, the point where you cannot do another rep. Effort is often rated with a scale such as RPE (rate of perceived exertion) or counted as reps in reserve (RIR): how many reps you could still have done. See [[rpe]]. A light weight can be a high-effort set if you keep going until the reps get slow.

:::takeaway
For maximum strength the ACSM found the biggest gains with loads of **80% of your 1RM or more** [1]. For muscle growth a wide range of weights worked, from about **30% to 100% of 1RM** in the reviews it analysed [1]. Sets to **failure are not necessary** [1].
:::

## Which weights build strength?

In the 2026 ACSM position stand, strength was enhanced by lifting heavier loads, 80% of 1RM or more [1]. The older 2009 position stand looked at this by experience. It cites meta-analytical data in which 60% of 1RM gave the largest effects for strength in novices, while 80% of 1RM gave the largest effects in trained people [2].

The 2026 position stand replaces the one from 2009 and applies from complete novices to experienced trainees [1]. The 60% and 80% figures above come from the older document, so use them as background for how the picture looked then, not as the current advice. See [[rep-ranges]] for the reps that go with each load.

## Which weights build muscle?

Many. The ACSM overview found that muscle growth did not differ between programs using low loads (30% of 1RM) and high loads (100% of 1RM) [1]. An expert consensus on muscle growth in athletes says the same: comparable growth is possible across a wide spectrum of loading zones. The same experts say that using moderate loads for most sets may be more practical [3].

The ACSM asks for high effort whatever the load [1]. For a beginner, hard does not have to mean close to failure, see below.

## Do you have to train to failure?

No. The ACSM states that completing sets to momentary muscular failure does not enhance gains in strength, muscle size or power, so it is not necessary for benefits to occur [1]. In its abstract, training to momentary muscle fatigue did not consistently change the outcomes [1].

The ACSM still asks for high effort. Its primary recommendation is that healthy adults train with high effort at least twice a week, with all major muscle groups involved [1]. High effort and failure are not the same thing: you can have a hard set and still stop with two or three reps left. Failure may even be unwise for some people: the ACSM says lifting to fatigue may be inadvisable for certain groups, such as older individuals, because of risks to vascular health and an increased risk of injury from poor form [1].

## What about RIR and RPE?

The ACSM says sufficient effort can be reached by completing sets "near-failure" or by aiming for 2 to 3 reps in reserve. Later, in its limitations, it adds that while training to failure is not obligatory, there is insufficient evidence to quantify exact RIR and perceived exertion targets [1].

So 2 to 3 reps in reserve is a reasonable way to check that a set was hard enough. It is not a number the research can confirm as the right one.

## Do beginners need to train close to failure?

The expert consensus for athletes says novice lifters can gain muscle robustly without training close to failure. As you get more experience, the need for effort appears to grow [3].

This consensus is written for athletes who want to maximize muscle growth, so it comes from a different group than the ACSM overview of healthy adults.

:::checklist
- Strength | Want to get stronger? Include sets with heavy loads, 80% of 1RM or more [1].
- Muscle | Pick a weight you can handle with good form. A wide range works [1][3].
- Effort | Make sets hard, but you do not need to reach failure [1].
- Check | Use 2 to 3 reps in reserve as a guide, not as an exact target [1].
- Beginner | You can build muscle without ending every set close to failure [3].
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Going to failure on every set.** The ACSM found no extra gain in strength, muscle size or power from it [1].
- **Stopping far from hard.** The ACSM still asks for high effort [1].
- **Treating RIR as exact.** The ACSM says there is not enough evidence for exact RIR or RPE targets [1]. Judging reps left is harder in long sets, see [[rep-ranges]].
- **Thinking only heavy weights build muscle.** Growth was comparable from 30% to 100% of 1RM in the reviews [1]. See [[volume]] and [[progressive-overload]].
