## What are compound exercises?
Compound exercises are multi-joint lifts that work multiple muscle groups at once (e.g., squats, deadlifts, presses, rows). They deliver high mechanical tension and efficiency, forming the backbone of strength and [[hypertrophy]] programs.

:::takeaway
Anchor programs with **4-6** staple compounds, trained **2-3x/week**, holding **1-3 RIR** to balance stimulus and fatigue.
:::

## How does it work?
Multi-joint lifts recruit large muscle mass and high-threshold motor units, enabling heavier loads and strong progressive potential. They also teach bracing, coordination, and force transfer. Because compounds are taxing, they demand careful management of [[volume]] and [[intensity]] to avoid limiting accessories and recovery.
Compounds often set the ceiling for weekly fatigue; dialing their effort dictates how much quality accessory work (for weak points or injury-proofing) you can perform afterward.

## Practical application
Pick a few patterns: squat/hinge, horizontal/vertical press, horizontal/vertical pull. Program 2-4 sets per compound per session, 6-10 reps for hypertrophy, and occasional 3-6 reps blocks for strength. Place compounds early when fresh. Track RPE to keep 1-3 reps in reserve on most work sets, using top-set/back-off or straight sets. Rotate variations (e.g., front squat, RDL, incline press) to manage joint stress while maintaining skill overlap.
Use accessories to shore up sticking points revealed in compounds (e.g., hamstrings for deadlifts, upper back for squats) while keeping total weekly fatigue within recoverable limits.

:::checklist
- Pattern coverage | Include squat, hinge, press, and row/pull variants each week.
- Effort control | Keep most sets at **RPE 7-9** (1-3 RIR); use deloads every **4-8** weeks.
- Variation | Swap in close variants to manage joints without losing movement skill.
:::

## Common mistakes
- Chasing maximal loads weekly instead of accumulating quality near-failure work.
- Neglecting bracing and setup, causing technique drift and lost progress.
- Letting compounds monopolize fatigue so accessories for weak points get skipped.
