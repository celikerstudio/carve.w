## How much alcohol is low risk?

The cleanest answer comes from the Canadian guidance on alcohol and health, which is about health in general, not about training. It says nothing about muscle or training performance [1].

:::takeaway
Less alcohol means less risk [1]. The Canadian guidance rates **2 standard drinks or less per week** as low risk, and **more than 2 on one occasion** as a higher risk of harm, such as injuries [1]. In one small study, eight active men who drank about 120 g of alcohol after a hard workout built muscle protein 24% more slowly than with protein alone, even though they also had protein [3]. That was a binge, and the study did not test one or two drinks.
:::

The Canadian panel concluded that when it comes to drinking alcohol, less consumption means less risk of harm, and recommends that people in Canada consider reducing their alcohol use [1]. It rates the risk like this [1]:

- 2 standard drinks or less per week: low
- 3 to 6 standard drinks per week: moderate
- 7 standard drinks or more per week: increasingly high

On a single occasion, more than 2 standard drinks is linked to a higher risk of harm to yourself and others, including injuries and violence [1].

This scale does not apply to everyone. The guidance says that when you are pregnant or trying to get pregnant, there is no known safe amount of alcohol, and that when breastfeeding, not drinking is safest. It also lists situations where no alcohol is safest: driving, using machinery and tools, taking medicine or other drugs that interact with alcohol, doing any kind of dangerous physical activity, being responsible for the safety of others, and making important decisions [1].

Because these are Canadian figures, the size of a "standard drink" matters. See the next section.

## What counts as a standard drink?

In Canada, a standard drink is 13.45 grams of pure alcohol [1]. That is about [1]:

- a bottle of beer (341 ml, 5% alcohol)
- a glass of wine (142 ml, 12% alcohol)
- a shot of spirits (43 ml, 40% alcohol)

So 2 Canadian standard drinks a week is about 27 g of pure alcohol (2 × 13.45 g). Around the world, a standard drink ranges from 8 to 20 grams of pure alcohol [1], so check how many grams your own glass has before you compare it with these numbers.

## Does alcohol slow muscle growth?

Possibly, but none of these studies measured muscle growth. A systematic review of alcohol after strength training found 12 studies, each with only 8 to 19 people. It concluded that muscle protein synthesis (how fast your muscles build new protein) was lower after alcohol [2]. Only one study in the review measured it directly: Parr and colleagues, 2014 [2][3]. The review only looked at effects up to 48 hours, so it cannot say what happens to muscle over months [2].

In Parr's study, eight physically active young men (average age 21) did leg extensions and then cycling. Afterwards they drank alcohol at 1.5 g per kg of bodyweight. For their average weight of 79 kg that is about 120 g of alcohol, roughly 9 Canadian standard drinks. The study counted 12 standard drinks, which means glasses of about 10 g [2][3]. Compared with drinking protein alone, muscle protein synthesis was 24% lower when they also had alcohol with protein [3]. It was 37% lower when the alcohol came with carbohydrate instead of protein [3], but that comparison also lacks the protein, so only the 24% shows the effect of alcohol alone. The study had no condition without alcohol and without protein. Muscle protein synthesis was still higher than at rest in every condition [3].

Two limits matter. The group was eight men, and the dose was a binge, based on what team athletes report drinking after sport [3]. The study says nothing about one or two drinks, and nothing about women.

## Does alcohol hurt strength or soreness?

In most of the studies, no difference. The review concluded that force, power, muscular endurance and soreness were unchanged when people drank after resistance exercise [2]. The doses were heavy too: 0.6 to 1.5 g of alcohol per kg, on average 1.1 g per kg [2]. For an 80 kg person that is about 90 g, roughly 7 Canadian standard drinks. So this says little about one or two drinks.

There are exceptions. In two experiments by Barnes and colleagues (2010), using 1 g of alcohol per kg, the strength loss was larger with alcohol about 36 hours after very hard leg exercise [2]. Jump height was lower with alcohol at 24 and 48 hours in another Barnes study, a difference the review calls trivial [2]. The review notes that this dose was neither the lowest nor the highest used across studies [2].

"No difference" in small studies is not the same as "no effect". The studies had 8 to 19 people each and followed them for at most 48 hours [2]. A small effect could be missed.

## What should you do with this?

The sources give a direction, not a prescription:

- The fewer drinks, the lower the risk [1].
- The research on muscle is about heavy drinking right after a hard workout [3]. No study here tested one or two drinks after training.
- If you do drink, count the drinks in your weekly total from the Canadian guidance [1].
- To compare your own drinks with it, count in grams and compare with 13.45 g per Canadian standard drink, not in "glasses".

:::checklist
- Weekly | **2 standard drinks or less** is low risk in the Canadian guidance (13.45 g each) [1].
- Per occasion | More than **2** raises the risk of harm [1].
- After training | About 120 g of alcohol lowered muscle protein building by 24%, even with protein [3]. One or two drinks were not tested.
- Count | Check the grams in your glass, not just the number of glasses.
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Counting glasses, not grams.** A Canadian standard drink of wine is 142 ml at 12%. A bigger pour, or a stronger wine, is more than one [1].
- **Thinking protein cancels out a binge.** Even with protein, muscle protein synthesis was 24% lower than with protein alone [3].
- **Treating the research as a rule.** The studies are small and short [2].
