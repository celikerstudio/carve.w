## What is zinc?

Zinc is a mineral you get from food and from supplements [2]. This article covers what the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) says about how much adults need, where the upper limit is, and what the research says about zinc and testosterone.

:::takeaway
EFSA recommends **9.4 to 16.3 mg of zinc a day for men and 7.5 to 12.7 mg for women**, depending on how much phytate is in your diet [1]. The upper limit is **25 mg a day, counting your food and supplements together** [2][3]. A common single zinc supplement holds 30 mg, which is above that limit on its own [2]. Too much for a long time goes with symptoms of copper deficiency [2].
:::

## How much zinc do you need?

EFSA's recommended intake for adults runs from 9.4 to 16.3 mg a day for men and from 7.5 to 12.7 mg for women. Where you land depends on phytate, a substance in plant foods that binds zinc and stops it being absorbed [1][2]. EFSA gives a value for phytate intakes of 300, 600, 900 and 1,200 mg a day, which covers the range of average intakes in European countries [1]. For men the values are 9.4, 11.7, 14.0 and 16.3 mg, and for women 7.5, 9.3, 11.0 and 12.7 mg [5].

Which foods are which? The report lists red meat, whole wheat, raisins, unrefined cereals and fortified cereals as good sources of zinc, and notes that unrefined cereals have a high zinc content but low bioavailability [2]. A diet built on unrefined cereals therefore sits towards the higher figures. For vegetarians, the report says the dietary requirement may be as much as 50% greater [2]. Zinc deficiency has been observed in people taking penicillamine, a drug that binds metals [2]. Larger doses of calcium and a high iron content in the diet can also lower zinc absorption [2].

These figures were calculated for people at the 97.5th percentile of reference body weight. EFSA's average requirements are lower: 6.2 to 10.2 mg for women and 7.5 to 12.7 mg for men [1]. So the recommended intake is set high enough that most people need a bit less.

## What is the upper limit for zinc?

The upper limit is 25 mg a day. It was set by the Scientific Committee on Food, the predecessor of EFSA, and EFSA's 2025 overview still lists it for adults. It also applies to pregnant and breastfeeding women [2][3].

The limit is for all the zinc you take in, food and supplements together. That is how an upper limit is defined: the maximum level of total chronic daily intake from all dietary sources [3]. Zinc is not like magnesium here, where food is left out. The studies behind the zinc limit counted food too: in one pair of studies people got 30 mg a day from supplements on top of about 10 mg from food, 40 mg in total [2].

That matters because food already gives you a good part of it. The report puts the average intake from food in Europe at 13 mg a day for men and 9 mg for women [2]. The most common single zinc supplements hold 30 mg per capsule (range 15 to 50 mg) [2]. So an average diet plus one such capsule is over 25 mg. Supplements that combine several nutrients usually hold 10 to 15 mg (range 2 to 20 mg) [2]. Check the label.

The limit is a ceiling. Nothing in the sources suggests working towards it. EFSA's predecessor took the no-adverse-effect level of around 50 mg a day of total intake, from studies in small groups over a relatively short time, and halved it [2].

## What happens if you take too much zinc?

A large single dose gives nausea, vomiting, stomach pain, cramps and diarrhoea [2].

Over a long time, the effect is on copper. Chronic zinc toxicity is associated with symptoms of copper deficiency [2]. The report gives these findings:

- Short balance studies saw an effect on how much copper the body kept at intakes as low as 18.2 mg of zinc a day. A 90-day study in post-menopausal women found no effect on copper balance at 53 mg a day, but those women also got 3 mg of copper a day, which the report calls adequate to high [2].
- Prolonged supplements of 50 to 300 mg a day have been linked to lower copper in the blood, fewer white blood cells and a type of anaemia [2].
- Clear effects such as anaemia and impaired immune responses were seen with supplements above 150 mg a day over long periods. Below 100 to 150 mg a day, the report says, the critical effect is much harder to identify [2].

The report also names groups that may be more sensitive to excess zinc: people with haemochromatosis (too much iron in the body) and with insulin-dependent diabetes [2].

## Does zinc raise testosterone?

A systematic review looked at 52 studies of 27 proposed testosterone boosters, including 5 on zinc and magnesium. It found that most fail to increase total testosterone, and zinc and magnesium were not among the few it rated effective or possibly effective [6].

The International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) looked at ZMA, a blend of zinc, magnesium aspartate and vitamin B6. Of three studies, two found no change in testosterone. The one that found a rise was in football players in spring training. It also reported depletion of zinc and magnesium, so the ISSN notes that the rise could come from a deficient nutritional status and not from the supplement itself. One of the studies with no change was an 8-week trial in resistance-trained men, which also found no change in strength or fat-free mass [4]. The ISSN adds that earlier zinc deficiency may lower testosterone production [4]. It places ZMA in the category with little to no evidence for muscle building [4]. See [[testosterone-boosters]].

- Intake | EFSA: **9.4 to 16.3 mg** (men) or **7.5 to 12.7 mg** (women) a day, depending on phytate [1].
- Limit | **25 mg** a day for adults, food and supplements together [2][3].
- Supplement | A common single capsule holds 30 mg, already over the limit on its own [2].
- Long term | Too much zinc for a long time goes with copper deficiency [2].
- Testosterone | Reviews of zinc and ZMA did not find that it raises testosterone [4][6].

## Common mistakes

- **Counting only the supplement.** The 25 mg limit includes the zinc in your food [2][3].
- **Adding a high-dose zinc capsule to a normal diet.** A common single capsule holds 30 mg [2].
- **Treating the upper limit as a dose.** It is a ceiling [2].
- **Buying zinc to raise testosterone.** The studies reviewed here do not show that it does [4][6].
