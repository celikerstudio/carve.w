## Is ashwagandha safe?

Ashwagandha is a herb used in Ayurvedic medicine, including for stress, and sold as a supplement [2][3]. The German Federal Institute for Risk Assessment (BfR) says its health risks have not been well investigated, and that the effects people claim for it are not scientifically proven [1].

:::takeaway
The Dutch RIVM advises consumers **not to use** ashwagandha preparations, and the German BfR and other European authorities advise against ashwagandha supplements [1][5]. No safe dose could be derived from the data, so this article gives none [1]. There are reports of liver damage, including liver failure [1][2].
:::

## Who should not take ashwagandha?

Everyone should avoid it, according to these two institutes. The RIVM advises consumers not to use herbal preparations with ashwagandha, in particular pregnant women [5]. The BfR advises against ashwagandha supplements, and especially children, pregnant and breastfeeding women and people with acute or pre-existing liver disease should not take them [1].

This also goes for ashwagandha tea. Nobody has studied the tea, so the RIVM assumes the conclusion for supplements applies to it as well [5].

## Can ashwagandha damage your liver?

There are reports that it can. The BfR says the reports of liver damage, including cases of acute liver failure, give reason for caution [1]. In the Netherlands, the pharmacovigilance centre Lareb had received four notifications of liver injury linked to ashwagandha products by the time of the RIVM report [5], and eight more by July 2025 [6]. Lareb says liver toxicity with these products appears to be rare [6]. Case reports cannot prove that ashwagandha was the cause [5].

A case series from India described patients whose liver injury was linked to products with ashwagandha as the only herbal ingredient. Of the 8 patients who met that criterion, 5 had chronic liver disease, and 2 of them only found out during the investigation. Three patients came in with acute-on-chronic liver failure, and all 3 died. The others had long-lasting liver injury that went away on its own, except for one patient whose injury became chronic [2].

The most common first sign was yellow skin or eyes (7 of 8 patients), followed by itching (5 of 8). The symptoms started a median of 41 days after people began taking it, with a range of 14 to 540 days [2].

The doses in that series ranged from 500 mg to 10-15 g a day, depending on the formulation [2]. That does not make a capsule from the drugstore safe. The RIVM looked at nine case reports of harm and found daily amounts in the same range as the estimated exposure from supplements sold in the Netherlands [5].

The researchers analysed five of the products. They found no harmful contaminants or adulterants, apart from trace amounts of lead, cadmium and arsenic in one product, and they could not test all the products [2].

How often this happens is not known. A series of cases is not a measure of frequency. The RIVM judges it unlikely that a large part of the population is at risk, but also says it is unknown which people are sensitive [5].

## Does ashwagandha affect hormones and medicines?

It might. The BfR notes evidence that ashwagandha supplements may affect the immune system and the endocrine system, with effects on cortisol, blood glucose, thyroid hormones and sex hormones [1]. The RIVM reports that physicians have seen harmful effects on the liver and on thyroid hormones and cortisol [5].

The BfR also says ashwagandha supplements can interact with medicines that regulate blood glucose (antidiabetics), blood pressure (antihypertensives) or the immune system (immunosuppressants), and that medical advice should be sought when taking certain medicines [1]. If you already take ashwagandha and use medicine like this, or medicine for your thyroid, talk to your doctor.

Reported everyday side effects include nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, drowsiness, headaches, dizziness and skin rashes [1].

## Does ashwagandha reduce stress?

Not clearly. Two meta-analyses of randomised trials disagree. One, with 12 trials and 1,002 adults aged 25 to 48, found less stress than on placebo, but the trials differed a lot from each other and the certainty of the evidence was low [4]. Another, of seven trials on cortisol and six on perceived stress (488 people in all), found lower cortisol but no significant effect on how stressed people felt [3]. The BfR's position is that the claimed positive effects have not been scientifically proven [1].

Lower cortisol is not a clear benefit here. The BfR lists effects on cortisol among the possible effects of ashwagandha on the endocrine system [1].

## What should you do if you already take it?

The advice from both institutes is not to use it [1][5]. Stop, and see a doctor if your skin or the whites of your eyes turn yellow, or you get itchy all over [2]. If stress or sleep is the reason you looked at ashwagandha, start with [[stress-management]] and [[sleep]].

:::checklist
- Who should avoid it | Everyone, according to the RIVM and the BfR. Children, pregnant or breastfeeding women and people with liver disease especially.
- Tea | The RIVM assumes the same advice applies to ashwagandha tea.
- Dose | There is no safe dose, so there is no dose to follow.
- Already taking it | Stop. See a doctor if your skin or eyes turn yellow or you itch all over. Tell your doctor if you use medicine for blood glucose, blood pressure, your thyroid or your immune system.
- Alternatives | Work on [[stress-management]] and [[sleep]] first.
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Treating "natural" as "safe".** Liver injury was seen with single-ingredient ashwagandha, and the products that were tested held no harmful contaminants or adulterants, apart from trace metals in one [2].
- **Thinking you are not at risk.** The RIVM says it is unknown which people are sensitive, and in the Indian series 2 of the 5 patients with chronic liver disease only learned of it during the investigation [2][5].
- **Copying a dose from a study.** The BfR could not derive a safe intake level from the available data [1].
