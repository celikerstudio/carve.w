## What is meal timing?

Meal timing is when you eat: how many meals, how far apart, whether you eat breakfast, how late you eat and what you eat around training. It gets a lot of attention online. For most people who train, it matters much less than how much you eat over the whole day.

:::takeaway
Get your daily **calories and protein** right first; that matters more than the clock [1]. The ISSN suggests spreading protein over the day, roughly **20 to 40 g every 3 to 4 hours** [4]. In 21 trained men, protein right before or right after training gave similar results over 10 weeks [3]. Eating more often did not raise energy use [5], and breakfast did not help weight loss in trials [6], so pick the pattern you can keep up.
:::

## Does timing matter more than your daily total?

No. The protein position stand of the ISSN, a sports nutrition society, says total protein and calorie intake appears to be the most important thing for gains from strength training. It adds that the effect of eating right before or right after training appears minimal for people who are not athletes [1].

A 2013 meta-analysis of 23 studies on protein timing and muscle growth points the same way. At first glance, protein close to training seemed to give a small to moderate boost to muscle growth. Once the authors accounted for how much protein people ate in total, timing no longer made a significant difference, and total protein was the strongest predictor of growth [2]. In the studies where intake was not matched, the timing groups simply ate more protein: 1.66 against 1.33 g per kg a day on average [2]. Only three studies matched protein intake, so the authors could not rule out a small timing effect [2]. Two newer meta-analyses agree on the main point. One, a review of 65 trials, found that extra protein added lean mass regardless of when it was taken [10]. The other, of the five studies that directly compared protein before and after training, found no difference in lean mass; for strength the results were mixed [11].

So start with how much you eat. See [[calories]] for your energy needs and [[protein]] for how much protein you need.

## How should you spread your protein?

Over several meals, if that suits you. The ISSN position stand on nutrient timing calls meeting your total daily protein, preferably in evenly spaced feedings about every 3 hours, a primary area of emphasis [4]. It says a dose of 20 to 40 g of high-quality protein every 3 to 4 hours appears to stimulate muscle protein synthesis best compared with other patterns [4]. Muscle protein synthesis is the short-term building process measured in the lab; it is not the same as measured muscle growth over months, and [[protein]] notes that long-term studies on spreading are mixed. The ISSN position stand on meal frequency adds one specific case: athletes who are dieting and eat enough protein may keep more lean mass by eating more often [5].

In practice, three or four meals with a decent portion of protein each make the ISSN pattern easy. Your daily total still comes first.

## Does protein have to come right after training?

No. In a 10-week trial with 21 trained men (9 and 12 per group), one group took 25 g of protein immediately before training and the other immediately after. The two groups did not differ on any measure, and neither group gained significant lean mass in the arms or legs [3]. It was a small trial. The authors think the window for protein around a session may be as wide as several hours, depending on when you ate before training, but the trial did not test that [3]. The ISSN says the same: the size and timing of your meal before training affects how much the meal after it matters [4]. See [[post-workout-nutrition]] and [[pre-workout-nutrition]].

## Does eating more often speed up your metabolism?

It does not appear to. The ISSN position stand on meal frequency concludes that eating more often does not appear to significantly raise the energy you burn digesting food, your total energy use or your resting metabolic rate [5]. In people who do not exercise, it did not appear to improve body composition either [5]. See [[metabolism]].

Meal frequency can still matter for hunger. The ISSN position stand on nutrient timing says the evidence is stronger that meal frequency affects appetite and satiety than that it affects weight loss [4]. If three meals leave you starving, eat four. If two big meals keep you full, that works too. See [[hunger-and-satiety]].

## Do you need breakfast to lose weight?

No. A meta-analysis of randomised trials in adults in high-income countries found that people assigned to eat breakfast did not lose more weight. They weighed 0.44 kg more than people who skipped it and ate about 260 kcal a day more [6]. The trials were short and mostly of low quality, so this does not prove that skipping breakfast helps. It does show that adding breakfast did not help weight loss in these trials. Eat breakfast if it keeps you full and on track, skip it if you are not hungry.

## Does eating late make you gain fat?

That has not been settled. Two trials looked at when in the day the calories come. In a trial with 30 adults with overweight or obesity, people followed two calorie-restricted diets for 4 weeks each, with the same calories. In one, breakfast was the biggest meal; in the other, dinner was. Weight loss and energy expenditure were the same, and people were less hungry with the big breakfast [7]. All meals in that trial fell within a window of about 11 hours early in the day, so the big dinner was not a late-night meal [7].

A 12-month trial in 139 adults with obesity in China compared the same calorie-restricted diet with and without a time limit (eating only between 8:00 and 16:00). Weight loss did not differ significantly [8].

A tightly controlled lab trial points the other way. In adults with overweight or obesity, eating the same food later in the day increased hunger and lowered the energy they burned while awake [9]. Weight was not the outcome of that trial, so it does not show that late eating leads to fat gain. If you eat late, watch whether it pushes up your daily total. See [[intermittent-fasting]] for fasting patterns.

:::checklist
- Total | Hit your daily calories and protein first [1].
- Protein | The ISSN suggests 20 to 40 g per meal, about every 3 to 4 hours [4].
- Training | A protein-rich meal before or after training, whichever suits your day [4].
- Meals | Choose the number of meals that keeps hunger under control [4][5].
- Breakfast | Optional. It did not help weight loss in trials [6].
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Rushing a shake after training while missing the daily total** - Total protein predicted muscle growth, timing did not [2].
- **Eating six small meals to boost metabolism** - Eating more often does not appear to raise energy use [5].
- **Forcing breakfast down to lose weight** - In trials, breakfast eaters ate more and did not lose more [6].
- **Letting late snacks add up** - Late eating raised hunger in a lab trial [9], and extra snacks raise your daily total.
