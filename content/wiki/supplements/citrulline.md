## What is citrulline?

Citrulline is an amino acid your body makes and recycles into arginine, which the body uses to make nitric oxide [1]. Nitric oxide is involved in blood flow, which is why citrulline is sold for pumps. None of the studies below measured pumps, and the one that measured muscle swelling found no extra effect [4]. The ISSN notes that taken by mouth, citrulline raised arginine and several markers of nitric oxide [1]. In supplements it often comes as citrulline malate, citrulline bound to malate, which is a compound used in your cells' energy cycle [1].

:::takeaway
The ISSN lists citrulline under **limited or mixed evidence** [1]. In three studies **8 g of citrulline malate** gave more reps over several sets [1], but other studies with the same dose found no difference [4][5]. A meta-analysis of studies with 6 to 8 g found about **3 more reps** (6%) than placebo [3].
:::

## Did citrulline help people lift more?

In three studies, yes, and in others no. The ISSN reviews three studies of 8 g of citrulline malate in resistance training, and in all three it significantly improved the number of reps over multiple sets [1]:

- Trained men did chin-ups, reverse chin-ups and push-ups to failure.
- Trained men did five sets at 60% of their one-rep max on the leg press, hack squat and leg extension.
- Trained women did six sets each of bench press and leg press at 80% of their one-rep max.

All three groups were trained, so they say nothing about beginners. They measured reps, not muscle size.

Other studies with the same 8 g dose found nothing. In 12 recreationally trained men, 8 g of citrulline malate 40 minutes before 5 sets of bench press did not add reps or muscle swelling [4]. In recreationally active men and women doing 10 sets of 10 on an isokinetic chair, the number of reps was the same on citrulline malate and placebo, and soreness over the next 72 hours was higher with citrulline malate [5].

## How big is the effect?

Small. A meta-analysis of 8 trials with 137 people, mostly strength-trained, found that 6 to 8 g of citrulline malate taken 40 to 60 minutes before lifting gave about 3 more reps per exercise (6.4%) than placebo, on top of roughly 51 reps over about 5 sets [3]. The result varied a lot (3 reps, give or take 5), and most of the people were strength-trained men (101), with 26 women and 9 untrained men [3]. The effect was small, borderline for the lower body and not significant for the upper body [3]. An earlier meta-analysis of 12 studies with 198 healthy people also found a small benefit for strength and power [2]. The two meta-analyses draw on much of the same lifting research, so the second is not an independent confirmation. Its authors point out that no individual study was clear on its own, and that the result may be relevant to high-level athletes, where small margins decide the outcome [2].

## Is it the citrulline or the malate?

The ISSN says it is not clear whether the benefits come from citrulline alone [1]. The role of malate is largely undetermined [1]. The ISSN also describes studies of L-citrulline without malate, and they point both ways [1]:

- 22 trained men took 2.4 g a day for 7 days, plus 2.4 g an hour before a 4 km cycling time trial. Blood arginine rose and the time trial was 1.5% faster than on placebo [6].
- 10 healthy men took 6 g a day for 7 days. Blood arginine rose, nitrite tended to rise (not significantly), and in hard cycling their oxygen uptake adapted faster, they lasted longer (661 against 589 seconds) and did slightly more work in the performance test (125 against 123 kJ) [7].
- 22 people (11 of them men) took a single dose of 6 g before testing. It made no significant difference to the total repetitions over 5 sets, time to exhaustion, VO2max or anaerobic threshold [8].

The ISSN adds that 12 g of citrulline malate did not improve one high-intensity cycling effort [1]. Only the last of the three studies above counted lifting reps, and it was negative. The other two tested cycling, so together they do not settle the question. Trexler's authors list the form of citrulline among the things still to be sorted out [2].

One practical point: 8 g of citrulline malate is not 8 g of citrulline. In the study that used it, the ratio was 1.11 to 1, and its authors warn that what products contain can differ from what the makers claim [5].

## What do the studies not tell you?

The meta-analyses looked at the acute effect on strength, power and reps, in healthy people [2][3]. They did not test whether you build more muscle over months. The doses (6 to 8 g of citrulline malate) are what was studied; they are not a proven best dose [2][3].

## Is citrulline safe?

The sources here did not test safety, so this article cannot say. If you have a health condition or take medication, ask your doctor before you start.
