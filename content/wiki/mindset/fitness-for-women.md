## Do women gain from lifting weights?

:::takeaway
Yes, women gain strength and muscle from resistance training [1]. What can change your plan is **pregnancy** (the WHO backs regular activity including muscle strengthening, and ACOG calls physical activity safe and desirable, when there are no complications or contraindications [2][3]) and **eating too little** for how much you train [6]. Training by menstrual cycle phase is not backed by convincing evidence [4][5].
:::

A meta-analysis of 24 studies in women found large strength gains from resistance training and a medium gain in muscle size. After the authors corrected for publication bias, the strength effect shrank (still large for upper body, medium for lower body) [1]. Training frequency and volume seemed to matter for strength, while none of the factors they tested clearly mattered for muscle growth [1]. The meta-analysis did not compare women with men, so it does not show that the sexes respond the same. For how much protein helps you build muscle, see [[protein]].

## Is strength training safe when you are pregnant?

The advice below holds only without complications or contraindications, so ask your midwife or doctor whether it applies to you. ACOG writes that without obstetric or medical complications or contraindications, physical activity in pregnancy is safe and desirable, and pregnant women should be encouraged to continue or start safe activities [3]. The WHO recommends that all pregnant and postpartum women without contraindication do regular physical activity, including a variety of aerobic and muscle-strengthening activities [2]. It says the evidence supports regular strength training for them [2]. Neither source names a safe weight.

The WHO sets at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity a week as the target for pregnant and postpartum women, for example brisk walking [2]. If you are not there yet, it advises starting with small amounts and building up [2].

## Can you keep training if you already did before?

Yes, under the same condition of no contraindication. Both the WHO and ACOG say that women who were active before pregnancy, including those doing vigorous aerobic activity, can continue during pregnancy and after the birth [2][3]. The WHO advises women who plan to compete or exercise well above the guideline to get supervision from a specialist healthcare provider [2].

## What should you avoid while pregnant?

The WHO advises avoiding exercises on your back after the first trimester [2]. It also says to avoid activities with physical contact or a high risk of falling, activities that might limit oxygen (such as high altitude, when you do not normally live there), and exercise in high heat and humidity, and to stay hydrated [2]. Your healthcare provider should tell you which warning signs mean you stop and consult them immediately [2].

## What about after the birth?

The WHO says to return to activity gradually after delivery, and after a caesarean section to do so in consultation with a healthcare provider [2]. During pregnancy and after the birth, pelvic floor muscle training can be done daily to reduce the risk of urinary incontinence [2].

## Should you plan your training around your menstrual cycle?

There is no convincing evidence that you need to. An umbrella review of reviews found highly variable results on cycle hormones and strength, performance and muscle growth. It also found poor and inconsistent methods for confirming which phase women were in, and the authors called it premature to conclude that short-term hormone shifts appreciably change acute performance or strength and muscle gains [4]. A UEFA expert panel for women's football says the evidence linking cycle phase to performance or injury risk is inconclusive [5]. The panel wrote for football players at all levels, mainly with elite teams in mind, and its recommendations apply to players who are naturally menstruating, not those on hormonal contraception [5].

The same panel sees a use for tracking: spotting irregular periods, managing symptoms, and learning about your own body [5]. It names missed cycles, irregular bleeding or recurring symptoms as reasons for further evaluation, to recognise conditions such as low energy availability, hormonal imbalances or other health issues early [5]. If your periods stop or become irregular, see your GP.

## Can you eat too little for the amount you train?

Yes, and it applies to men and women. The International Olympic Committee describes Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs): when an athlete takes in too little energy for the amount of exercise, health and performance can suffer, in female and male athletes [6]. The statement is written for athletes, so it does not tell recreational lifters where their own limit is. If you train a lot and also diet hard, [[calorie-deficit]] explains how large a deficit is reasonable.
