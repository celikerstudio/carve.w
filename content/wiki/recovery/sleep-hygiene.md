## What is sleep hygiene?

Sleep hygiene is the set of habits and bedroom conditions that are meant to help you sleep: when you have caffeine and alcohol, when you train, how light and warm your room is, and how regular your bedtime is. [[sleep]] covers how much sleep you need and what short sleep does to fat loss and training. This article is about the habits.

:::takeaway
Have your last caffeine early. In a review of 24 studies, caffeine cut sleep by **45 minutes**, averaged over studies with different doses, and the authors estimate that a cup of coffee should come at least **8.8 hours** before bed [3]. Alcohol disturbs REM sleep from about two drinks [6]. A single evening session did not worsen sleep in a review of 23 studies; hard sessions ending an hour or less before bed might [7].
:::

## How much does sleep hygiene help?

Less than its popularity suggests, and it depends on the habit. A review of the evidence found that each of the usual tips is generally linked to how people sleep, but that what each tip does on its own has mostly not been tested in ordinary sleepers [1]. As a treatment for insomnia, a review of 42 trials found that symptoms improved from before to after sleep hygiene education, but less than with cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), exercise or acupressure, and most of those trials had a high risk of bias [2]. If you lie awake most nights for weeks, see a doctor; tips alone are not the treatment that works best.

For people who simply sleep too little, it can help. In a trial with 80 adults with overweight who slept less than 6.5 hours, personal sleep hygiene counseling raised measured sleep by about 1.2 hours a night over two weeks, compared with a control group [12]. Which tip did the work is not known.

The habits below are ordered by how directly they have been tested.

## When should you stop caffeine?

Early in the day. A review of 24 studies found that caffeine cut total sleep by 45 minutes on average across the studies, made falling asleep take 9 minutes longer and reduced deep sleep [3]. From those studies the authors estimated how long before bed caffeine no longer measurably shortens sleep: at least **8.8 hours** for a cup of coffee (107 mg) and at least **13.2 hours** for a standard serving of pre-workout (217.5 mg) [3]. For a bedtime of 23:00, that means coffee before about 14:00. Those cut-offs are estimates from a model and not a measured limit for you. They mark the first point at which the effect on sleep was no longer statistically significant, and the authors assume that a statistically significant drop in sleep is also one that matters [3]. The studies were in healthy adults who mostly drank low to moderate amounts of caffeine, so the cut-offs may not fit heavy coffee drinkers, people who never have caffeine, teenagers or adults over 65 [3]. The authors suggest using them as a starting point and adjusting to how you sleep [3].

One home study tested the timing directly. 400 mg of caffeine taken at bedtime, 3 hours before or 6 hours before each disturbed sleep compared with a placebo [5]. 400 mg at once is about four cups of coffee, going by the 107 mg a cup used in the review above [3].

The sports nutrition position stand on caffeine says caffeine makes falling asleep take longer and reduces deep sleep. Its half-life is generally around 4 to 6 hours, but people differ, partly through their genes [4]. It says athletes may consider taking caffeine earlier in the day when possible, to avoid the effect on sleep [4]. If you train in the evening, a strong [[pre-workout]] is the thing to look at first. See [[caffeine]] for doses.

## Does alcohol help you sleep?

It disturbs REM sleep. A review of 27 studies in healthy adults found that alcohol delayed and shortened REM sleep, already from a low dose of about two standard drinks, and more so with more drinks [6]. Falling asleep faster happened only at a high dose of about five drinks, and the effect on total sleep time was uncertain [6]. In almost all studies the drinks came within about 3 hours of bedtime, so the review cannot say whether drinking earlier helps [6]. See [[alcohol-and-fitness]].

## Can you train in the evening?

Yes. A 2019 review of 23 studies found that, in the pooled results, a single evening session did not worsen sleep, and if anything the opposite [7]. The exception was vigorous exercise that ended one hour or less before bed, which might make falling asleep slower and sleep shorter [7]. A second review, of hard sessions only, found that high-intensity exercise ending 2 to 4 hours before bed did not disrupt sleep in healthy young and middle-aged adults; across sessions ending half an hour to four hours before bed, the only change was slightly less REM sleep (2.34%) [8]. Regular evening training did not disrupt sleep either [8]. So if evening is when you can train, train in the evening. Finishing the hard part at least an hour before bed is the minimum these reviews suggest, and more time is safer.

## Do screens and light keep you awake?

Bright screens in the evening can. In a lab study, 12 healthy young adults read for about 4 hours before bed, in an otherwise very dim room, for five evenings in a row: once on a light-emitting e-reader and once from a printed book. With the e-reader they took about 10 minutes longer to fall asleep (about 26 against 16 minutes), made less melatonin, had a later body clock and were less alert the next morning [9]. Total sleep time did not differ [9]. It was one small study with long reading sessions, so it does not tell you how much a short look at your phone does. Dimming screens or reading on paper in the last hour is an easy thing to try.

## How warm should your bedroom be?

Not too warm. A review of the research on temperature and sleep found that with normal bedding and clothing, heat makes you wake more and reduces deep and REM sleep, while cold did not change sleep stages [10]. Cold is not harmless either: it changed how the heart is regulated during sleep, and the review says enough bedding is what keeps sleep normal in the cold [10].

:::check
A specific bedroom temperature (often given as 18 to 20 °C) is not supported by the sources here. The review gives no ideal number [10].
:::

## Does a regular bedtime matter?

It may be worth it, though the evidence is from observation. In 60,977 people in the UK Biobank, aged about 63 on average, sleep was measured with a wrist device for one week. Compared with the least regular fifth, the more regular groups had a 20 to 48% lower risk of dying over the following years, and regularity predicted that risk better than sleep duration did [11]. That shows a link, not that changing your bedtime causes the benefit. Going to bed and getting up at about the same time also makes it easier to plan the 7 or more hours from [[sleep]]. Treat it as a habit like any other; see [[consistency]].

:::checklist
- Caffeine | Last coffee at least 8 to 9 hours before bed; a pre-workout even earlier [3].
- Alcohol | Even two drinks disturb REM sleep [6]. Fewer drinks.
- Training | Evening training is fine. Finish hard sessions at least an hour before bed, more if you can [7][8].
- Screens | Dim them or read on paper in the last hour.
- Room | Keep it from getting too warm [10].
- Timing | Similar bed and wake times, weekends included.
:::

## Common mistakes

- **An afternoon pre-workout before an evening session** - A standard serving may need more than 13 hours to stop affecting sleep, by one estimate [3].
- **A nightcap to fall asleep** - Alcohol disturbs REM sleep from about two drinks [6].
- **Skipping evening training to protect sleep** - In pooled results, evening sessions did not worsen sleep [7][8].
- **Expecting tips to fix insomnia** - Sleep hygiene education helps less than CBT-I [2]. Talk to a doctor if poor sleep lasts for weeks.
