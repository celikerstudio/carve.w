## What is body recomposition?

Body recomposition means losing fat and gaining muscle at the same time. Practitioners use the term, and the review in this article notes that there is no standard terminology for it [4].

:::takeaway
Researchers have reported gains in lean mass during a calorie deficit [1]. In one 4-week trial, 20 young men with overweight or obesity who ate about 40% below their needs and trained six days a week gained **1.2 kg of lean mass and lost 4.8 kg of fat** on 2.4 g of protein per kg [3][5]. That is a reported result under hard, supervised conditions. It is not a typical result: across trials, lean mass gains are on average smaller in a deficit [7], and the ISSN still says a calorie surplus is the likely best route to gain lean mass [1]. See [[bulking]] for that and [[calorie-deficit]] for losing fat.
:::

## Does it really happen in a deficit?

It has been reported. The ISSN position stand on diets says lean mass gains have been reported in the literature during hypocaloric (low-calorie) conditions [1]. It also says that diets focused on gaining lean mass are probably best served by a sustained surplus, because that supports muscle building and increasing training demands [1].

On average, the gains are smaller. A meta-analysis of trials in which people lifted weights in a deficit found that lean mass gains were impaired compared with lifting without a deficit, while strength gains were comparable. A deficit of about 500 kcal a day prevented gains in lean mass [7]. One study the ISSN cites also found that 10 days of a moderate deficit (80% of estimated energy needs) lowered muscle protein building [1].

## What did the Longland study find?

One example is Longland and colleagues (2016). They randomly split 40 young men into two groups of 20. For four weeks both groups ate a diet with about 40% less energy than they needed. Both trained six days a week, combining resistance training with high-intensity interval training [3]. The 2026 ISSN position stand describes the participants as overweight and obese [5].

The only difference was protein. The group on 2.4 g per kg a day gained 1.2 kg of lean mass and lost 4.8 kg of fat. The group on 1.2 g per kg kept its lean mass (+0.1 kg) and lost 3.5 kg of fat [1][3]. Both groups improved their exercise performance by a similar amount [3].

Read it for what it is. It was four weeks, in young men with overweight or obesity, on a very hard training plan, and the researchers provided all the food and drink, which the ISSN counts as a strength [1]. Lean mass is everything that is not fat, so 1.2 kg of lean mass is not necessarily 1.2 kg of muscle. The ISSN notes that over short interventions, changes in lean mass may reflect shifts in glycogen and body water rather than contractile tissue [5].

A 40% deficit is also far bigger than the deficit the meta-analysis says to stay under to keep lean mass, about 500 kcal a day [7]. For someone who burns 2,800 kcal a day, 40% is about 1,100 kcal. For a pace to keep muscle, see [[calorie-deficit]].

In another study, in an active group with a BMI of 22 to 29 over 21 days, going from double to triple the RDA (1.6 to 2.4 g per kg) did not keep more lean mass [1][2].

## How much protein and what training?

For protein, the ISSN says that with resistance training and an energy-restricted diet, 2 to 3 times the RDA can promote greater fat loss and better body composition [2]. The RDA is 0.8 g per kg, so that is about 1.6 to 2.4 g per kg a day. Much of that work was done in overweight and obese people [2], so it may not carry over to someone already lean. For lean people who lift, the ISSN says 2.3 to 3.1 g per kg of fat-free mass a day may be required to keep muscle in a deficit [1]. See [[protein]] and [[calorie-deficit]].

For training, a review of recomposition in resistance-trained people names progressive resistance training together with evidence-based nutrition as the two key factors [4]. That means lifting where you keep trying to add load or reps over time, see [[progressive-overload]].

## Who is it for?

The research suggests it is more likely in some people than in others. Slater and colleagues (2019) propose that muscle growth in a deficit may be more likely among people new to resistance training and people with overweight or obesity. They also note that fat loss together with a gain in fat-free mass has been seen in elite and professional athletes who return to their sport after an off-season break or injury, and that this is less evident in highly trained people who train regularly [6]. The 2026 ISSN position stand says recomposition is common in people doing progressive resistance training while dieting, especially in the beginning or early intermediate stages of muscular development [5].

It is not limited to beginners. The authors of a review say there is a substantial body of literature showing recomposition in resistance-trained people as well [4]. The abstract does not say how large the effect was in each group. The 2026 ISSN position stand adds that the leaner you are and the closer you are to your genetic muscular potential, the less productive it is to pursue recomposition on purpose [5].

## Should you recomp, bulk or cut?

It depends on what matters more. If you mainly want muscle, the ISSN favours a surplus [1], and that is covered in [[bulking]]. If you mainly want to lose fat, run a [[calorie-deficit]] with enough protein and lifting to keep your muscle. The ISSN says fat loss runs on a sustained deficit and lean mass gain is probably best served by a sustained surplus [1]. Recomposition is what has been reported in between, and the sources here do not show it beating either route at its own goal.

:::checklist
- Reported | Lean mass gain in a deficit has been reported, e.g. 1.2 kg lean mass and 4.8 kg fat loss in 4 weeks in men with overweight [1][3][5]. On average, gains are smaller in a deficit [7].
- Deficit | The Longland deficit (40%) is far above the 500 kcal a day the meta-analysis advises staying under [7].
- Protein | With lifting in a deficit, 2 to 3 times the RDA (about 1.6 to 2.4 g per kg) can help [2].
- Training | Lift, and keep adding load or reps [4].
- Goal | Mainly muscle: [[bulking]]. Mainly fat loss: [[calorie-deficit]].
:::
