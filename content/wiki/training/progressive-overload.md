## What is progressive overload?

Progressive overload means asking your muscles for a little more over time than they are used to. The idea behind it is that your body adapts to the demands you put on it. Once a workout is easy, it stops being a reason to change. The ACSM position stand on resistance training puts it this way: to keep stimulating adaptation toward a training goal, progressive protocols are necessary [5].

:::takeaway
Muscles only adapt to more than they are used to, so make your training slightly harder over time. Add **2 to 10% weight** once you can do one or two reps more than planned [5]. Beginners can start with **8 to 12 reps** and **2 to 3 days a week** [5].
:::

## Why do muscles need more than last time?

Your muscles grow and get stronger as a response to a stress they cannot yet handle well. After a few weeks of the same weight, the same reps and the same sets, that stress is no longer new. You stay at the level you have, which is fine for maintaining but gives no reason to build.

That is also why "more" should be a small step. A big jump in weight makes it hard to keep your form and hit your reps. A small step that you repeat every few weeks adds up over a year.

## How can you progress?

Weight is only one of the levers. You can also change how much you do.

- **Load** - Put more weight on the bar. This is the most direct option, and heavier loads gave bigger strength gains in a meta-analysis comparing load levels [2].
- **Reps** - Do more reps with the same weight. The same meta-analysis found similar muscle growth with lighter and heavier loads when every set was taken to failure, so extra reps count for growth too [2]. See [[rep-ranges]].
- **Sets** - Add a set. In a meta-analysis, more weekly sets per muscle group went with more muscle growth [1]. See [[volume]].
- **Frequency** - Train a muscle on more days. For muscle growth, studies that matched volume found that training a muscle twice a week beat once a week, and it is unknown whether three times beats twice [3]. For strength, higher frequencies went with larger gains, but the difference vanished when total volume was the same, so extra days mainly give room for extra volume [4]. See [[frequency]].

Technique counts too. A deeper, controlled rep is harder than a bouncy one, even at the same weight.

## When should you add weight?

The ACSM gives a rule of thumb for when you train at a set rep target: add **2 to 10%** to the load once you can do the current weight for one to two reps more than the number you aimed for [5].

Say you aim for 10 reps and you now get 12 on your last set. Next session, raise the weight. If 10 kg becomes 10.5 or 11 kg, that is a 5 to 10% step. On small lifts like lateral raises, a 2% step may be smaller than the smallest plate you own, so keep adding reps until you can make a jump.

## Where should a beginner start?

For people who have no experience with resistance training, or have not trained for several years, the ACSM recommends loads you can lift for 8 to 12 reps and training 2 to 3 days a week [5]. In these first weeks, learning the movements matters more than the weight on the bar.

For intermediate lifters (roughly six months of steady training) the ACSM advises 3 to 4 days a week, and for advanced lifters 4 to 5 [5]. You do not need that on day one.

## Do you have to add weight every session?

No. Progress also shows up as an extra rep, a cleaner rep, or one more set at the same weight. Over a month, a plan like 60 kg for 8, 9, then 10 reps, then 62.5 kg for 8 reps is real progress even though the bar only changed once.

Single sessions also vary with sleep, food and stress. One bad day does not mean you lost anything. Look at the trend over three or four weeks.

## What if you stop progressing?

Stalls are normal. Check these in order:

- **Logging** - Without a record of weights and reps you cannot tell a stall from a feeling. See [[progress-tracking]].
- **Recovery** - Short sleep or too little food can hold your lifts back. See [[sleep]] and [[calories]].
- **Volume** - If you have done the same number of sets for months, try adding one or two per muscle group per week [1].
- **Smaller steps** - Move up in the smallest increments you have, or add a rep first.
- **A planned easier week** - After a long block of hard training, a lighter week can help. See [[deload]].

For the longer view, see [[plateaus]].

:::checklist
- Pick | Choose a weight you can lift for 8 to 12 reps with good form.
- Record | Write down weight, sets and reps for every exercise.
- Progress | Hit the top of your rep range on all sets, then add weight.
- Step | Raise the load by 2 to 10% when you get one or two reps more than planned [5].
- Stall | No progress for three or four weeks? Check sleep, food and volume before changing the plan.
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Adding weight too fast** - Form breaks down and you cannot hit the reps. Take the smaller step.
- **Chasing weight only** - Reps and sets are progress too [1][2].
- **Not tracking** - If you do not know what you lifted last week, you cannot beat it.
- **Expecting a new record every session** - Judge your progress over weeks, not days.
