## What is a calorie deficit?

A calorie deficit means eating fewer calories than you burn, on average over time. Whatever diet you follow, fat loss comes down to this: a sustained deficit [1]. For what a calorie is and how to estimate what you burn, see [[calories]] and [[tdee]]. This article is about the next question: how big the gap should be, and how to keep your muscle while it lasts. People also call this phase a cut; see [[cutting]].

:::takeaway
Fat loss runs on a sustained deficit. The more fat you carry, the bigger the deficit you can run; the leaner you get, the slower you should go [1]. A common target for keeping muscle is **0.5 to 1% of bodyweight a week**, a figure from bodybuilding research [1]. The focus is enough protein, strength training and a moderate pace [1].
:::

## How big should the deficit be?

It depends on how much fat you have to lose. The ISSN position stand says: the higher your starting body fat, the more aggressively the deficit may be imposed [1]. It gives no single number for that.

The numbers that do exist come from different groups, so check which one you are in:

- Athletes who trained with weights aimed to lose 0.7% or 1.4% of bodyweight a week; the fast group actually lost about 1%. The slow group gained lean mass, and the fast group's lean mass stayed about the same [10].
- Bodybuilders preparing for a contest are advised to lose 0.5 to 1% of bodyweight a week. That is a target from bodybuilding research, not a limit [1].
- A meta-regression of trials where people lifted weights in a deficit found that lean mass gains were impaired, and that a deficit of about 500 kcal a day prevented gains. The authors advise staying under 500 kcal a day if you lift to keep muscle while losing weight (Murphy and Koehler 2022) [4].


- Very low calorie diets (around 800 kcal) preserved muscle in untrained people with obesity who trained with weights. The ISSN mentions deaths on such diets with low-quality protein and no medical supervision, and says they have limited relevance for healthy and athletic people [1]. Do this only under medical supervision.

If you have a lot of fat to lose, a bigger deficit is allowed at the start, and it should shrink as you get leaner [1]. If you are already lean, the slower end of that range is easier on muscle [1].

## How do you keep your muscle?

The ISSN names three things to focus on: adequate protein, resistance training and an appropriate rate of weight loss [1]. All three work together.

**Protein.** In a deficit you may need more than usual. For lean people who lift, the ISSN says 2.3 to 3.1 g per kg of fat-free mass a day may be needed to keep lean mass during hypocaloric periods [1]. Fat-free mass is what is left after subtracting fat, so with a lot of fat to lose the amount in grams is lower than a bodyweight calculation suggests. Combined with training and a deficit, extra protein can also mean more fat loss [2]. See [[protein]] for the everyday amount.

**Training.** Keep lifting. In the meta-regression above, strength gains in a deficit were comparable to eating at maintenance, even where lean mass gains were not [4]. So there is a reason to keep training hard with weights, and [[progressive-overload]] still applies, though you may not build as much muscle as in a surplus.

**Pace.** Slower is gentler on muscle once you are lean [1].

**Meal frequency.** If your protein is adequate, eating more often during a deficit may help preserve lean mass in athletes [3]. The same source suggests the protein in each meal may matter more than how many meals you eat. Pick a pattern you can keep up.

## What should you expect?

Your weight will jump around. In a study of 80 adults, weight was highest on Sunday and Monday and fell during the week, and the authors call that normal rather than a sign of weight gain [9]. Compare weekly averages, not single mornings.

It will also slow down. You get lighter, so you burn less, and your body spends less energy than your fat and fat-free mass predict. In people with obesity on 1000 kcal a day for 8 weeks, resting metabolic rate ended about 92 kcal a day below prediction, and about half of that once their weight had stabilised [7]. The ISSN adds that most research showing a bigger slowdown used aggressive restriction with low protein and no strength training [1], and cites one study in untrained people with obesity where lifting kept resting metabolic rate up [1]. See [[metabolism]]. If the scale stops moving for weeks, see [[plateaus]].

## Do diet breaks help?

They are probably not needed, but they can make dieting easier. In the ICECAP trial (Peos 2021), 61 resistance-trained adults took a 1-week break at maintenance after every 3 weeks of dieting. They lost about as much fat and kept as much fat-free mass as people who dieted continuously for 12 weeks, and they felt less hungry [8]. In the MATADOR trial (2018), men with obesity who alternated 2 weeks of dieting with 2 weeks at maintenance lost more fat than men who dieted continuously for 16 weeks, with similar loss of fat-free mass [5]. That was one small trial, and the break group needed 30 weeks in total.

Fasting patterns are a different thing. A review of 28 trials of time-restricted eating, alternate-day fasting and 5:2 found that, combined, they did not change weight or fat loss compared with continuous restriction, and they lost slightly more fat-free mass (0.20 kg). Time-restricted eating alone lost a bit more weight and fat, but also more fat-free mass [6].

The ISSN says to choose between a daily deficit and a deficit spread across the week by preference and tolerance [1]. If breaks keep you going, use them.

## Your checklist

:::checklist
- Pace | A common target for keeping muscle is 0.5 to 1% of bodyweight a week. With a lot of fat to lose you can start faster and slow down as you lean out.
- Protein | Eat enough every day. If you are lean and lift, 2.3 to 3.1 g per kg of fat-free mass may help in a deficit; see [[protein]].
- Training | Keep lifting and keep adding load where you can.
- Tracking | Judge weekly averages, not one weigh-in.
- Adjust | Weight flat for two to three weeks? Cut 100 to 200 kcal or add steps, then wait again.
:::
