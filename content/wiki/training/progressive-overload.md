## What is progressive overload?

Progressive overload means asking your muscles for a little more over time than they are used to. Your body adapts to the demands you put on it, so a workout that never changes gives it little new to adapt to. The ACSM position stand on resistance training puts it this way: to keep stimulating adaptation toward a training goal, progressive protocols are necessary [5].

:::takeaway
Make your training slightly harder over time. Add **2 to 10% weight** once you can do one or two reps more than planned on two sessions in a row, a smaller step for small muscles and a bigger one for large muscles [5]. Beginners can start with **8 to 12 reps** and **2 to 3 days a week** [5].
:::

## Why do muscles need more than last time?

Your muscles grow and get stronger in response to a demand they cannot yet handle well. The ACSM says progression is needed to keep stimulating further adaptation toward your goal [5]. If you want to keep improving, plan for the demand to go up, even if only a little.

That is also why "more" should be a small step. A big jump in weight makes it hard to keep your form and hit your reps. A small step that you repeat every few weeks adds up over a year.

## How can you progress?

Weight is only one of the levers. You can also change how much you do.

- **Load** - Put more weight on the bar. This is the most direct option. In a meta-analysis where every set was taken to failure, heavier loads gave bigger 1RM strength gains, though not bigger gains in isometric strength [2].
- **Reps** - Do more reps with the same weight. That same meta-analysis found similar muscle growth with lighter and heavier loads [2]. In an 8-week trial of 43 people with lower body training experience, adding reps at a fixed weight and adding weight at a fixed rep range both worked, with similar results [6]. See [[rep-ranges]].
- **Sets** - Add a set. In a meta-analysis, more weekly sets per muscle group went with more muscle growth [1]. See [[volume]].
- **Frequency** - Train a muscle on more days. This is about how often you train each muscle, not how many days you are in the gym. When weekly volume was equal, meta-analyses found no significant difference in muscle growth, also in resistance-trained people [3], or in strength gains [4], between training a muscle more or less often. Extra days mainly make room for extra sets. See [[frequency]].

Technique counts too. A deeper, controlled rep is harder than a bouncy one, even at the same weight.

## When should you add weight?

The ACSM gives a rule of thumb for when you train at a set rep target: add **2 to 10%** to the load once you can do the current weight for one to two reps more than the number you aimed for, on two training sessions in a row. Use a lower percentage for small muscle exercises and a higher one for large muscle exercises [5].

Say you aim for 10 reps on the squat and you get 12 in two sessions in a row. Raise the weight next time. For a 100 kg squat, 5% is 5 kg. On a small lift like lateral raises, 2% may be less than the smallest plate you own, so keep adding reps until you can make the jump.

## Where should a beginner start?

For people who have no experience with resistance training, or have not trained for several years, the ACSM recommends loads you can lift for 8 to 12 reps and training 2 to 3 days a week [5]. Those are training days for the whole body, not times per muscle. In these first weeks, learning the movements matters more than the weight on the bar.

For intermediate lifters (roughly six months of steady training) the ACSM advises 3 to 4 days a week, and for advanced lifters 4 to 5 [5]. How often each muscle is trained then depends on how you split the work over those days. You do not need that on day one.

## Do you have to add weight every session?

No. Progress also shows up as an extra rep, a cleaner rep, or one more set at the same weight. Over a month, a plan like 60 kg for 8, 9, then 10 reps, then 62.5 kg for 8 reps is real progress even though the bar only changed once.

Single sessions also vary with sleep, food and stress. One bad day does not mean you lost anything. Look at the trend over three or four weeks.

## What if you stop progressing?

Stalls are normal. Check these in order:

- **Logging** - Without a record of weights and reps you cannot tell a stall from a feeling. See [[progress-tracking]].
- **Recovery** - Short sleep or too little food can hold your lifts back. See [[sleep]] and [[calories]].
- **Volume** - If you have done the same number of sets for months, try adding a set or two per muscle group per week. More weekly sets went with more growth in a meta-analysis [1].
- **Smaller steps** - Move up in the smallest increments you have, or add a rep first.
- **A planned easier week** - After a long block of hard training, a lighter week can help. See [[deload]].

For the longer view, see [[plateaus]].

:::checklist
- Pick | Choose a weight you can lift for 8 to 12 reps with good form.
- Record | Write down weight, sets and reps for every exercise.
- Step | Raise the load by 2 to 10% when you get one or two reps more than planned on two sessions in a row. Go lower for small muscles, higher for large ones [5].
- Stall | No progress for three or four weeks? Check sleep, food and volume before changing the plan.
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Adding weight too fast** - Form breaks down and you cannot hit the reps. Take the smaller step.
- **Chasing weight only** - Reps and sets are progress too [1][6].
- **Not tracking** - If you do not know what you lifted last week, you cannot beat it.
- **Expecting a new record every session** - Judge your progress over weeks, not days.
