## What should you eat before a workout?

The meal before training gives you energy for the session and starts the supply of protein your muscles use afterwards. For most people who lift, a normal meal with protein and carbohydrate some hours before training covers it. You do not need a special pre-workout product.

:::takeaway
A normal meal with **protein and carbs in the hours before training** covers most lifters. Carbs before lifting seem to help most when the session lasts **longer than 45 minutes**, or when it comes after **8 hours or more without food**, such as first thing in the morning [3]. A meal **less than five hours before the end of your workout** may make protein straight after less urgent [1][2].
:::

## Do carbs before lifting improve your workout?

Sometimes. A meta-analysis compared carbohydrate before or during a lifting session with a placebo or water, usually after people had fasted. In the 12 studies that measured total work, carbs let people do more, but the certainty of that evidence was low. The benefit showed up in sessions longer than 45 minutes, and separately in people who had not eaten for 8 hours or more. The more all-out sets in the session, the bigger the effect; a bigger dose of carbs did not help more [3].

A systematic review of 49 studies, mostly in people who lift, comes to a similar answer. Carbs improved performance in 6 of 19 short-term studies, mostly when the comparison group was fasted and the workout had more than 10 sets per muscle group. The authors conclude that carbs are unlikely to help if you have eaten and do up to 10 sets per muscle group [4].

So if you had lunch and train in the afternoon, you are probably covered. If you train early on an empty stomach, or your sessions are long, a carb snack beforehand is worth trying. The ISSN, a sports nutrition society, also calls the evidence for carbs before lifting mixed [1]. See [[carbohydrates]].

## How does your pre-workout meal change what you eat after?

A meal before training keeps supplying amino acids during and after the session. The ISSN says the size and timing of your pre-workout meal may change how much you need protein straight after training [1]. Its protein position stand reasons that if you ate a meal less than five hours before the end of your workout, there is less need for protein right away afterwards [2].

That fits a trial in 21 trained men who took 25 g of protein either right before or right after training for 10 weeks. Both groups gained similar strength and muscle, though the groups were small (9 and 12 men) and both ate less than planned. The authors suggest the window for protein may be several hours wide, depending on when you last ate [5].

In practice: if you ate a proper meal two or three hours before training, your next normal meal is fine. If you train fasted in the morning, eat protein soon after. See [[post-workout-nutrition]].

## Can you train on an empty stomach?

For lifting, probably yes, if you eat enough over the day. A small meta-analysis of four studies found similar gains in muscle and strength whether people lifted fasted or fed. The fasted groups lost slightly more fat, but three of the four studies had a high risk of bias [7], so that is a weak finding.

For fat loss through cardio, fasting does not seem to add anything, though the research is small. A meta-analysis of five studies with 96 healthy adults found only trivial differences in body weight and body fat between cardio after an overnight fast and cardio after a meal; the authors call the data insufficient [11]. In one such study, 20 young women eating in a calorie deficit did an hour of cardio three times a week for four weeks, fasted or after a meal, and lost the same amount of fat [8].

Performance is another matter. A meta-analysis found that eating beforehand improved longer aerobic exercise, but not shorter aerobic sessions [6]. If you feel flat or dizzy training fasted, eat something small beforehand. See [[intermittent-fasting]] if you fast on purpose.

## What about long endurance sessions?

They need more planning. The ISSN advises carbohydrate-rich meals or snacks in the hours before hard endurance exercise that lasts longer than 90 minutes [1]. It also warns against eating or drinking too much shortly before competing, to keep your stomach calm [1]. If you get stomach trouble, try a smaller meal or eat earlier.

## Does caffeine help before training?

It can. The ISSN finds caffeine improves performance at 3 to 6 mg per kg of bodyweight, most often taken about 60 minutes before exercise [9]. Mind the safety limits, though. The European Food Safety Authority sees no safety concern for healthy adults in a single dose of up to 200 mg, also shortly before hard exercise under normal environmental conditions, or in up to 400 mg a day from all sources; in pregnancy and while breastfeeding the limit is 200 mg a day [10]. For anyone above about 67 kg even the low end of the performance range is above 200 mg (3 mg per kg is 210 mg at 70 kg), so keep a single dose at 200 mg or less, check the label and count your coffee. Late in the day caffeine can cost you sleep. See [[caffeine]] and [[sleep]].

## Your checklist

:::checklist
- Meal | A normal meal with protein and carbs some hours before training covers most sessions.
- Carbs | Add a carb snack if you train after 8 or more hours without food, or for longer than 45 minutes.
- Fasted | Lifting fasted is fine if you eat enough over the day; eat protein soon after.
- Long sessions | For hard endurance work over 90 minutes, eat carb-rich meals or snacks in the hours before.
- Stomach | Too full before training? Eat less, or eat earlier.
:::
