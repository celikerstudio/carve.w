## What is beta-alanine?

Beta-alanine is an amino acid your body makes itself. You also get some from meat and poultry [1]. On its own it does little. Its use is that it is the limiting building block for carnosine, a compound stored in your muscles [1]. Taking beta-alanine as a supplement raises the carnosine in your muscle, and carnosine acts as a buffer against the acid that builds up during hard effort [1].

:::takeaway
The ISSN says **4 to 6 g a day for at least 2 to 4 weeks** improves performance, most clearly in efforts that last **1 to 4 minutes** [1]. It is a daily supplement that builds up over weeks, not something you take before a workout.
:::

## How much beta-alanine should you take, and for how long?

The ISSN review describes 4 to 6 g a day, split into doses of 2 g or less [1]. The minimum is two weeks, which raises muscle carnosine by 20 to 30%. Four weeks gives a larger rise, 40 to 60%, and the ISSN calls a loading phase of about four weeks essential for raising carnosine [1]. The highest figures it cites come from single studies: up to 64% after four weeks in one and up to 80% after ten weeks in another [1]. People respond differently. In one study of 4.8 g a day for 5 to 6 weeks, the people who responded most raised their carnosine by 55% on average, the ones who responded least by 15% [1].

Splitting the dose matters. The ISSN notes that single large doses cause strong tingling and did not work for performance, probably because of that tingling, fast changes in acidity and more of it leaving the body unused [1]. In one study it cites, taking beta-alanine with a meal during the loading period raised carnosine further [1].

If you stop, it takes a while to wear off. Muscle carnosine took 6 to 15 weeks to return to normal in one study [1].

The ISSN also says that the best loading and maintenance doses are not known yet, because it is not known how much carnosine a muscle can hold [1].

## Which efforts does beta-alanine help?

Efforts of roughly 1 to 4 minutes at a hard pace, where acid build-up is what makes you stop. A meta-analysis cited by the ISSN found benefits in tasks of 60 to 240 seconds, but not in tasks under 60 seconds [1][2]. Studies of repeated 5-second sprints and of a 400 m sprint found no clear improvement [1].

The ISSN says the effect is most clear in tests where people go on until they cannot continue [1]. The meta-analysis it cites separated those tests from fixed-endpoint performance, such as a race over a set distance. Exercise capacity improved, but no improvement was seen for performance [2]. A larger meta-analysis of 40 studies with 1,461 people came to the same split for efforts of 0.5 to 10 minutes: an effect size of 0.50 for capacity tests and 0.11 for performance, where the interval ran from below zero to above zero [3]. If you race over a fixed distance, the effect is less certain.

Across all tests, the first meta-analysis (15 studies, 360 people, 57 outcomes) found a median improvement of 2.85% compared with placebo, at a median total of 179 g of beta-alanine [2]. The middle half of the results lay between -0.37 and 10.49% [2]. That figure includes tests of every length, also the ones under 60 seconds where nothing was found. The larger meta-analysis found an overall effect size of 0.18, which is small, and no difference for training status or for the total amount taken [3]. For efforts over 4 minutes the benefit is smaller [1]. Beyond about 25 minutes the research is limited and has not shown a consistent benefit [1].

## Why does beta-alanine make you tingle?

Beta-alanine can cause paraesthesia, a prickling feeling, mostly in the face, neck and backs of the hands [1]. It is common above 800 mg of regular (non-sustained-release) beta-alanine at once, it is dose-dependent, and with regular powder it usually fades within 60 to 90 minutes [1]. The ISSN found no evidence that the tingling is harmful [1].

The ISSN says divided lower doses of about 1.6 g or a sustained-release product reduce it [1]. Reduce is the word: smaller doses tingle less, not necessarily not at all.

## Is beta-alanine safe?

For healthy people at the recommended doses, it appears so, although the ISSN calls the information limited [1]. Tingling is the only side effect the ISSN reports [1]. It also mentions a possible drop in taurine, an amino acid that uses the same route into muscle. In animals blood taurine fell by about half, but the ISSN found no human data showing a decrease [1]. There is one limit. The ISSN says there are no safety data on use beyond a year, and that more long-term data are needed [1]. It expects few problems because the body makes beta-alanine itself [1], but that is a reasoning, not a test.

The ISSN statement is about healthy people. The review says nothing about people with a health condition or on medication. If that is you, or if you are pregnant, ask your doctor before you start.

## Does beta-alanine build strength or burn fat?

That is not established. The ISSN lists strength as something that still needs research [1]. It notes that beta-alanine may improve training volume and fatigue in resistance exercise, but that longer studies are needed to see whether that turns into more strength or a different body composition compared with placebo [1]. Fat loss is not among its conclusions at all [1].

## Common mistakes

- **Taking one big dose before training.** Carnosine takes at least two weeks to rise meaningfully [1], so one dose before a workout is not what the studies tested. The ISSN also says single large doses were not effective for performance [1].
- **Quitting after a week.** Carnosine builds over weeks [1].
- **Expecting it to help in very short efforts.** The meta-analysis found no benefit in tasks under 60 seconds [1].
