## What are vitamins and minerals?

Vitamins are essential organic compounds that help regulate metabolic and neurological processes and energy production. Minerals are essential inorganic elements, needed for many metabolic processes [3]. Together they are called micronutrients.

Some vitamins are stored in the body. The fat-soluble ones (A, D, E and K) can build up and become toxic in excessive amounts. Most water-soluble vitamins you pass in urine when you take too much, with exceptions such as vitamin B6, which in excess can damage nerves [3].

:::takeaway
If you eat a varied diet, you probably do not need a vitamin or mineral supplement. The Voedingscentrum (the Dutch nutrition information centre) says most people get enough from food [1], and the sports nutrition position of the ACSM and two dietitian organisations says the same about athletes who eat enough from a variety of foods [2]. Supplements do not improve performance unless they fix a deficiency [2]. More than you need gives no extra health benefit [1], and the upper limit is a ceiling for the total, not a target [4].
:::

## Do most people need a supplement?

No. The Voedingscentrum says most people do not need dietary supplements, because they already get enough nutrients from eating varied. Only special groups are advised a supplement [1]. It adds that with a healthy, varied diet there is no good reason for most people to take extra vitamins and minerals, because it gives no extra health benefit [1].

This is Dutch advice. The sources here do not cover the rules of other countries.

## Who is advised a supplement?

In the Netherlands the Voedingscentrum names, among others, pregnant women (folic acid and vitamin D), young children (vitamin D) and older people (vitamin D) [1]. The advice is often about vitamin D, and there is no supplement advice for minerals [1]. See [[vitamin-d]] for who is advised what and how much.

The Voedingscentrum also advises vitamin B12 for people who eat vegan, enough to reach at least the recommended daily amount [1]. That is the main exception to "eat varied, no supplement" for vegans.

A few groups may benefit from a supplement without an official advice: older people who eat little, people who eat one-sidedly, and people on an extreme weight-loss diet. For them, the Voedingscentrum says the main thing is to improve lifestyle and eating pattern [1].

## What about athletes?

The ACSM position says vitamin and mineral supplements are generally unnecessary for an athlete whose diet gives high energy availability from a variety of nutrient-dense foods [2]. A multivitamin/mineral may be appropriate in some cases when that is not so, for example when an athlete follows an energy-restricted diet or does not eat enough variety [2]. The ISSN, reviewing sports nutrition, adds that few studies find a performance benefit of vitamins in athletes [3].

The ACSM says athletes should be told that vitamin and mineral supplements do not improve performance unless they reverse a pre-existing deficiency [2]. The ISSN agrees that fixing a deficiency can improve health and performance [3].

The ISSN adds that many sport dietitians recommend a low-dose daily multivitamin during heavy training, because dietary analyses of athletes often show they do not eat enough calories [3]. The ISSN also says that daily vitamin and mineral supplementation has not been found to improve exercise capacity in athletes [3]. The Voedingscentrum, for the general public, says a supplement gives no extra health benefit if you eat varied [1].

## When do you eat too few micronutrients?

Mostly when you eat too little or too narrowly. The ACSM position says athletes who often restrict energy, rely on extreme weight-loss practices, cut out one or more food groups, or eat a poorly chosen diet may get less than the optimal amount of micronutrients and may benefit from a supplement [2]. That can describe a [[calorie-deficit]] you keep up for a long time. The position does not say how long or how strict.

The ACSM also says many athletes take micronutrient supplements for no reason even when their food covers their needs. Instead of deciding yourself that you are short, it advises a clinical assessment of your micronutrient status, as part of a look at your whole diet [2]. 

For vitamin D the ISSN says it is often recommended to athletes, especially in indoor sports or cloudy regions, that direct evidence linking it to performance is equivocal, and that dosing should depend on baseline levels, which a physician can measure [3].

## How much is too much?

A supplement can contain more than your body should take for a long time. The Voedingscentrum says that a large amount of a vitamin or mineral over a long time can be harmful, and that supplements sometimes hold more than the upper limit [1]. EFSA defines the upper limit as the maximum level of total chronic daily intake that is not expected to pose a risk [4]. It is a ceiling, not a target. More than you need gives no extra health benefit [1].

For adults, these are some of the limits [4]:

- Vitamin D: 100 microgram (4,000 IU) a day, from all sources together. See [[vitamin-d]].
- Zinc: 25 mg a day, food and supplements together. See [[zinc]].
- Magnesium: 250 mg a day, but only for magnesium from supplements, water and additions to food, not the magnesium in food itself. See [[magnesium]].
- Vitamin A: 3,000 microgram a day. This applies to preformed vitamin A (retinol and retinyl esters), not to beta-carotene.
- Vitamin B6: 12 mg a day.
- Iron: 40 mg a day. EFSA calls this a safe level of intake, because it could not set a true upper limit [4]. Treat it like an upper limit.

Antioxidants deserve a separate warning. The Voedingscentrum says supplements with high doses of antioxidants can raise the risk of cancer. This was found for more than 15 mg of beta-carotene a day and lung cancer, and for more than 15 mg of beta-carotene combined with vitamin A or vitamin E and cancer of the stomach and intestines [1]. EFSA says smokers should avoid food supplements containing beta-carotene [4].

The ACSM says the literature does not support antioxidant supplements to prevent exercise-induced oxidative stress, that there is some evidence they may negatively influence training adaptations, and that athletes who still choose to supplement should not exceed the Tolerable Upper Intake Levels since higher doses could be pro-oxidative [2]. The ISSN also notes that higher antioxidant doses might interfere with training adaptations [3].

:::checklist
- Food first | Eat varied. Most people need no supplement [1].
- Athletes | Supplements do not improve performance unless they fix a deficiency [2].
- Strict diet | A long deficit or a diet that cuts food groups can leave gaps; have your status assessed by a dietitian or doctor [2].
- Dose | More than you need gives no extra health benefit [1]. The upper limit is a ceiling for the total, not a target [4].
- Antioxidants | Supplements with high antioxidant doses can raise cancer risk; this was found for more than 15 mg beta-carotene a day and lung cancer [1]. Smokers should avoid beta-carotene supplements [4].
- Vegan | The Voedingscentrum advises vitamin B12 [1].
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Using a multivitamin instead of fixing your diet.** It can fill a gap when you eat too little or too narrowly [2], but the Voedingscentrum says the main thing is to improve the eating pattern [1].
- **Deciding yourself that you are deficient.** The ACSM advises having your status assessed [2].
- **Adding supplements on top of each other.** Upper limits are for the total from all sources, so a multivitamin, a vitamin D tablet and fortified food add up [4].
- **Treating the upper limit as a dose.** It is a ceiling [4].
- **Taking high-dose antioxidants to recover faster.** The Voedingscentrum links high doses to cancer risk, and the ISSN notes they might interfere with training adaptations [1][3].
