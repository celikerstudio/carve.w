## How much sleep do adults need?

Adults need **7 or more hours** a night, on a regular basis. That is the recommendation of the two main US sleep societies (the AASM and the SRS), and it applies to adults aged 18 to 60 [1]. A panel of experts reached it after reviewing the evidence on sleep and health.

:::takeaway
Sleep **7 or more hours** a night, at fairly regular times [1]. Short sleep makes you hungrier, costs you muscle while dieting and weakens your training. Cut caffeine at least 6 hours before bed [7].
:::

## What does short sleep do to fat loss?

Sleep affects what you lose when you diet. In a small study, 10 overweight adults followed a moderate calorie restriction for 14 days, twice. Once they had an 8.5 hour sleep opportunity each night, once 5.5 hours. With the shorter sleep they lost less of their weight as fat (0.6 kg against 1.4 kg) and more fat-free mass (2.4 kg against 1.5 kg). They also reported more hunger [2]. Fat-free mass is mostly muscle, but also water and organs, so this is not a pure muscle number.

The study was tiny and short, and the authors say so. It fits the point of [[calorie-deficit]]: the deficit sets how much you lose, and sleep seems to influence what that loss is made of.

## Does short sleep make you hungrier?

It does, and the effect on food intake is large. In a study of 12 healthy men, one night of about 4 hours of sleep was followed by about 559 kcal more eaten the next day than after a night of about 8 hours. They were also hungrier before breakfast and dinner [3]. One night in 12 young men proves little about months of short sleep, but it shows the direction.

A longer trial points the same way from the other side. In 80 adults with overweight who normally slept less than 6.5 hours, half got a counseling session aimed at sleeping about 8.5 hours. Over two weeks they slept about 1.2 hours more per night and ate about 270 kcal a day less than the control group, with no diet instructions [4]. See [[hunger-and-satiety]] for how appetite works.

## What does short sleep do to muscle and training?

In a study of 13 young adults, one night without any sleep lowered muscle protein synthesis after a meal by 18%. Their cortisol was 21% higher and testosterone 24% lower the next day [5]. This was one extreme night, not a normal short one, and it measured a few hours of the next day. It does show that a body without sleep is less ready to build muscle.

Performance drops too. A review of 45 trials found that sleep deprivation lowered maximal force, power, speed and endurance, and made the same effort feel harder [6]. The drop in maximal force was the smallest of these. If your lifts feel heavy for days in a row, look at your sleep as well as your [[volume]], your [[rest-days]] and the risk of [[overtraining]].

## Which habits improve sleep?

The best-supported habit here is timing your caffeine. In a home study, 400 mg of caffeine reduced sleep when taken 0, 3 or even 6 hours before bed [7]. The sports nutrition position stand on caffeine says that caffeine can lengthen the time to fall asleep and reduce deep sleep, and that people differ in how strongly it hits them [8]. So stop caffeine at least 6 hours before bed, earlier if you are sensitive. See [[caffeine]] for doses.

The rest of the standard advice is in [[sleep-hygiene]]. The 80-adult trial above used personal sleep hygiene counseling to get people to sleep longer, so such advice can work [4]. Which single tip did the work in that trial is not known.

:::checklist
- Hours | Plan for 7 or more hours in bed, every night.
- Caffeine | Last cup at least 6 hours before bed.
- Rhythm | Go to bed and get up at similar times.
- Dark | Keep the room dark and cool.
- Wind down | Stop hard training and screens in the last hour before bed.
:::

:::check
Going to bed and waking at similar times, including weekends, is common sleep advice, but this article has no source that tested it against sleep length or fat loss. A dark, cool room and no screens in the last hour are also unsourced here. The 6 hour caffeine cut-off comes from one study at 400 mg; the exact safe time for a smaller dose or for you is not established. The 8.5 hour and 5.5 hour figures in the dieting study are time in bed offered, not measured sleep.
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Treating sleep as optional** - Cutting sleep to fit in more training or work can cost you in both [2][6].
- **Dieting on little sleep** - You get hungrier and may lose a larger share of your weight from lean tissue [2][3].
- **Late coffee or a pre-workout** - Caffeine taken 6 hours before bed still disturbed sleep in one study [7].
- **Expecting one number to fit everyone** - The recommendation is 7 or more hours, not exactly 7, for adults aged 18 to 60 [1].
