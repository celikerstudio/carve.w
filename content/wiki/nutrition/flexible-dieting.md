## What is flexible dieting?

Flexible dieting means you set targets for calories and protein (and often carbs and fat) and reach them with whatever foods you like. No food is banned. It is also called IIFYM, for "if it fits your macros". The opposite is a rigid diet: a fixed list of allowed foods, set meals, and foods you are never supposed to eat.

:::takeaway
Many diets work about equally well, from low-fat to low-carb, so you can pick one you can keep up [1]. In one small trial with people who lifted, a flexible and a rigid diet with the same deficit gave **similar fat loss** over 10 weeks [3]. Flexible dieting still runs on a calorie deficit, and food choice matters for how much you eat: on an ultra-processed diet people ate about **500 kcal a day more** without feeling hungrier [6].
:::

## Why can you choose your own foods?

Because no single diet type wins for body composition. The ISSN, a sports nutrition society, concludes in its position stand on diets that a wide range of approaches, from low-fat to low-carbohydrate or ketogenic and everything in between, can be similarly effective for improving body composition. It adds that this allows flexibility when you design a diet [1]. It also says the long-term success of a diet depends on compliance [1].

A large trial shows what that looks like. 609 adults with overweight or obesity followed either a healthy low-fat or a healthy low-carbohydrate diet for 12 months. They lost 5.3 and 6.0 kg, a difference that was not significant [2]. What drives fat loss is the deficit, see [[calorie-deficit]]. Which foods make up that deficit is mostly up to you.

## Does flexible dieting work as well as a strict diet?

In the one trial that tested it in people who lift, about as well. 23 resistance-trained men and women ate about 20% below their needs for 10 weeks. One group got daily targets for carbs, protein and fat, counted them and chose its own foods. The other got a fixed plan of specific foods with the same calories and protein. Both groups also followed a training program, which they did on their own without supervision. The flexible group lost 2.3 kg of fat and the rigid group 3.2 kg, a difference that was not significant [3]. About 40% of the people who started dropped out, slightly more in the flexible group, and the authors suggest the meal plan was easier to follow because it removed the planning [3].

After the diet, everyone ate freely for 10 more weeks. The flexible group gained more fat-free mass in that phase (1.7 kg against a loss of 0.7 kg), but the authors do not attribute that to the diet, because training and food intake did not differ between groups [3]. It was a small study. It shows that flexible dieting can work, and it does not show that it beats a strict plan. The authors also warn that hitting very specific macro targets can itself turn into an unhealthy relationship with food [3].

## Is a flexible mindset healthier?

Whether flexible dieting is easier to keep up has hardly been tested; in the one trial above, slightly more people dropped out of the flexible group [3]. What exists beyond that is observational research on two styles of dieting. Psychologists measure two styles of dieting with a questionnaire: flexible control, with room to give and take, and rigid control, with strict rules. These are styles of thinking, which is not the same thing as tracking macros. In one study of more than 50,000 people in a weight loss program, and a second of 1,838 people from the German population, flexible control went with lower BMI and less binge eating, and in the weight loss program also with a higher chance of losing weight over a year. Rigid control went with higher BMI and more binge eating [4]. In 188 women without obesity, rigid dieting went with eating disorder symptoms and mood problems, and flexible dieting did not [5].

These studies show links and cannot show cause and effect; the authors of the second study say so themselves [5]. Someone who already struggles with food may also be the one who sets rigid rules. So they do not prove that a flexible diet prevents binges.

## Do food choices still matter?

Yes, for two reasons. The first is how much you end up eating. In a study where 20 adults could eat as much as they wanted, they ate about 500 kcal a day more on an ultra-processed diet than on an unprocessed one, although the meals offered had the same calories, sugar, fat and fibre. They gained 0.9 kg in two weeks on the ultra-processed diet and lost 0.9 kg on the unprocessed diet [6]. They did not feel hungrier on the ultra-processed diet; they simply ate more [6]. If you do not count, food choice can push you past your target without you noticing. See [[hunger-and-satiety]].

The second is everything a calorie count does not show, such as fibre, vitamins and minerals. See [[fiber]] and [[micronutrients]].

A practical rule: build most meals from foods that fill you up and bring protein, and fit the foods you would miss into what is left. Planned treats are part of the diet.

## Do you have to track everything?

No, but it may help. A systematic review of 22 studies consistently found that people who recorded their food, exercise or weight lost more weight [7]. That is a link, the evidence was weak, and most people in the studies were white women [7]. Flexible dieting usually relies on tracking, because without a count "it fits" is a guess. If tracking every gram wears you down, track protein and calories only, or track for a few weeks until you can estimate portions. See [[macros]] and [[calories]].

:::checklist
- Targets | Set calories and protein for your goal. Carbs and fat can move around.
- Base | Build most meals from foods that fill you up and bring protein.
- Room | Plan the foods you would miss into the day instead of banning them.
- Track | Log your food, at least until you know your portions [7].
- Check | Judge your weekly average weight, not one day.
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Treating "it fits" as "anything goes"** - Calories still decide fat loss, and people ate more on ultra-processed food [6].
- **Strict food bans** - Rigid rules went together with more binge eating in observational studies [4][5].
- **Guessing instead of tracking** - Without a count, flexible dieting is just eating; self-monitoring is linked to weight loss [7].
- **Looking for the one right diet** - Low-fat and low-carb gave similar results after a year [2].
