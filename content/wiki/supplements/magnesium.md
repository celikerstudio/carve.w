## What is magnesium?

Magnesium is a mineral you get from food, and also from supplements, drinking water and foods with magnesium added [2]. This article covers what the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) says about how much adults take in, and where the upper limit for supplements is.

:::takeaway
EFSA's adequate intake for adults is **350 mg a day for men and 300 mg for women** [1]. The upper limit of **250 mg a day** applies only to magnesium from supplements, water and added to food, not to the magnesium that is naturally in food [2]. Mild diarrhoea is the first side effect of too much supplement magnesium [2].
:::

## How much magnesium do adults need?

EFSA could not work out an average requirement or a recommended intake for magnesium. It set an adequate intake instead, based on what healthy people in the EU actually eat. For adults that is 350 mg a day for men and 300 mg for women. Pregnant and breastfeeding women have the same adequate intake as other women, because EFSA found no evidence that they need more [1].

So the number describes what healthy people typically take in. It is not a dose that was shown to do something. The sources do not say how many people fall short. The 2006 report does name what makes it harder to keep magnesium: diarrhoea and bowel diseases reduce absorption, and the kidneys can lose more magnesium in diseases that make you pass a lot of urine, such as diabetes, and with most diuretics (water pills) or alcohol [2].

## What is the upper limit for magnesium?

The upper limit is 250 mg a day. It applies to magnesium from supplements, from water and from magnesium added to food and drinks. It does not include the magnesium that is naturally in food and drinks [2]. EFSA's 2025 overview table still lists the same 250 mg for adults [3].

The report gives two reasons for leaving food out. First, magnesium in food has not been shown to cause diarrhoea or other harm in healthy people, probably because it is bound in the food and does not dissolve easily. The salts in supplements, water and drugs do dissolve easily, and they have a laxative effect that grows with the dose [2]. Second, the 250 mg comes from studies in which people took supplements on top of their normal food. Nobody measured the magnesium in that food, so the limit cannot be set for intake from all sources [2].

That is why the limit (250 mg) is lower than the adequate intake (300 to 350 mg) without being a contradiction. The adequate intake describes your total intake. The limit is for the magnesium that comes from supplements, water and additions to food [1][2]. Mineral water counts: the report cites German mineral water with a median of 33.5 mg of magnesium per litre and 101 mg per litre at the 95th percentile [2].

The limit was set for readily dissociable salts, such as chloride, sulphate, aspartate and lactate, and for magnesium oxide [2]. The report names these salts as examples and does not give a separate figure for other forms, such as glycinate [2]. The limit is a ceiling. Nothing in the sources suggests working towards it.

## What happens if you take too much magnesium?

Mild diarrhoea. In the report, this is the most sensitive undesirable effect of easily dissociable magnesium salts. Magnesium salts in the gut have an osmotic effect, which is why some, such as Epsom salt (magnesium sulphate), are used as laxatives [2]. It occurred in a small percentage of adults at supplement doses of about 360 to 365 mg magnesium a day, on top of the magnesium in their normal food, the lowest dose at which an effect showed up. The upper limit is set at 250 mg a day, the highest dose without an observed adverse effect [2].

Kidney function matters. The report names severely impaired kidney function as a risk factor for too much magnesium in the blood. In patients who were given very large doses, several grams a day, it caused low blood pressure, nausea, vomiting and slowed breathing [2]. Those doses are far above 250 mg. If you have a kidney condition, ask your doctor before taking magnesium supplements.

Magnesium can also affect other things you take. The report mentions that magnesium hydroxide, as used in antacids, lowered iron absorption in a trial (it found that a water-soluble magnesium salt did not interfere with iron), and that magnesium may affect zinc absorption and certain drugs such as tetracycline, penicillin and digoxin [2]. If you use medication, ask your pharmacist.

## Does magnesium help sleep, cramps or training?

For training, the International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) writes that most well-controlled research indicates magnesium supplementation does not affect exercise performance in athletes unless there is a deficiency [4]. The dose in that table is 500 mg a day, which is twice the EU limit above, so do not read it as a dose to take [4][2].

The sources behind this article do not cover sleep or muscle cramps, so this article says nothing about those. See [[sleep]] for what the wiki covers on sleep.

:::checklist
- Intake | EFSA's adequate intake: **350 mg** (men) or **300 mg** (women) a day [1].
- Limit | At most **250 mg** a day from supplements, water and added magnesium. Magnesium in food does not count [2].
- Warning sign | Diarrhoea [2].
- Ask first | Kidney disease or medication: ask your doctor or pharmacist [2].
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Counting the magnesium in your food against the 250 mg limit.** The limit does not include it [2].
- **Reading the adequate intake as a dose to supplement.** It describes total intake, based on what healthy people eat [1].
- **Ignoring diarrhoea.** It is the first side effect of too much supplement magnesium [2].
- **Forgetting the water.** Mineral water with magnesium counts towards the limit [2].
