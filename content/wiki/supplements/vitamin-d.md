## What is vitamin D?

Vitamin D is a vitamin you get from the sun, from food and from supplements [1]. This article covers what the Dutch nutrition information centre (Voedingscentrum), the Dutch Health Council (Gezondheidsraad) and the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) say about how much you need, who is advised a supplement, and where the upper limit is.

:::takeaway
In the Netherlands, the recommended amount is **10 microgram (400 IU) a day**, and **20 microgram (800 IU)** from age 70 [1][4]. More than that gives no extra health benefit [1]. The upper limit for adults is **100 microgram (4,000 IU) a day** from all sources together [4][5]. That is a ceiling for the total.
:::

## How much vitamin D do you need?

In the Netherlands, the recommended daily amount is 10 microgram for everyone. People aged 70 and older should get 20 microgram a day [1][2]. Supplement labels often use IU instead of microgram. One microgram equals 40 IU [4], so 10 microgram is 400 IU and 20 microgram is 800 IU.

The Voedingscentrum says many people reach 10 microgram through the sun and healthy food [1]. The Gezondheidsraad estimates that for a light-skinned person who is regularly in the sun, roughly two thirds of the vitamin D comes from sunlight and one third from food, averaged over the year [2].

## Who is advised a vitamin D supplement in the Netherlands?

Some groups need more than they can get from sunlight and food. For these groups the Voedingscentrum advises a supplement [1]:

- Children aged 0 to 3: 10 microgram a day.
- Everyone aged 4 to 69 with a dark or tinted skin colour: 10 microgram.
- Everyone aged 4 to 69 who is not outdoors much during the day, or who covers the face: 10 microgram.
- Women aged 50 to 69: 10 microgram.
- Everyone aged 70 and older: 20 microgram.
- Pregnant women: 10 microgram.

This is the Dutch advice. The sources here do not cover other countries.

## Does the winter sun give you vitamin D?

Not in the Netherlands. The Voedingscentrum says that in the winter months the sun is too low there to make vitamin D [1]. From March to October its advice is to be outside every day between 11:00 and 15:00 for 15 to 30 minutes, with an uncovered face and uncovered hands [1].

## Why does EFSA say 15 microgram and the Netherlands 10?

EFSA sets the adequate intake for adults at 15 microgram (600 IU) a day [3]. An adequate intake is the value EFSA estimates when it cannot establish a recommended intake from requirements [3].

Both figures are meant for people who get little vitamin D from the sun. EFSA gives its 15 microgram under the assumption of minimal skin synthesis, and adds that when your skin does make vitamin D, the requirement from food is lower or may even be zero [3]. The Gezondheidsraad says it derived its norms only for insufficient sun exposure [2].

The Dutch figure comes from a target blood level. For ages 4 to 70 the Gezondheidsraad aims for a serum 25(OH)D of at least 30 nmol/l all year, which gives 10 microgram a day. For people from 70 it aims for at least 50 nmol/l, which gives 20 microgram [2]. The EFSA document used here does not say which blood level EFSA aimed for, so the sources do not explain the gap between 10 and 15 microgram. They are two separate derivations, not one number and a correction of it.

## Is more vitamin D better?

No. The Voedingscentrum says you do not need to take more than the recommended amounts, because that gives no extra health benefit [1]. You can only get too much if you use too many supplements for a long time [1].

The upper limit is the maximum level of total chronic daily intake, from all sources, that is not expected to pose a risk [4]. For adults it is 100 microgram (4,000 IU) a day [1][4]. EFSA reviewed the limit in 2023 and kept it at 100 microgram for adults, including pregnant and breastfeeding women, and for ages 11 to 17. For ages 1 to 10 it is 50 microgram [5]. It based the limit on persistent high calcium in the urine (hypercalciuria), seen at 250 microgram a day in two trials, and applied a safety factor of 2.5 [5]. EFSA says European populations are unlikely to exceed it, except regular users of food supplements with high doses of vitamin D [5].

For adults under 70 the limit is ten times the Dutch recommended amount. It is a ceiling for the total. Nothing in the sources suggests working towards it.

## Does vitamin D raise testosterone?

A systematic review looked at 52 studies of 27 proposed testosterone boosters, including 10 studies of vitamin D3. It found that most boosters fail to increase total testosterone, and vitamin D was not among the few it rated effective or possibly effective [6]. The review looked at male athletes, men with low testosterone, infertile men and healthy men. It does not say in which of these groups the vitamin D studies were done [6]. See [[testosterone-boosters]].

The sources behind this article do not cover vitamin D and muscle growth or strength, so this article says nothing about those.

## Common mistakes

- **Taking much more than advised for extra benefit.** The Voedingscentrum says it gives no extra health benefit [1].
- **Treating the upper limit as a dose.** It is a ceiling for the total from all sources [4].
- **Treating 15 microgram or 10 microgram as the wrong one.** Both are for little sun, and they come from separate derivations [2][3].
- **Counting on the sun in a Dutch winter.** The sun is too low to make vitamin D then [1].
