## What is a calorie?

A calorie is a unit of energy. Food labels say "calories" but mean kilocalories (kcal): the energy your body gets from eating that food. You spend it on staying alive, on digesting what you eat, and on moving.

:::takeaway
To lose fat, eat a little less than you burn and aim to lose **0.5 to 1% of your bodyweight a week** [1]. To build muscle, eat a little more than you burn. How much you burn depends on your size and how much you move; [[tdee]] shows how to work it out.
:::

## Where do calories come from?

- **Protein** - 4 kcal per gram
- **Carbohydrates** - 4 kcal per gram
- **Fat** - 9 kcal per gram
- **Alcohol** - 7 kcal per gram

That's why fat adds up fast. One tablespoon of cooking oil is about 120 kcal, and it rarely makes it into anyone's food diary.

## How many calories do you need?

Three things decide it: what your body burns at rest, what it costs to digest your food, and how much you move. Together that's your total daily energy expenditure, or [[tdee]].

The resting part is the biggest one for most people. You can estimate it from your weight, height, age and sex with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation. In the study that introduced it, the older Harris-Benedict equation came out about 5% too high against measured resting energy use [2]. Multiply the result by your activity level and you have a starting number.

Treat that number as a guess. Eat it for two or three weeks and watch your weekly average weight. If the average doesn't move, that's your maintenance.

## How big should a deficit be?

Fat loss comes from a calorie deficit you keep up over time. Which diet you use to get there (low-carb, low-fat or anything in between) matters much less [3].

Speed matters more than most people think. The usual advice for keeping muscle is to lose 0.5 to 1% of your bodyweight a week [1]. For someone of 80 kg that's 0.4 to 0.8 kg. In a study with elite athletes who all trained with weights, the group that lost about 0.7% a week gained lean mass, while the group that lost faster did not [4].

If you have a lot of fat to lose, you can go faster at the start. The leaner you get, the slower it should be [3].

## Why does weight loss slow down?

On the same deficit, your weight won't keep dropping at the same pace. As you get lighter you burn less, so the gap between what you eat and what you burn gets smaller. A model of human metabolism by Hall and colleagues found that bodyweight responds to a change in intake slowly, with a half-time of about a year [5].

That's normal, and it's the reason to work out your number again every few kilos.

## Why is counting harder than it looks?

In a 1992 study, people who said they couldn't lose weight on less than 1,200 kcal a day were measured precisely. Their metabolism was normal. They had underreported what they ate by 47% on average, and overreported their exercise by 51% [6].

Small things are easy to miss: cooking oil, drinks, a few bites while cooking. Logging every meal, including the small ones, matters more than having a perfect target.

## Does the kind of calorie matter?

For your weight, the total matters most [3]. But what you eat changes how much you end up eating. In a month-long trial where people could eat as much as they wanted, they ate about 500 kcal a day more on ultra-processed food than on unprocessed food. The meals on offer were matched for calories, sugar, fat and fiber. On the ultra-processed diet they gained 0.9 kg in two weeks [7].

Protein is the other lever. In a deficit, eating more of it helps you keep your muscle [3]. See [[protein]] for how much.

## How do you eat to build muscle?

Building muscle works best with a steady calorie surplus and training that keeps asking more of you [3]. See [[bulking]] and [[progressive-overload]].

:::checklist
- Start | Estimate your [[tdee]] and set a deficit that loses 0.5 to 1% of your weight a week.
- Track | Log everything for two weeks, oil and drinks included.
- Check | Compare weekly averages, not single days.
- Adjust | No change after two or three weeks? Eat 100 to 200 kcal less and check again.
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Cutting too hard** - Losing fast can mean keeping less muscle, especially once you're lean [3][4].
- **Trusting the formula over the scale** - Equations are estimates. Your weekly average is the real test.
- **Forgetting the small stuff** - Oil, sauces and drinks add up and are easy to leave out.
- **Ignoring protein** - In a deficit, more protein helps you keep your muscle [3].
