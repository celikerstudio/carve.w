## What are testosterone boosters?

Testosterone boosters are supplements that promise higher testosterone, and with it more muscle and strength. In one study of 50 products, 90% claimed to boost testosterone [3].

:::takeaway
In a review of 52 studies, most "boosters" **failed to raise total testosterone** compared with placebo [2]. Tribulus appears to have no effect on strength or body composition, and the ISSN does not recommend D-aspartic acid [1]. Prohormones lead to a **positive doping test** and are best skipped by everyone [1]. If you think your testosterone is low, see a doctor instead of buying a blend.
:::

## Do testosterone boosters work?

Mostly not. A systematic review compared 27 proposed boosters with placebo, in 52 studies. Its conclusion was that most fail to increase total testosterone. A few ingredients were rated effective or possibly effective in specific groups, but those ratings rest on very few studies [2]. The review also rated the herbs Eurycoma longifolia and ashwagandha as possibly effective, on three studies each [2]. The German risk assessment institute (BfR) and other European authorities advise against ashwagandha supplements, after reports of liver damage including liver failure [5]. See [[ashwagandha]].

The products themselves are a weak point. A study of 50 testosterone-booster supplements counted 109 different ingredients. For 27 of them (24.8%) a study had shown a rise in testosterone, and for 15 of those 27 other studies showed no change or a drop. For 67 ingredients (61.5%) there were no studies on testosterone at all [3]. The products contained a median of 272% of the recommended daily allowance of zinc, and 13 went over an upper intake limit, mostly for magnesium [3].

### Does tribulus work?

Probably not. Several human studies, of tribulus alone or combined with other ingredients, found no effect on body composition or strength during resistance training [1]. The review above looked at four tribulus studies, and tribulus was not among the ingredients it rated as effective [2].

### Does D-aspartic acid work?

No. In studies of men who already lifted weights, it did not raise testosterone [1][4]. In one of them, 24 men took 6 g a day, 3 g a day or a placebo for 14 days. 6 g a day lowered total and free testosterone, and 3 g made no difference [4]. In another trial, 22 men took either 6 g a day or a placebo for 12 weeks. Testosterone did not change, and strength and muscle grew about the same in both groups [1]. The ISSN does not recommend D-aspartic acid to improve muscle health [1].

### What about fenugreek?

The ISSN puts fenugreek among the supplements with little to no evidence. Two small studies in men who lifted weights found better training results with fenugreek extract, but the ISSN says more research in other groups is needed before any further recommendations can be made [1]. So it is too early to say.

### What about zinc and vitamin D?

A review of 10 vitamin D studies and 5 zinc and magnesium studies did not rate either as raising testosterone [2]. The ISSN looked at ZMA, a zinc, magnesium and vitamin B6 blend. Two of three studies found no change in testosterone. The one study that did find a rise also reported depletions of zinc and magnesium, so the ISSN notes the rise could come from a deficient nutritional status instead of from the supplement itself [1]. See [[zinc]] and [[vitamin-d]].

:::check
Vitamin D and zinc may help men who are deficient.
:::

### What are prohormones?

Prohormones (such as androstenedione and DHEA) are precursors of testosterone or other anabolic steroids. The ISSN found virtually no evidence of better training results in younger men with normal hormone levels. Most studies show no effect on testosterone, and some may raise estrogen and lower HDL cholesterol. Its conclusion is that prohormones appear to have no training value [1].

They also show up in drug tests. Supplements with prohormones lead to a positive test for anabolic steroids, and supplements that contained them, knowingly or not, are believed to have contributed to positive tests among athletes [1]. If you compete in a tested sport, look for products certified by a third-party program such as Informed Choice or NSF Certified for Sport [1].

## What should you do if you think your testosterone is low?

Go to your doctor. A blood test can show whether it is low, and a supplement cannot. Sleep and eating enough matter for your training anyway ([[sleep]], [[calories]]), but they do not replace a test.

:::checklist
- Low testosterone | Ask your doctor for a test instead of guessing.
- Blends | Most ingredients in one study of 50 products had never been tested for their effect on testosterone [3]. Do not pay for the label.
- Prohormones | Skip them. They appear to have no training value and may lower HDL cholesterol [1].
- Tested sport | Pick products with third-party testing [1].
- Training | Keep using [[progressive-overload]].
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Trusting the label.** Only 27 of 109 ingredients in one study of 50 products had any study showing a rise in testosterone [3].
- **Using prohormones.** They appear to have no training value and cause positive doping tests [1].
- **Guessing instead of testing.** Whether your testosterone is low is a medical question.
