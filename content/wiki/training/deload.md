## What is a deload?

A deload is a period in which you deliberately do less training, to recover. A widely cited definition comes from a 2023 Delphi study, in which coaches of strength and physique athletes (powerlifters, weightlifters, strongman and bodybuilders) answered questionnaires in three rounds until they agreed. Thirty-four coaches took part in the first round and 21 finished the last. Based on what the coaches said and on earlier research, the authors proposed this definition: "Deloading is a period of reduced training stress designed to mitigate physiological and psychological fatigue, promote recovery, and enhance preparedness for subsequent training" [1].

A deload is not a taper. A taper comes before a competition and aims for peak performance. A deload can happen anywhere in the programme and aims at recovery and getting ready for the next block [1].

:::takeaway
A deload is a period of **reduced training stress** meant to lower fatigue and get you ready for the next block [1]. Coaches of strength and physique athletes use fewer sets, fewer reps or fewer sessions [1]. Competitive athletes often deload every 4 to 8 weeks, but that rests on an assumption, not on evidence [1][2]. Whether you **need** a deload is **not shown** [1].
:::

## How do you deload?

The coaches agreed that a deload could reduce overall training demand by reducing volume, intensity or frequency. On volume, they agreed it can come from fewer sets per session, fewer repetitions per set, or fewer sessions. The authors say there was universal agreement that volume should go down in a deload [1].

On intensity (the weight on the bar and how hard you push), the coaches agreed it could go up or down. It can also stay the same, but only when volume is reduced [1]. In other words, the same weight is fine if you do clearly less of it.

The coaches agreed that you can keep the same exercises, which avoids the soreness that a new exercise can bring, or bring in new ones. They agreed that a deload should focus on technique in the main exercises, keep multi-joint exercises, and may cut or change accessory exercises. Session length can drop or stay the same [1]. They also agreed that volume could be kept at the minimum that still works [1].

What athletes actually do looks similar. In a survey of 246 competitive strength and physique athletes, a typical deload lasted about 6 days (6.4 ± 1.7). Most reported fewer sets per week (79%), about half reported fewer reps per set (53%), and most kept the same number of sessions (63%). Most also lifted lighter weights and stopped further from failure, and they generally kept the same exercises [2]. That tells you what a group of competitors says it does, not what works. It was a convenience sample, so it also does not say what most lifters do.

## When do you deload?

The coaches agreed that a deload could be planned in advance or used when an athlete felt physically or mentally tired, wherever they were in the programme. How often, they said, depends partly on how the athlete responds to training and on other commitments [1]. In the survey, 47% planned deloads ahead, 13% only reacted to how they felt, and 39% did both. Common reasons were that the programme said so, soreness or joint aches, and performance that stalled or dropped [2].

The survey athletes deloaded every 5.6 weeks on average, with a wide spread (± 2.3 weeks) [2]. The authors of the Delphi study describe regular planned deloading every 4 to 8 weeks as common, and say it likely rests on the assumption that periods of reduced training stress are needed for adaptation. They also write that there is limited evidence that planned, periodised training is better than non-periodised training, so regular planned deloads might not be necessary [1]. Treat "every 4 to 8 weeks" as a habit among coaches, not as a finding.

## Do you need a deload at all?

That is not known. The authors write that it is unclear whether deloading is a necessary part of a programme to avoid the harmful effects of non-functional overreaching and overtraining syndrome. They also note that non-functional overreaching is rare in strength sports and resistance-trained people, even when researchers tried to cause it [1]. The coaches did agree that a deload could lower the risk of overreaching, injury and training monotony, and could help you stick to the programme. The authors call the injury link plausible but under-researched [1].

The few direct studies tested stopping completely, not an easier week. The Delphi authors describe two studies in which a group that stopped training for three weeks after six weeks of training was compared with a group that trained continuously, over 15 or 24 weeks. There was no significant difference in strength or muscle size [1]. In a randomized trial in 39 resistance-trained people, a full week without training halfway through 9 weeks gave similar lower-body muscle size, endurance and power, but less gain in lower-body strength than training straight through [3]. The authors of the Delphi study add that it is not known whether a deload makes your body respond better to training afterwards [1].

Even among competitors who deload, about two in three (65%) said they thought they could keep progressing without deloads [2]. Only athletes who already deloaded took part in that survey [2].

The ACSM position stand of 2026, which does not mention deloading, found that periodization (planned variation in training) was not significantly better than non-periodized training when progressive overload is applied well [4].

## What if you feel worn down for a long time?

An easier week is meant for ordinary fatigue. If you have felt flat for weeks and your performance does not come back, the problem may be bigger than a deload fixes. See [[overtraining]], which also covers what to rule out first, such as eating too little and sleeping badly.

## How could you use this?

If you feel worn out or your lifts have stalled, you can try a deload. Design it from the options above: fewer sets, fewer reps or fewer sessions, with the exercises you know. The weight can stay the same if the volume drops. Most of this comes from coaches and athletes in strength and physique sports, not from people who train for general fitness. See [[volume]], [[intensity]] and [[rpe]] for those terms.

:::checklist
- Definition | Reduced training stress to lower fatigue and prepare for the next block [1].
- How | Fewer sets, fewer reps or fewer sessions [1].
- Weight | Can stay the same, but only if volume goes down [1].
- Timing | Planned ahead or based on how you feel [1][2].
- Frequency | Every 4 to 8 weeks is common practice, not consensus [1].
- Need | Not shown to be necessary [1].
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Treating "every 4 to 8 weeks" as a rule.** It is common practice among coaches, and the authors say regular planned deloads might not be necessary [1].
- **Keeping the volume the same.** The coaches agreed that volume goes down in a deload. The weight can stay the same only if it does [1].
- **Expecting a deload to fix a long slump.** If you are worn out for weeks, see [[overtraining]].
