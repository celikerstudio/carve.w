## How much do you need to move each week?

:::takeaway
Aim for **150 to 300 minutes** of moderate activity a week, plus strength training on **2 or more days** [1]. Short bouts count. Doing a little is better than nothing, and sitting less helps even if you only add light movement.
:::

The World Health Organization sets the same target for all adults, older adults included; pregnant women have a separate recommendation. You need 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity a week, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, or a mix of the two [1]. Aerobic activity is anything that moves your large muscles in a rhythm for a while: walking, cycling, swimming, running.

The guideline sets intensity on a 0 to 10 effort scale. Moderate is usually a 5 or 6, and vigorous is usually a 7 or 8 [1]. For most people brisk walking feels like a 5 or 6, though this depends on your fitness. Vigorous minutes are harder, so you need fewer of them, and you can mix the two kinds [1].

The target is about your health. For what to eat while you lose fat, see [[calories]]. Going past 300 minutes has extra benefits, but the guideline rates that as less certain, and the gains get smaller [1].

## Do you also need strength training?

Yes. On top of the aerobic minutes, the guideline asks for muscle-strengthening activity at moderate or higher intensity on 2 or more days a week, working all the major muscle groups [1]. The guideline says this gives extra health benefits on top of the aerobic activity.

In a review of studies that followed people over time, muscle-strengthening activity went with a lower risk of death and of major diseases. The biggest drop in risk, around 10 to 20%, showed up at roughly 30 to 60 minutes a week, and it was unclear whether more helped [5]. These were observational studies, so they show a link, not proof. Two sessions of 30 minutes already land in that range. [[full-body]] shows one way to train all muscle groups in a session.

## Does a little bit still count?

It does. The guideline says that for everyone, some physical activity is better than none, and that people who do not meet the targets still gain health from what they do [1]. The guideline describes a curve: health benefits start below the targets, and the extra gain gets smaller at higher amounts [1].

Steps show the same pattern. A meta-analysis of 15 groups of adults found that people who walked more had a lower risk of dying during follow-up. This is observational, so it shows a link, not proof. Risk fell as steps went up, until about 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day in people aged 60 and over, and until about 8,000 to 10,000 steps in younger adults [2]. The 10,000 steps you hear about is not a magic line.

## Do short bouts count too?

Yes. The 2010 guideline said activity only counted in bouts of at least 10 minutes. The 2020 guideline dropped that rule, so moderate or vigorous activity of any length now counts toward your weekly minutes [1]. A systematic review of mostly observational studies found links with better health for bouts shorter than 10 minutes as well [4].

In practice, three 5-minute brisk walks count the same toward your weekly minutes as one 15-minute walk. Randomized studies have only tested bouts of 10 minutes or longer [4], so the evidence for very short bouts comes from observational studies. Taking the stairs, walking to the shop or a quick lap around the block counts if you do it at a brisk pace, around a 5 or 6 out of 10.

## Does walking count as exercise?

Walking is aerobic activity, and the guideline lists it as an example [1]. If your pace feels like a 5 or 6 out of 10, it is moderate, and 30 minutes on five days gives you 150 minutes. Slow strolling is light activity. It does not count toward the 150 minutes, but it still helps, as the next section shows.

## Why does sitting less matter?

The guideline tells adults to limit the time they spend sedentary, and says that replacing sitting with activity of any intensity, light included, gives health benefits [1]. It did not set a limit in hours, because the evidence was not strong enough to name one.

A meta-analysis of eight studies where people wore a movement sensor (average age 63) found that more activity at any intensity, light included, and less sitting both went with a lower risk of dying in middle-aged and older adults. The people who sat the most had about 2.6 times the risk of the people who sat the least [3]. This is observational, so part of the gap may come from other differences between these people.

The guideline could not name a safe number of hours, but it does say that if you sit a lot, aiming for more than the recommended 150 to 300 minutes helps to offset it [1]. Standing up, taking a call on foot or walking after dinner is worth doing even if you do not reach 150 minutes.

## How do you start if you do nothing now?

Start small and build up. The guideline says to begin with small amounts and slowly increase how often, how hard and how long you move [1].

For most healthy adults there is no need for a medical check before starting. The guideline says pre-exercise medical clearance is generally unnecessary for people who are not active now and have no contraindications [1]. If you have a heart condition, chest pain on effort or another condition that affects exercise, ask your doctor first. The guideline also says to see a healthcare provider if new symptoms appear while you build up [1].

One way to build up (the guideline gives no schedule, this is only an example):

- **Week 1 to 2** - A 10 minute walk on most days.
- **Week 3 to 4** - Make it 15 to 20 minutes, or add a second walk.
- **From week 5** - Keep adding a few minutes until you reach about 30 minutes on five days.
- **Strength** - Add one full-body session in the first weeks, then a second.

Pick up the pace once a walk feels easy.
