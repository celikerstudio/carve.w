## What is collagen?

Collagen is the most abundant structural protein in your body. It is about a third of all the protein in you, and it makes up most of the dry weight of your tendons [3]. Collagen supplements are usually hydrolysed collagen, also called collagen peptides: collagen cut into small pieces so that it dissolves easily. They come from cattle, pigs, fish or poultry [3].

:::takeaway
For skin, EFSA found that one collagen product (2.5 g a day for 8 weeks) had **not been shown to improve how the skin works** [1]. For joints, EFSA did not accept the evidence for collagen hydrolysate (10 g a day) either [2], and a review counts five small trials with some benefit, one of them the trial EFSA rejected [2][3]. For tendons, small trials suggest a benefit with heavy training [4]. For muscle, collagen does not replace whey or other complete protein [3].
:::

## Does collagen help your skin?

EFSA, the European food safety authority, looked at this for one product, a porcine collagen hydrolysate called VeriSol P. The company wanted to claim it maintains skin health through more elasticity and fewer wrinkles [1]. EFSA considered two human studies [1]:

- 114 women took 2.5 g a day for 8 weeks. The main outcome was the volume of eye wrinkles. EFSA said this study did not assess a function of the skin, so no conclusions could be drawn from it.
- 69 women took 2.5 g or 5 g a day for 8 weeks. There was no significant difference between the groups in water loss through the skin or in skin hydration at any time.

EFSA concluded that a cause and effect relationship had not been established between this product and a change in skin elasticity leading to an improvement in skin function [1].

The last part matters. EFSA accepted a change in skin elasticity as a benefit only if it leads to an improvement in skin function [1]. In the second study, elasticity and hydration were the main outcomes, but the only skin function EFSA could judge was the water barrier, and there was no difference [1]. EFSA did not conclude that elasticity stayed the same. It concluded that no better-working skin had been shown.

That opinion is about one product and one claim from 2013. It does not say that no collagen supplement can help skin, and it does not say that any can. Other skin studies are not covered here.

## Does collagen help your joints?

Small trials report some benefit, and the regulator was not convinced. Both are in the record.

A systematic review of 15 randomised trials of collagen with exercise found that five of them, lasting 3 to 6 months, reported less joint pain or better joint function [3]. The doses were 5 or 10 g a day, except in one trial that used 40 mg of a different type of collagen [3]. The people were mostly recreational athletes with joint discomfort, and the trials included knee pain, ankle instability and Achilles tendon problems [3].

EFSA looked at one of these trials, a 24-week study of 10 g a day in 147 active student athletes with joint discomfort, when a company applied for a joint health claim in 2011 [2]. Everyone's pain went down, but there were no significant differences between the collagen and placebo groups once the results were adjusted for the 15 outcomes that were tested [2]. Only 97 of the 147 people were in the analysis [2]. A separate analysis of people with knee pain was not planned in advance, and EFSA drew no conclusions from it [2]. EFSA concluded that a cause and effect relationship between collagen hydrolysate and maintenance of joints had not been established [2].

The trial EFSA judged is the one the review counts among its five positive ones, so the two are not independent. Other cautions apply. The review excluded people with osteoarthritis, so it cannot tell you whether collagen helps arthritis [3]. Its authors point to differences between the trials, to details that were often not reported, such as how people were randomised, and to the fact that most of the studies were funded commercially, which makes publication bias possible [3]. And five trials is a small base.

## Does collagen help your tendons?

Maybe, when it is combined with heavy training. A systematic review of 8 randomised trials, with 257 people aged 18 to 52 (246 of them men), tested collagen alongside resistance or plyometric training for 3 to 15 weeks [4]. In three of four studies, tendon cross-sectional area grew more with collagen than with placebo [4]. The two studies that gave 30 g with vitamin C on training days found stiffer tendons with collagen, while in the two studies with about 5 g tendons got stiffer in both the collagen and the placebo group, with no difference between them [4]. The reviewers call this a comparison across trials, not proof of a dose effect [4].

Those are measurements of the tendon, not of injuries. The review authors name small samples, different protocols and short studies as limits [4]. They suggest 15 to 30 g with at least 50 mg of vitamin C, together with heavy training [4], but the trials differed in dose and in whether vitamin C was part of the supplement, so these numbers are a suggestion, not a tested protocol [4].

## Does collagen build muscle?

Not as protein. Collagen lacks the essential amino acid tryptophan [3]. In two studies that compared it with other proteins, the muscle protein synthesis response was lower with collagen than with the same amount of other protein: 30 g a day of collagen against 30 g of whey in one study, and 60 g a day against 60 g of lactalbumin in another [3]. The review notes that whey and lactalbumin have more leucine and tryptophan [3].

The training studies are similar. In the tendon review, strength rose with training in all six trials that measured it, and collagen added nothing on top [4]. For muscle size the results were split: two trials found more growth with collagen, two did not [4]. In the joint review, collagen with resistance training changed body composition and strength a bit, with smaller changes in young active people than in older men who were losing muscle [3].

## Common mistakes

- **Using collagen as your main protein for muscle.** It lacks the essential amino acid tryptophan [3].
- **Taking it without training.** In the tendon trials collagen was combined with resistance or plyometric training [4].
- **Expecting a result in days.** The joint trials ran for 3 to 6 months [3].
