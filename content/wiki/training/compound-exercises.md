## What are compound exercises?

A compound exercise moves more than one joint at a time, so several muscle groups work together. A squat bends your hips, knees and ankles and trains your thighs and glutes in one movement. Researchers call these multi-joint exercises.

An isolation exercise moves one joint and mainly trains one muscle group, like a biceps curl or a leg extension. These are single-joint exercises. See [[isolation-exercises]].

Common compound exercises:

- **Lower body** - squat, leg press, lunge, deadlift, Romanian deadlift.
- **Pushing** - bench press, push-up, overhead press, dips.
- **Pulling** - rows, pull-ups, lat pulldown.

:::takeaway
Do the lifts you most want to get stronger at **first** in a session. Strength went up more in the exercises done at the **start** of training, according to the ACSM's 2026 overview of reviews [1], which includes a 2021 meta-analysis on exercise order [2]. For muscle growth, the order made **no difference** [1][2]. A small meta-analysis found **similar growth** from compound and isolation exercises in the arms and legs [7], although the ACSM found too few data to decide which type gives more [1]. If your main goal is a stronger squat, bench press or deadlift, do those lifts early.
:::

## Should compound exercises go first?

The ACSM's 2026 position stand is an overview of 137 reviews in healthy adults, from complete beginners to experienced lifters, although in most cases the reviews looked at people with little or no training experience. It found that strength went up more in exercises done at the beginning of a training session than in exercises done at the end. For muscle growth, exercise order did not matter [1].

One of the reviews behind this, a 2021 meta-analysis of 11 studies, shows what that means in practice. Strength in compound exercises went up more when they came first. Strength in isolation exercises went up more when those came first. Muscle growth was similar with either order [2]. In short: whatever you want to get strongest at, do it early in the session. If your main goal is a bigger squat or bench press, put that lift first.

## Are compound exercises better than isolation exercises?

For strength and fitness, they had the edge in one trial. Young amateur soccer players with no strength training experience trained for 8 weeks, after 4 weeks of getting used to the exercises, with either only compound or only isolation exercises. To keep the total work equal, the compound group lifted heavier weights for 6 to 8 reps per set and the isolation group lighter weights for 12 to 18 reps. The compound group gained more on the bench press, squat and leg extension, and improved their aerobic fitness more. Body fat and lean mass changed the same in both groups. The authors point to the difference in reps as one possible explanation for the strength results, and note that the players' soccer training may have affected the outcome [5].

For muscle growth, the studies found no clear winner. In untrained young men, 10 weeks of lat pulldowns grew the elbow flexors about as much as 10 weeks of biceps curls [4]. A 2017 review found that long-term studies of the upper limbs showed no difference in size and strength between the two types, and no extra gain from adding isolation exercises to a compound program [3]. A 2023 meta-analysis of 7 studies on the muscles of the arms and legs found similar growth of the whole muscle with either type. Its authors suggest that combining both may help to grow specific parts of a muscle, but add that this has hardly been studied [7]. The ACSM, whose overview includes this meta-analysis, judged the data too limited to say which type gives more growth [1].

The practical reason to build around compound exercises is time. One squat trains your thighs and glutes in a single movement, while covering the same muscles with isolation exercises takes several. The 2017 review was written with that in mind: lack of time is a common reason people do not exercise, and for the upper limbs adding isolation work gave nothing extra [3]. Compound exercises make it easier to train every major muscle group, which the WHO advises on 2 or more days a week [6].

## Do you need isolation exercises?

You do not have to, but they have a place. The 2017 review concluded that isolation work may be useful for the lower back muscles and for correcting imbalances between muscles [3].

If a muscle you care about gets little work from your compound exercises, an isolation exercise is a simple way to add sets for it. Put them after your compound work, so your heavier lifts get the early spot, where strength gains were largest [1][2].

## How do you build a workout around compound exercises?

Pick one or two compound exercises for each part of the body, and add isolation work after them if you have time.

- **Start** with a lower body compound, such as a squat or leg press.
- **Then** a push and a pull, such as a bench press and a row.
- **Finish** with isolation exercises for the muscles you want more of, such as curls, lateral raises or calf raises.

Over the week, make sure every major muscle group gets trained [6]. How often to train each muscle is covered in [[frequency]], how many sets in [[volume]].

:::checklist
- Order | Put the lifts you most want to get stronger at first [1][2].
- Muscle | For growth, the order did not matter [1][2].
- Coverage | Train every major muscle group over the week [6].
- Extras | Add isolation work for muscles your compound exercises miss.
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Doing isolation work first when you want a stronger squat or bench.** Strength went up most in the exercises done first [2].
- **Thinking isolation exercises are useless.** In a small meta-analysis they did as well as compound exercises for muscle growth in the arms and legs [7], although the ACSM found too few data to decide [1].
- **Rushing heavy compound lifts with poor form.** These are the exercises with the most weight on the bar. Learn the movement with a lighter weight first. See [[warm-up]] and [[injury-prevention]].
