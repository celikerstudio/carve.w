## What is in a pre-workout?

A pre-workout is a powder you drink before training that mixes several ingredients. There is no fixed recipe. In an analysis of the 100 best-selling products, the average product listed 18 ingredients [2]. The most common were beta-alanine (in 87% of products), caffeine (86%), citrulline (71%), tyrosine, taurine and creatine (49%) [2].

:::takeaway
A pre-workout works mostly through its **caffeine**: products with caffeine improve both strength-type and endurance performance [1]. The average product holds about **250 mg** of caffeine [2], more than the **200 mg** single dose the European Food Safety Authority sees as safe for healthy adults [4]. A meta-analysis estimates that a standard serving should be taken at least **13 hours before bed** to protect your sleep [5].
:::

Almost half of all ingredients (44%) sat in a "proprietary blend", where the label does not say how much of each ingredient is in it [2]. That means you often cannot tell whether a product contains enough of something to work.

## Does pre-workout work?

Partly, and the caffeine does most of the work. The ISSN, a sports nutrition society, states that energy drinks and pre-workouts containing caffeine improve both short, hard efforts and endurance [1]. A review of multi-ingredient pre-workouts found they may improve muscular endurance and mood, while results for strength and power are mixed [3].

What is hard to know is which ingredient does what. Results of pre-workout studies may be driven by other ingredients in the mix, like beta-alanine or citrulline [1]. And because of the blends, studies of one product often cannot be compared to another [3].

## Which ingredients matter, and how much?

Look at the ingredients one by one. Each has its own article.

### Caffeine

The ingredient with the strongest evidence. Studies use 3 to 6 mg per kg of bodyweight, usually about 60 minutes before training [1]. For safety, keep a single dose at 200 mg or less, whatever you weigh [4]. The average pre-workout has 254 mg [2]. See [[caffeine]] for the dose, timing and limits.

The amount on the label is not always what is in the tub: the caffeine content of pre-workout products has been measured to differ 8 to 9 fold [1].

### Beta-alanine

Beta-alanine does nothing on the day you take it. It works by building up in your muscle over weeks: 4 to 6 g a day for at least 2 to 4 weeks [6]. The effect shows mostly in hard efforts of 1 to 4 minutes [6]. A meta-analysis of 40 studies found a small overall effect [7].

The average pre-workout has 2.0 g per serving, below the dose that works [2]. Beta-alanine can make your skin tingle. That is the only side effect reported, and splitting the dose into portions of about 1.6 g reduces it [6]. See [[beta-alanine]].

### Citrulline

Citrulline is sold to improve blood flow and delay fatigue. In eight studies, 6 to 8 g of citrulline malate 40 to 60 minutes before training added about 3 repetitions (6.4%) to sets taken to failure, a small effect [8]. In resistance-trained adults, it did not raise maximal strength [9]. The average pre-workout has 4.0 g of citrulline [2]. See [[citrulline]].

### Creatine

Creatine is in about half of all pre-workouts, at 2.1 g on average [2]. It works by filling your muscles over weeks, which takes 3 to 5 g a day [11]. 2 g on training days only is below that. See [[creatine]].

The ISSN also suggests it may be wise to take caffeine and creatine at different times, or to avoid high caffeine doses while you use creatine [1].

## Is pre-workout safe?

In the short studies done so far, pre-workouts look relatively safe [3]. There are still real risks.

- **Little long-term data.** Most safety studies of pre-workouts lasted less than eight weeks [3].
- **What is in the tub.** Some products contain banned substances, on purpose or as contamination [3]. If you compete in a tested sport, that can cost you.
- **Your heart.** In US emergency department data, products for weight loss or more energy caused 72% of supplement-related visits for palpitations, chest pain or a racing heart. Such visits commonly involved young adults [10]. Pre-workouts were not counted as a separate group.
- **Caffeine adds up.** A pre-workout plus two coffees can pass the 400 mg a day that EFSA considers safe for healthy adults [4].
- **Sleep.** For a standard serving of about 217 mg caffeine, a meta-analysis estimates you need at least 13.2 hours before bed to avoid losing sleep [5]. For most people that rules out an evening dose.

## Who should not take pre-workout?

- **Pregnant or breastfeeding:** EFSA's caffeine limit is 200 mg a day from all sources [4]. The average serving already holds more than that [2].
- **Under 18:** EFSA found too little information to set a safe caffeine intake for children and teenagers, and suggests 3 mg per kg a day as a basis [4]. The average serving of 254 mg [2] is more than that for anyone under about 85 kg, so for almost every teenager one scoop is already too much.
- **Heart problems, high blood pressure or on medication:** according to EFSA's website, its safe levels were not assessed for people with a medical condition or for caffeine combined with medicines [4]. Talk to your doctor first.

:::checklist
- Label | Pick a product that lists the amount of every ingredient, without a proprietary blend.
- Caffeine | Pick a product with no more than **200 mg** per serving, or take part of a scoop. Count it with your coffee and stay under **400 mg** a day.
- Timing | Use it for morning or early-day training; skip it or use a caffeine-free version in the evening.
- Doses | Compare beta-alanine and creatine with the amounts that work; the average product has too little beta-alanine.
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Expecting beta-alanine to work today.** It takes weeks of daily use [6].
- **Taking a full scoop late in the day.** By the meta-analysis estimate, a standard serving can still cut your sleep up to 13 hours later [5].
- **Trusting the blend.** Without amounts on the label, you cannot know what you are taking [2].
