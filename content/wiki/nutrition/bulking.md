## What is bulking?

Bulking is eating more than you burn for a while, on purpose, while you train to build muscle. The extra food gives your body the energy to build new tissue. The training tells it where to put that tissue.

:::takeaway
To build muscle, eat a small surplus of **about 10 to 20% above maintenance**, the low end if you are more advanced [4][5]. For novice and intermediate lifters, a gain of **0.25 to 0.5% of bodyweight a week** is a common target [4][5]. Beginners can handle a larger surplus, and the more trained you are, the higher the risk that the extra turns into fat [1]. Keep your protein up and keep adding load in the gym.
:::

## Why does a surplus help you build muscle?

Building muscle costs energy, and a calorie deficit works against it. The ISSN, a sports nutrition society, concludes that diets aimed at gaining lean mass are likely best served by a sustained calorie surplus that supports growing training demands [1]. In one study the ISSN cites, muscle protein synthesis dropped after 10 days at 80% of estimated energy needs [1].

It is not a strict requirement. Muscle has been gained in a deficit too, and the authors of one review think that is more likely among beginners and people with overweight or obesity [3]. A surplus is likely to support growth. It does not switch it on. See [[calories]] for how to work out what you burn.

## How big should the surplus be?

No long-term controlled trial has found the best size, and the authors of one review say so [3]. What exists is a small trial and some expert advice.

In an 8-week trial, 17 trained lifters were assigned to maintenance, a 5% surplus or a 15% surplus. In practice both surplus groups gained the same 3.3 kg, against 0.4 kg at maintenance, so the two surpluses ended up similar. Muscle thickness did not clearly differ between groups. The 15% group improved its bench press more. Across all lifters, more weight gain went with slightly more biceps growth, but the evidence was weak [4]. The 15% group added more skinfold thickness (a fat measure) than maintenance, and the 5% group probably did too. Across all lifters, faster weight gain went with larger skinfold increases [4]. It was small and short, so treat it as a hint.

The authors recommend conservative surpluses of 5 to 20% over maintenance, smaller for more advanced lifters [4]. A review written for natural bodybuilders gives 10 to 20% [5]. For a person who maintains on 2,800 kcal, 10% is 280 kcal and 20% is 560 kcal. Another review suggests starting with about 360 to 480 kcal a day and adjusting from there [3].

## Do beginners and trained lifters bulk differently?

Yes, in the ISSN's reading. Larger surpluses suit people new to training, who are primed for fast progress. Smaller surpluses suit advanced trainees, who run a higher risk of gaining extra fat [1].

That rests on two studies of different people. Elite athletes from various sports, who all lifted four days a week, were guided to eat about 544 kcal above their usual intake. They gained more fat than a comparison group that ate as it pleased, and the extra lean mass was not statistically significant. In a separate study, untrained people who added a supplement of about 2,000 kcal a day for 8 weeks gained 2.9 kg of lean mass and only 0.2 kg of fat [1].

The ISSN adds that not everyone fits this. Some beginners need a smaller surplus and some advanced lifters need a bigger one [1]. It also names a high level of daily non-exercise movement as a reason for a larger surplus. In a study it cites, non-obese adults who ate 1,000 kcal over maintenance for 8 weeks stored only 432 kcal of it, and most of the extra energy they burned came from that kind of movement [1]. If the scale does not move, you may be burning more than you think.

## How fast should you gain?

No trial has tested which rate is best. A narrative review for natural bodybuilders recommends about 0.25 to 0.5% of bodyweight a week for novice and intermediate lifters, and a more conservative pace for advanced ones [5]. The authors of the 8-week trial give the same range [4]. For an 80 kg person that is 0.2 to 0.4 kg a week.

That is an expert recommendation, not a measured result. Treat it as a starting point.

## How much protein do you need in a surplus?

Protein matters in a surplus. In a metabolic ward study the ISSN cites, untrained and sedentary people were overfed for 8 weeks. The group on a very low protein diet (5% of energy) lost 0.7 kg of lean mass, while the groups on 15% and 25% gained 2.9 and 4.0 kg. All three groups gained about the same amount of fat [1].

The ISSN says protein together with a hyperenergetic diet and heavy resistance training may increase muscle size and lean mass [2]. A review for natural bodybuilders recommends 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg a day [5]. See [[protein]] for how to reach it, and [[progressive-overload]] and [[hypertrophy]] for the training side. The ISSN speaks of a surplus that supports increasing training demands [1], so the food only makes sense with training that keeps getting harder.

## When should you stop bulking?

This part is advice, not a research finding. Stop when your weight has gone up faster than planned for several weeks in a row, or when the fat gain bothers you. A big surplus makes the diet afterwards longer: for bodybuilders, the review points out that an aggressive surplus adds fat that has to be lost later [5].

Then go to maintenance, or lose the fat in a [[calorie-deficit]]. If you are new to lifting, overweight or obese, [[body-recomposition]] may let you build muscle without a surplus [3].

:::checklist
- Maintenance | Estimate it, eat it for two or three weeks and check that your weekly average weight is flat.
- Surplus | Add about 10 to 20%. Beginners can go higher, advanced lifters stay at the low end.
- Rate | Novice and intermediate lifters: aim for 0.25 to 0.5% of bodyweight a week. Compare weekly averages.
- Protein | Hit your daily target, see [[protein]].
- Training | Add reps or weight over time. Lifts stuck for weeks? Change the plan.
- Adjust | No change after two or three weeks? Eat 100 to 200 kcal more. Gaining faster than planned? Eat less.
- Stop | Switch to maintenance or a deficit when your weight has gone up faster than planned for several weeks, or when the fat bothers you.
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Eating as much as possible** - A large surplus raises the risk of extra fat, especially once you are trained [1][4].
- **Skimping on protein** - In the ward study above, the group on very little protein lost lean mass in a surplus [1]. Your intake will be far above that, so keep it at your target.
