## What does caffeine do?

Caffeine is a stimulant found in coffee, tea, cola, energy drinks and many pre-workout products. In your brain it blocks the receptors for adenosine, a substance that builds up in the brain and makes you sleepy [1]. With those receptors blocked, you feel more alert and hard work feels a little easier [1].

:::takeaway
To improve a workout, studies use **3 to 6 mg per kg of bodyweight**, usually about 60 minutes before [1]. The European Food Safety Authority sees no safety concern in up to **200 mg in one go and 400 mg a day** for healthy adults [2]. Take **no more than 200 mg at once**, whatever you weigh; 2 mg per kg may already work [1]. Caffeine late in the day costs you sleep [5].
:::

That effect on the brain is the main reason caffeine helps you train [1]. An older idea was that caffeine makes you burn more fat and save your muscle sugar for later. Researchers dropped it, because caffeine also helps in short, hard efforts where muscle sugar never runs low, and because low doses help without changing fat burning at all [1].

## Does caffeine make you stronger?

A little, on the day you take it. The ISSN, a sports nutrition society, lists small to moderate benefits for muscular endurance, bar speed, strength, sprinting, jumping and throwing [1]. For strength the effect is small: 2 to 7% in three meta-analyses [1]. A review of 21 meta-analyses came to the same conclusion, with moderate-quality evidence for endurance, strength, muscular endurance and power [3].

Caffeine helps most with endurance. Across dozens of studies it improved endurance performance by 2 to 4% at 3 to 6 mg per kg [1], and endurance is where the benefits are most consistent, although how much people gain differs between individuals [1].

It works in beginners as well as in trained people [1]. Most studies were done in young men [3]. In the eight studies in women, caffeine improved muscular endurance and strength by about as much as in men, although for lower-body exercises the effect was not significant [4].

Caffeine also sharpens the mind. In most people it improves attention and alertness [1].

## How much caffeine should you take?

The ISSN gives 3 to 6 mg per kg of bodyweight. The smallest dose that works is not known, but it may be as low as 2 mg per kg [1]. Very high doses such as 9 mg per kg give many side effects and do not seem to be needed for the effect [1].

Then there is safety. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) concluded that for healthy adults, a single dose of up to 200 mg (about 3 mg per kg for a 70 kg adult) raises no safety concerns. That also holds when you take it less than two hours before hard exercise; EFSA's conclusion applies to normal conditions. Up to 400 mg a day raises no safety concerns for adults who are not pregnant [2].

The ISSN range is per kg, the EFSA limit is not. At 3 mg per kg, someone of 100 kg would take 300 mg and someone of 130 kg 390 mg, both well above 200 mg. At 6 mg per kg, 70 kg already means 420 mg in one go, more than the limit for a whole day. So keep a single dose at 200 mg or less, whatever you weigh, and only go up from a smaller dose if you need to.

How much is in what you drink, roughly, according to EFSA's website [2]:

- An espresso (60 ml): 80 mg
- A cup of filter coffee (200 ml): 90 mg
- A cup of black tea (220 ml): 50 mg
- A can of cola (355 ml): 40 mg
- A can of energy drink (250 ml): 80 mg

These are rough figures. Coffee bought at similar shops has been measured with 8 to 9 times more caffeine in one cup than in another, and pre-workout products vary just as much [1]. Count every source, including your morning coffee.

## When should you take caffeine before a workout?

Most studies give it about 60 minutes before exercise [1]. After you swallow it, caffeine peaks in your blood after 30 to 120 minutes [1]. The best timing depends on the form: caffeine chewing gum is absorbed faster than a capsule, so you can take it closer to the start [1]. In long events, a dose later on, when fatigue builds up, may help more than one at the start [1].

## How long does caffeine stay in your body?

On average, half of the caffeine is gone after 4 to 6 hours. Between adults it varies from 1.5 to as much as 10 hours [1]. Over 95% of caffeine is broken down in the liver by one enzyme, CYP1A2 [1]. Pregnancy and the contraceptive pill slow that down; heavy caffeine use and smoking speed it up [1].

A half-life of 5 hours means that a 200 mg pre-workout at 6 pm still leaves about 100 mg in your blood at 11 pm. That is why an afternoon dose can still be with you at bedtime.

## Does caffeine affect your sleep?

Yes, and more than most people think. Across 24 studies, caffeine cut total sleep by 45 minutes and made it take 9 minutes longer to fall asleep [5]. To avoid losing sleep, the authors calculated that a coffee (107 mg) should be drunk at least 8.8 hours before bed, and a standard pre-workout serving (217.5 mg) at least 13.2 hours before [5].

The dose matters. In a trial in 23 young men, 100 mg up to 4 hours before bed did not measurably change sleep. 400 mg within 12 hours of bed delayed falling asleep and changed the structure of sleep [6]. EFSA's website notes that even 100 mg may affect sleep in some adults when taken close to bedtime [2].

If you train in the evening, use a small dose or none. See [[sleep]] and [[sleep-hygiene]].

## What are the side effects of caffeine?

The most common side effects are a racing heart and palpitations, anxiety, headaches, and poor or short sleep [1]. They get worse as the dose goes up, roughly in a straight line [1]. Smaller doses still improve performance and give far fewer side effects, so a lower dose is the simplest fix [1].

At the doses used for training, caffeine does not meaningfully affect how hydrated you are [1]. See [[hydration]].

## Why does caffeine work differently for different people?

Genes and habits. Variation in the CYP1A2 gene changes how fast you break caffeine down, and variation in the ADORA2A gene possibly changes how strongly you react to it [1]. How much caffeine you normally take may also play a role [1].

The differences can be large. In one study of 10 km cycling in competitive male athletes, fast metabolisers improved by 6.8% at 4 mg per kg, while slow metabolisers got 13.7% slower [1]. That is one study. Others found no effect of this gene, or a benefit only in slow metabolisers [1]. The ADORA2A gene has also been linked to how anxious caffeine makes you and to how much it disturbs your sleep [1].

Whether daily coffee drinkers get less out of a pre-workout dose is unclear. In one study, people who rarely had caffeine lost the effect after four weeks of taking it daily. In another, the effect held for 20 days, though it got smaller. In a study of 40 cyclists, normal daily intake made no difference [1].

## Who should limit caffeine?

- **Pregnant or breastfeeding:** up to 200 mg a day from all sources [2].
- **Under 18:** EFSA found too little information to set a safe intake. It suggests the adult figure of 3 mg per kg a day as a basis [2]. For a 50 kg teenager that is 150 mg a day, and a single dose in the ISSN range (3 to 6 mg per kg) already reaches or passes it.
- **Heart problems, high blood pressure, an anxiety disorder, or on medication:** according to EFSA's website, its safe levels were not assessed for people with a medical condition or for caffeine combined with medicines [2]. Ask your doctor what is safe for you.
- **Slow metabolisers:** in the general population, people with the slow-acting CYP1A2 gene variant had a higher risk of heart attack and high blood pressure the more coffee they drank [1]. You only know your variant from a gene test.
- **Prone to anxiety:** the ISSN suggests limiting caffeine at stressful moments if you know it triggers anxiety in you [1].

:::checklist
- Dose | No more than **200 mg** in one go, whatever you weigh. 6 mg per kg is the top of the range the ISSN recommends, not a target.
- Daily total | Stay under **400 mg** a day from all sources if you are a healthy adult and not pregnant.
- Timing | About 60 minutes before training; with gum you can take it closer to the start.
- Sleep | Keep a pre-workout dose well away from bedtime; the bigger the dose, the further away.
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Adding it all up too late.** A pre-workout, two coffees and an energy drink can pass 400 mg without you noticing [2].
- **Thinking more is better.** Very high doses, such as 9 mg per kg, bring many side effects and are not needed for the effect [1].
- **Training at night on a full dose.** You trade a slightly better session for less and worse sleep [5][6].
