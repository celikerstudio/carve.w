## Why do you get hungrier when you diet?

Losing weight changes the signals that tell your brain how hungry you are. In a study of 50 people with overweight or obesity, a 10-week very-low-energy diet took off 13.5 kg on average among those who finished. Afterwards several appetite hormones had shifted towards more hunger, and the people reported being hungrier. A year later hunger and several of the hormone levels were still different from before the diet [1]. That was one group on a very strict diet, with no comparison to a normal deficit, so it cannot tell how much of this happens on a milder one.

:::takeaway
Hunger on a diet is normal, and you can lower it. Eat enough **protein**, plenty of **high-volume, fiber-rich foods**, and sleep enough. In one trial, ultra-processed food did not make people hungrier, but they ate about 500 kcal a day more of it. Meal frequency has limited effect on weight loss, and eating more often may ease hunger; that evidence comes from people who were not training [7][8]. Research on training and meal frequency together is preliminary.
:::

So hunger is not a sign that you are doing it wrong or that you lack discipline. It is part of how the body responds to losing weight. Your job is to make it as small as you can, so the deficit from [[calories]] and [[calorie-deficit]] stays livable.

## Does protein keep you full?

Yes, and it is the first thing to check. In a small study, 19 people ate a normal diet, then a diet with protein raised from 15% to 30% of calories, then could eat as much as they liked on that high-protein diet for 12 weeks. They felt fuller and ate about 441 kcal a day less without being told to, and lost 4.9 kg on average [2]. Fat in the diet also dropped from 35% to 20% of calories, so the protein is not the only change. Everyone ate each diet in turn and there was no separate control group, so treat the size of the effect with care. See [[protein]] for how much to aim for.

## Does fiber help?

Some fiber does. A systematic review of randomized trials found that viscous fibers (pectins, beta-glucans and guar gum) reduced appetite in 59% of the comparisons, against 14% for less viscous fibers. Effects on how much people ate and on bodyweight were relatively small, and the reviewers did not see a clear dose-response [3]. Fiber helps, but it is not a fix on its own. [[fiber]] lists where to find it.

## Why do big, light meals fill you up?

Foods with few calories per gram, like fruit, vegetables and soup, let you eat a large portion for fewer calories. In a one-year trial, 97 women with obesity were advised either to cut fat, or to cut fat and eat more fruit and vegetables. Both groups lost weight. The second group ate a greater weight of food and reported less hunger [4]. Among the 71 women who finished the year they lost 7.9 kg against 6.4 kg, with a different pattern of weight loss between the groups.

## Does processed food make you hungrier?

It makes you eat more, without making you feel hungrier. In the trial from [[calories]], 20 adults living in a clinic ate both diets in turn, as much as they wanted. They ate about 500 kcal a day more on ultra-processed food than on unprocessed food, and their scores for hunger, fullness and satisfaction did not differ between the diets [5]. Mostly unprocessed food is the safer bet when you are cutting.

## What does sleep do to hunger?

One night of short sleep made men eat more. In 12 healthy young men, a night of about 4 hours of sleep was followed by about 559 kcal more eaten the next day than after a night of about 8 hours. They were also hungrier before breakfast and dinner [6]. It was a small study and a single night, but it matches the larger picture in [[sleep]].

## Does eating more often reduce hunger?

A little, maybe. The ISSN position stand on meal frequency says that increasing meal frequency appears to help decrease hunger and improve appetite control. The same stand says it does not appear to improve body composition in sedentary people, and that research is limited and not all studies agree [7]. The later ISSN stand on nutrient timing puts it this way: in non-exercising scenarios, changing meal frequency has limited impact on weight loss and body composition, with stronger evidence that it can improve appetite and satiety. It adds that preliminary research suggests a possible benefit when meals are combined with an exercise program [8].

So eating more often is no weight-loss trick. If it helps you stay full, it is a tool. If you feel fine on two or three meals, there is no reason to add more. See [[meal-timing]].

## What can you do about hunger in practice?

:::checklist
- Protein | Put a solid protein source in every meal, and check your daily total in [[protein]].
- Volume | Fill half the plate with vegetables or fruit, and start a meal with a soup or salad.
- Fiber | Add viscous fiber such as oats, beans or lentils to the foods you already eat, a bit at a time.
- Whole foods | Swap packaged snacks for foods you have to prepare or peel.
- Sleep | Protect your sleep during a cut. A short night can leave you hungrier the next day.
- Pattern | Try 3 to 4 meals if you are hungry between two big ones. Keep what works for you.
:::

Hungry in spite of all this? Try a smaller deficit for a while. See [[calorie-deficit]].
