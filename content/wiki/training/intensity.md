## What is intensity?
Intensity describes how heavy a set is relative to your maximum, often measured by percent of one-rep max or RPE. It governs motor unit recruitment and the stimulus each rep provides. Both strength and muscle gain require sufficient intensity paired with appropriate [[volume]].

:::takeaway
Use **60-80% 1RM** (about **6-15** reps) at **RPE 7-9** for hypertrophy; add **85-92%** work sparingly for strength.
:::

## How does it work?
Heavier loads create high mechanical tension and recruit fast-twitch fibers. Moderate loads taken near failure can match heavy loads for [[hypertrophy]] because effort raises fiber recruitment. Intensity and volume trade off: higher intensity usually requires fewer sets to stay recoverable. RPE and reps in reserve keep training in an effective effort band while controlling fatigue.
Higher intensity work also sharpens bracing and setup skills that transfer to later heavy singles or testing.

## Practical application
For muscle gain, center most work at 60-80% 1RM with 1-3 reps in reserve. For strength phases, include small amounts of 85-92% 1RM work with low set counts and longer rests. Progress by adding reps before load or by making small load jumps while keeping RPE targets steady. Estimate 1RM from recent top sets instead of frequent maxing. Match higher-intensity days with modest set volumes and full warm-ups to protect joints and technique.

:::checklist
- Hypertrophy zone | Train mostly at **60-80% 1RM**, RPE 7-9, for quality volume.
- Strength touches | Add limited **85-92%** top sets with low volume and longer rests.
- Progression guardrails | Add reps before load, and keep RPE drift in check to avoid form breakdown.
:::

## Common mistakes
- Staying too light without reaching sufficient effort, leading to under-stimulus despite many sets.
- Pushing every set to failure, crushing recovery and degrading technique for [[progressive-overload]].
- Ignoring RPE drift from fatigue, letting form break and injury risk rise.
