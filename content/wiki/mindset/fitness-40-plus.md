## Is there a guideline for fitness after 40?

:::takeaway
Neither the WHO nor the Dutch Health Council draws a line at 40. The adult advice is strength activities on **2 or more days a week** [1][2]. For older adults (65 and over in the WHO guideline) both add balance work, and the WHO asks for varied activity with an emphasis on balance and strength on **3 or more days a week** [1].
:::

The World Health Organization starts its advice for older adults at 65 [1]. The Dutch Health Council has one guideline for adults and older adults, adds balance exercises for older adults, and names no age [2]. Nothing in either changes at 40 or 50. If you are in your forties or fifties, the adult advice applies to you: the WHO asks adults for strength activities on 2 or more days a week [1], and the Health Council asks for muscle- and bone-strengthening exercises at least twice a week [2]. See [[physical-activity]] for the full weekly targets.

The Health Council chose one shared guideline for adults and older adults, because much of the research on adults also includes older people [2]. The ACSM position stand on resistance training says its recommendations are evidence-based across all ages [4]. The reviews behind it mixed younger and older adults and were not split by age [4]. It covers healthy adults, and the ACSM notes that separate guidelines for older adults could follow [4]. The NSCA has already written one [3].

## What does getting older do to muscle and strength?

The NSCA position statement says that aging, even without chronic disease, goes with biological changes that can lower muscle mass, strength and function [3]. Those losses reduce your resilience and make you more vulnerable to serious setbacks [3]. The WHO's advice for older adults aims at better functioning and fewer falls [1].

The same statement calls resistance training a powerful way to counter the loss of muscle strength and muscle mass, and the effects on daily functioning, mobility and independence that come with it [3].

## What do the WHO and the Health Council advise from 65?

The WHO advises older adults (65 and over) to do what adults do, plus varied multicomponent activity that emphasises balance and strength at moderate or higher intensity on 3 or more days a week. The aim is better functioning and fewer falls [1]. The Health Council advises everyone muscle- and bone-strengthening exercises at least twice a week, such as climbing stairs, getting up from a chair repeatedly and strength training. For older adults it adds balance exercises [2].

The Health Council concludes that muscle- and bone-strengthening exercises are proven to help, and that adding balance exercises for older adults is proven to help [2].

## Is strength training safe when you are older?

For healthy adults, yes. The ACSM states that resistance training is safe for healthy adults of all ages [4]. It also notes that many adults, older adults in particular, avoid it because of misperceptions about safety and injury risk [4]. In an analysis of more than 38,000 participants, of whom more than 11,000 were older adults, exercise did not increase the risk of serious adverse events [4]. Non-serious problems such as pain, fatigue, bursitis and edema do occur; the ACSM reports that their rate with resistance training was not different from aerobic exercise [4]. If you live with a chronic condition, the WHO says you may want to ask a healthcare professional or physical activity specialist which types and amounts of activity suit you [1].

That analysis covers exercise in general. Among the participants, over 6,700 did resistance training [4]. The ACSM limits its safety statement to healthy adults [4]. The WHO says that anyone who gets new symptoms while building up should see a healthcare provider [1].

The ACSM still asks for high effort at least twice a week, and says that reaching near-failure, or about 2 to 3 reps left in reserve, is enough effort [4]. It adds that lifting all the way to fatigue may be inadvisable for certain groups, older individuals among them, because of risks to vascular health and a higher risk of injury from poor form [4].

## How should you train after 40?

Start from the adult advice: strength training on 2 or more days a week, working all the large muscle groups [1][2]. For older adults, add balance exercises, as the WHO and the Health Council advise [1][2]. For food and rest, see [[protein]] and [[sleep]].

If you are new to lifting, begin with light loads and build up. The ACSM notes that in some groups, because of inexperience and potential safety concerns, loads are necessarily low, and progression is then needed to get a real benefit [4].
