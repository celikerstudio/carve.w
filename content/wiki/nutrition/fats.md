## What is fat in your food?

Fat is one of the three macronutrients. It gives you energy, the vitamins A, D and E, and fatty acids you need for your eyes, brain and muscles [2]. It is also the most energy-dense: 9 kcal per gram, against 4 for carbohydrate and protein and 7 for alcohol [2]. That is the figure [[calories]] uses too.

:::takeaway
In the Netherlands, fat should make up **20 to 40% of your energy** [1][2]. The lower end is a minimum for essential fatty acids [2], and for athletes the advice is not to stay under 20% for long [6]. How good the whole diet is matters more than the total amount of fat [1]. Keep saturated fat low (WHO: no more than **10%** of energy) and trans fat lower still (WHO: **1%**) [3][4].
:::

## How much fat should you eat?

Three authorities give a figure, and they are close but not the same:

- The Dutch Health Council (Gezondheidsraad, July 2026, a revision of earlier advice): 20 to 40% of energy from age 4 [1]. The Voedingscentrum, the Dutch nutrition centre, uses the same range [2].
- EFSA, the European food authority: 20 to 35% of energy for adults [5].
- WHO: adults should limit total fat to 30% of energy or less [3].

"% of energy" means the share of your daily calories. At 9 kcal per gram, someone eating 2,500 kcal a day gets 500 kcal (about 56 g) of fat at 20%, and 1,000 kcal (about 111 g) at 40%.

The Voedingscentrum ties the upper end to body weight: a diet with a lot of fat can promote overweight [2]. WHO's guideline on total fat is titled "for the prevention of unhealthy weight gain", and its news item says the new guidelines aim to reduce the risk of unhealthy weight gain and diet-related diseases [3]. The EFSA overview gives no reason for 35% [5]. Read the upper figures as edges of a range, not as amounts to aim for.

## Why not go below 20%?

Because you then risk missing things you need. The Voedingscentrum estimates the minimum need for fat at 20% of energy from age 4, to get enough essential fatty acids (linoleic acid and alpha-linolenic acid). At that amount you also get vitamins E, A and D [2].

The ACSM and its partner organisations say the same for athletes: do not stay under 20% of energy for long [6]. Their reason is that cutting fat often shrinks the variety of what you eat, which is likely to lower your intake of fat-soluble vitamins and essential fatty acids, especially omega-3 [6]. For most athletes, fat intakes that fit their goals run between 20 and 35% of energy [6]. For how much omega-3 the authorities advise, see [[omega-3]].

For fat loss, the ISSN (a sports nutrition society) mentions 0.5 to 1 g of fat per kg bodyweight a day, which in some situations is as little as 20% of calories. It bases that on weight-loss studies in non-athletes, where the people who lost weight and kept it off ate less fat, and adds that this is not always the case [7]. At the low end of 0.5 g per kg this can fall below 20% of your calories (for example 70 kg at 0.5 g is 35 g, about 315 kcal, which is 14% of 2,200 kcal). The 20% floor above still applies if you keep this up for long, so check the percentage, not just the grams.

## How much fat do athletes eat?

The ISSN says fat advice for athletes is similar to or slightly higher than for everyone else [7]. It calls about 30% of daily calories a moderate amount, and says up to 50% can be eaten safely during regular high-volume training [7]. That is above the Dutch, EFSA and WHO figures, so read it as a statement about athletes with a high training volume, not as a goal. The same document says that most of the evidence on high-fat diets shows no performance benefit, and that the evidence on ketogenic diets is limited and mixed [7]. See [[carbohydrates]] for that.

## Does more fat raise testosterone?

Cutting fat lowers it a little, in men. The ISSN says higher-fat diets appear to maintain circulating testosterone better than low-fat diets [7]. A review of nutrition for bodybuilders in the off-season puts a number on it: in studies in men, cutting fat in diets with the same calories from about 30-40% to about 15-25% of energy gave significant but modest drops in testosterone. The same review says it is not clear that testosterone changes within the normal range affect muscle gain much [8]. So this is a reason not to run fat very low, not a way to gain more muscle.

## Does eating fat make you fat?

Not automatically. The Voedingscentrum says that with overweight it comes down to how much energy your food gives you and whether that balances with the energy you use [2]. It adds that a lot of fat easily leads to a diet that gives more energy than you use [2]. Fat is the most energy-dense of the nutrients listed above, so it adds up fast; see [[calories]]. To lose body fat you need a deficit whichever nutrient you cut; see [[calorie-deficit]].

## Which fats are better?

The Gezondheidsraad says the quality of the whole diet matters more for health than the total amount of fat [1]. WHO also says both quantity and quality count, and that fat should be mainly unsaturated [3].

- **Unsaturated fat** (oil, nuts) lowers LDL cholesterol. **Saturated fat** (butter, fatty meat) raises it, which increases the risk of heart and vascular disease. The Voedingscentrum advises swapping products rich in saturated fat for products rich in unsaturated fat [2].
- **Saturated fat:** WHO strongly recommends getting down to 10% of energy, and conditionally suggests going lower [4]. The Gezondheidsraad advises as low as possible without naming a limit, and calls 10% an intermediate step for nutrition education [1]. The Voedingscentrum page words it as "as low as possible, in any case under 10%" [2]. So 10% is an edge, not a goal. WHO recommends replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat (a strong recommendation), and suggests plant-based monounsaturated fat or carbohydrate from whole grains, vegetables, fruit and pulses (conditional) [4].
- **Trans fat:** it raises LDL about as much as saturated fat, with a higher risk of heart and vascular disease [2]. WHO recommends getting down to 1% of energy and suggests going lower [4]. The Gezondheidsraad and EFSA say as low as possible [1][5]. The 1% is a ceiling.

:::checklist
- Range | **20 to 40%** of energy from fat in the Netherlands [1]. EFSA says 20 to 35%, WHO 30% or less [5][3].
- Floor | Do not stay under **20%** for long, especially as an athlete [2][6].
- Quality | Swap butter and fatty meat for oil and nuts [2]. Keep saturated fat as low as you can, trans fat even lower [1][4].
- Calories | Fat is 9 kcal per gram. Weight depends on energy in against energy out [2].
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Cutting fat very low for months.** Under 20% you risk too few essential fatty acids and fat-soluble vitamins [2][6].
- **Reading the upper end of a range as a target.** 40%, 35%, 30% and 10% saturated fat are edges, not goals [1][3][5].
- **Blaming fat itself for weight gain.** The Voedingscentrum looks at total energy against what you use [2].
- **Taking the testosterone finding as a muscle tip.** The ISSN only says higher-fat diets seem to keep testosterone up better than low-fat ones, and it is unclear that this matters for muscle [7][8].
