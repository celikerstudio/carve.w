## What makes a protein source good for muscle?

Protein is made of amino acids. Nine of them are essential: your body cannot make them, so they have to come from food [1]. These essential amino acids are what switch on muscle building, so a protein with more of them is a better protein for muscle. The ISSN, a sports nutrition society, puts it this way: sources with more essential amino acids are higher quality protein [1].

:::takeaway
Meat, fish, eggs and dairy have the most essential amino acids [1], and soy builds muscle about as well as milk protein [3]. Aim for portions with about **1 to 3 g of leucine**, the amino acid that switches on muscle building [1]. A portion of **20 to 40 g of protein** from a good source gets you there [5]. Most other plant proteins have less, so you need a bit more of them, or a mix.
:::

## Why does leucine matter?

Of the essential amino acids, leucine is the one that acts most like a switch. The ISSN says a meal needs about 1 to 3 g of leucine to start up the machinery that builds muscle protein [1]. Leucine on its own is not enough, though: building muscle also takes the other essential amino acids, so the ISSN advises getting your leucine from good protein sources rather than as a separate supplement [1].

The ISSN also gives a portion target: 20 to 40 g of a high-quality protein, containing about 10 to 12 g of essential amino acids [5]. In practice, if a meal holds 20 to 40 g of protein from a good source, the leucine takes care of itself.

## Which foods have the best protein?

Animal and dairy proteins generally contain the highest share of essential amino acids [1]. Leucine makes up about 9 to 11% of dairy protein, 8.6% of egg protein and 8% of meat protein. Whey is at 11% and casein at 9.3%. Plant proteins usually have less [1]. So 25 g of protein from meat gives about 2 g of leucine, and 25 g from dairy a little more.

Two examples from the ISSN:

- One large egg has 6 g of protein. Three eggs give you 18 g [1].
- A 113 g serving of lean beef gives 30 g of amino acids, of which 10 g are essential and 3.5 g are leucine [1].

Fish, chicken, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, quark, tofu and tempeh all work as the protein part of a meal. For the grams in a specific product, read the label; see [[reading-food-labels]].

## Can you build muscle on plant protein?

Yes, if you eat enough of it and pick your sources well. Plant proteins differ a lot from each other. In an analysis of protein isolates, oat, lupin and wheat protein were 21 to 22% essential amino acids, against 43% for whey, 39% for milk and 32% for egg. Leucine ranged from 5.1% in hemp to 13.5% in corn. The authors note that blends of plant proteins can come close to animal protein [2].

Studies on actual muscle growth show a small difference. A meta-analysis of 30 trials, comparing equal amounts of protein, found that plant protein gave slightly less muscle mass than animal protein, mainly in people under 60. Soy and milk protein did not differ. The gap came from other plant proteins, such as rice, oat and potato, and from plant-based diets. It showed up in trials where people also did strength training; without training there was no difference. Strength did not differ [3].

With soy, the amount seems to matter more than the source. In a 12-week study, young men who had not trained before and ate 1.6 g of protein per kg a day gained the same muscle and strength whether they were vegan with soy protein or ate a mixed diet with whey [4]. The ISSN describes a study in older men where 35 g of wheat protein built less muscle protein than the same amount of casein, while 60 g of wheat protein did raise it [1].

If you eat plant-based: rely on soy (tofu, tempeh, soy milk) for part of your protein, combine legumes with grains over the day, and aim for slightly bigger portions than you would with meat or dairy.

## Is food better than a protein shake?

The ISSN advises focusing on whole foods that contain all the essential amino acids [1]. Food also brings vitamins and minerals; the ISSN points out that meat supplies iron, selenium and vitamin B12, for example [1]. A shake is a practical way to reach your protein when meals fall short, without many extra calories [1]. See [[whey-protein]] and [[casein]], and [[protein]] for how much you need per day.

## Your checklist

:::checklist
- Every meal | Build it around a protein source: meat, fish, eggs, dairy, soy or legumes.
- Portion | 20 to 40 g of protein per meal covers the 1 to 3 g of leucine.
- Plant-based | Use soy, mix legumes and grains, and eat a bit more protein per meal.
- Shakes | Use them to close a gap, not to replace meals.
:::
