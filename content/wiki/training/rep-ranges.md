## What is a rep range?

A rep range is the number of repetitions you aim for in each set, such as 8 to 12. It goes hand in hand with the weight. A weight you can lift at most 10 times is called your 10-rep maximum, or 10RM. The heavier the weight, the fewer reps you can do.

So when a program says "3 sets of 8 to 12", it means a weight you can lift at least 8 times and no more than about 12. If you can do 20, the weight is too light for that range.

:::takeaway
For muscle growth, a wide range of weights works: the 2026 ACSM position stand found similar growth from **30% to 100% of your one-rep max** [1]. For maximum strength, heavier loads do better [2][3][4], and the ACSM names **80% of your one-rep max or more** [1]. Sets **to failure are not needed**, but the ACSM asks for hard sets, for example near-failure or 2 to 3 reps left [1]. The 2009 position stand advised beginners to use a weight they can lift **8 to 12 times** [7].
:::

## How many reps should a beginner do?

The 2026 ACSM position stand does not recommend a rep range of its own. It notes that reps and load are linked: with a given load and a near-fatiguing effort, the heavier the weight, the fewer reps you get [1]. It advises at least two sets per exercise [1]. The recommendations apply to healthy adults of 18 and older, from complete beginners to experienced lifters, although much of the evidence comes from people with little training experience [1].

The 2009 position stand advised people new to training to use loads they can lift for 8 to 12 reps [7]. In 2026 the ACSM says the program details of earlier position stands may be an appropriate starting point for some people, and that several other programs work as well [1]. So 8 to 12 reps with a weight that challenges you can be a place to start. It is not the only one.

The 2009 stand paired it with a rule for moving up: add 2 to 10% to the weight once you can do one or two reps more than you aimed for, on two sessions in a row [7]. The 2026 stand does not repeat that rule. See [[progressive-overload]].

## Which rep range builds the most muscle?

Several reviews found similar muscle growth across a wide range of loads.

- The 2026 ACSM overview of 137 reviews found that muscle growth did not differ between low loads (30% of your one-rep max) and high loads (100%) [1].
- A 2017 meta-analysis of 21 studies compared lighter loads (60% of your one-rep max or less) with heavier ones. With every set taken to failure, muscle growth was similar [2].
- A 2021 network meta-analysis of 28 studies split loads into low (more than 15 reps per set), moderate (9 to 15) and high (8 or fewer). Again with every set to failure, it found no difference in growth [3].
- A 2023 network meta-analysis of 119 studies on muscle size found that all the prescriptions it compared built muscle to a similar degree [4].

The ACSM found that completing sets to failure does not add gains in strength, muscle size or power [1]. How close to failure you end can still matter: a 2024 exploratory meta-regression, which estimated the reps left in each study, found more muscle growth when sets ended closer to failure [5]. The ACSM asks for high effort whatever the load, and says this can be reached by ending sets near failure or with 2 to 3 reps left. It adds that there is not enough evidence for an exact target [1]. With a light weight, high effort means the last reps are hard. See [[hypertrophy]].

## Are high reps less effective?

They work for growth, with two practical downsides. A set of 25 takes longer than a set of 8, and judging how close you are to failure is harder. In a meta-analysis of studies where people predicted how many reps they had left, predictions were less accurate in sets that went beyond 12 reps [6].

So high reps are a fine option for exercises where heavy weights feel awkward or hurt your joints. Just count honestly and make the set hard.

## Which rep range builds the most strength?

Heavier weights. In the 2026 ACSM position stand, strength was enhanced by lifting heavier loads, 80% of one-rep max or more, with a dose-response. The same overview names a full range of motion, 2 to 3 sets, exercises early in the session and at least two sessions a week [1].

These three reviews are among those the ACSM overview drew on. The 2017 meta-analysis found bigger gains in one-rep max strength with heavier loads [2]. The 2021 analysis found bigger strength gains with high and moderate loads than with low loads, with a non-significant edge for high over moderate [3]. In the 2023 network meta-analysis, prescriptions with loads above 80% of one-rep max gave the most strength [4].

## What are high reps for?

The ACSM states the principle of specificity: training adaptations are specific to the training stimulus you apply [1]. That is why you choose weight and reps for your goal. Heavy sets build the most strength, and a wide range of loads builds muscle [1].

Muscular endurance, being able to keep going for many reps, is improved by resistance training. There was too little data in the reviews to say whether the load, the number of sets or other choices change how much endurance you gain [1]. The 2009 position stand advised light to moderate weights (40 to 60% of your one-rep max) for more than 15 reps, with rests under 90 seconds [7]. The 2026 stand does not repeat that advice. If your sport or job asks you to keep going for many repetitions, you can include sets with many reps. The ACSM advises keeping specificity in mind when you plan training for a goal, but the reviews had too little data to show that lighter weights with more reps build endurance better [1].

:::checklist
- Start | Pick a weight you can lift 8 to 12 times with good form. That is the 2009 starting point, and other programs work too [1][7].
- Effort | Make sets hard. You do not have to reach failure; ending near failure or with 2 to 3 reps left counts as high effort [1].
- Strength | Want a bigger one-rep max? Include heavy sets, 80% of one-rep max or more [1].
- Goal | Choose weight and reps to fit what you want to get better at [1].
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Stopping a light set while it still feels easy.** Light weights build muscle when the effort is high [1][2][3]. Twelve reps with a weight you could lift 30 times leave 18 in the tank.
- **Believing only 8 to 12 builds muscle.** Growth was similar from 30% to 100% of one-rep max [1], and across a wide range of loads when sets went to failure [2][3].
- **Only training light if you want to get strong.** Heavier loads gave bigger strength gains [1][2][3][4].
- **Treating a rep range as fixed.** Once you pass the top of the range, add weight and work back up from the bottom. See [[progressive-overload]].
