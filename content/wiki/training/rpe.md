## What is RPE?
RPE (Rate of Perceived Exertion) scores how hard a set feels on a 1-10 scale, often tied to reps in reserve (RIR). The ACSM says effort can be measured with various scales [1].

:::takeaway
The ACSM advises **high effort** in your sets, for example **near-failure** or about **2 to 3 reps in reserve** [1]. There is **not enough evidence** for an exact RPE or RIR target [1], and training to failure is not required [1].
:::

## How does it work?
RPE links perceived difficulty to proximity to failure. The ACSM says high effort can be reached by ending sets near failure or with 2 to 3 reps in reserve. It adds that there is insufficient evidence to quantify exact RIR and perceived exertion targets [1]. So treat a number such as 2 to 3 RIR as a guide for a hard set, not an exact target. This advice is for healthy adults of 18 and older, and much of the evidence comes from people with little training experience [1]. See [[intensity]] and [[volume]].

:::check
It autoregulates load and effort based on daily readiness, improving training quality and safety.
By holding RPE targets steady, you adjust load or reps to account for fatigue, sleep, or stress. This preserves effective [[volume]] and [[intensity]] without overreaching. Over time, pairing RPE with logged loads refines accuracy.
Autoregulation keeps day-to-day variability from derailing progression: a tough day means lighter load for the same RPE, while a good day lets you nudge load up without adding extra sets. This keeps long blocks productive without constant program rewrites.
:::

## Practical application
Rate how hard each set felt, and make most sets hard: only a few reps left, or close to failure. No exact RPE is known to be best for any exercise [1].

:::check
Assign RPE targets per exercise: compounds at 7-9, accessories at 7-8. End sets when reps in reserve match the plan. If RPE is higher than prescribed, reduce load or reps; if lower, add small load or a rep. Track top sets plus back-offs for trend lines. Combine RPE with objective markers (bar speed, video) to calibrate.
Check RPE after you rack the bar, not mid-set, and note whether breakdown came from technique or pure effort. If RPE ratings drift upward week to week, consider a deload or slight volume cut instead of forcing prescribed loads.
:::

:::checklist
- Effort | Make sets hard, for example near-failure or about 2 to 3 reps in reserve [1].
- Target | Use a number as a guide. There is not enough evidence for an exact RPE or RIR target [1].
- Failure | Training to failure is not required [1].
:::

## Common mistakes
- Pushing every set to failure. The ACSM found no extra gain in strength, muscle size or power from it, and suggests it may be unwise for some groups, such as older adults, because of strain on blood vessels and injuries from poor form [1].
- Holding on to one exact RPE or RIR number as the right target. The evidence is too thin to say which number is best [1].

:::check
Treating RPE as optional and guessing wildly, leading to under- or overshooting effort.
Pushing every set to failure, eroding technique and recovery.
Ignoring fatigue signs (poor [[sleep]], stress) and holding loads fixed instead of adjusting.
:::
