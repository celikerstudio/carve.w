## What is whey protein?

Whey is one of the two proteins in milk; the other is [[casein]]. When acid is added to milk, the casein clumps together and the whey is left on top [1]. Whey dissolves easily in water and your body digests it fast [1].

:::takeaway
You do not need whey. You need enough protein, about **1.6 g per kg a day** if you lift and are under 65, and whey is an easy way to get there [2][3]. A scoop of **20 to 40 g** is a normal serving [1]. On top of training, protein supplements added on average **0.3 kg** of extra fat-free mass in trials of 6 weeks or more [2].
:::

Whey is also rich in leucine, one of the amino acids your body uses as a signal to build muscle. Whey has more of it than any other common protein source, at about 11% [1]. The ISSN, a sports nutrition society, advises 700 to 3000 mg of leucine per protein serving [1]. A 25 g serving of whey protein contains about 2.75 g.

## Do you need whey protein?

No. The ISSN states that people who train can get all the protein they need from whole food. A supplement is a practical way to make sure you get enough, with few extra calories, especially if you train a lot [1].

What counts is your total for the day. See [[protein]] for how much you need. A shake helps when meals fall short: a busy day, a small appetite, or a high target during a cut.

## Does whey protein help you build muscle?

It helps a bit, if your protein intake was too low without it. In a meta-analysis of 49 trials of 6 weeks or more, protein supplements during strength training added on average 0.30 kg of fat-free mass and 2.49 kg to the one-rep max, compared with training without them [2]. Above a total intake of about 1.6 g per kg a day, extra protein added no further fat-free mass [2].

A newer meta-analysis of 74 trials by the same research group found the same picture: eating more protein gives small extra gains in lean mass when you lift. In adults under 65, the effect showed up at 1.6 g per kg a day or more [3].

So if you already eat enough protein, a shake adds little. If you eat 0.8 g per kg, a shake can make a real difference to your total.

## Is whey better than other protein?

In the short term, it builds a bit more. In a small study in young men (6 per group, using hydrolysed whey), whey raised leucine in the blood faster and stimulated more muscle protein building in the next hours than the same amount of casein or soy, both at rest and after training [4]. That was measured over a few hours, not as muscle growth over months.

For long-term results, your daily total matters more than the type. Any complete protein works: meat, fish, eggs, dairy and soy all count. See [[protein-sources]].

## Concentrate, isolate or hydrolysate?

They differ in how much the whey is filtered.

- **Concentrate:** sports products are usually 70 to 80% protein [1].
- **Isolate:** filtered further, to over 90% protein [1]. Filtering separates the protein from the lactose and fat in milk [1], so an isolate has more protein per gram of powder.
- **Hydrolysate:** the protein is partly broken down before you drink it. Some studies found a faster rise of amino acids in the blood; one comparison of whey found no difference [1].

:::check
An isolate is often easier on the stomach if you are lactose intolerant, because filtering leaves less lactose. The ISSN describes the filtering, not how much lactose stays in a typical isolate or what lactose-intolerant users tolerate. Needs a source with lactose content or a tolerance study.
:::

## How much whey should you take, and when?

A normal serving is 20 to 40 g of protein, or about 0.25 g per kg of bodyweight [1]. For 80 kg that is 20 g. Take it when it fits your day: after training, with breakfast, or as a snack between meals.

The ISSN puts total protein and calories first, and advises spreading protein evenly over the day, every 3 to 4 hours [1]. A shake is an easy way to fill one of those moments. See [[meal-timing]].

:::checklist
- Daily total | Aim for about **1.6 g per kg a day** in total from food and shakes.
- Serving | **20 to 40 g** of protein per shake.
- Role | Use it to fill a gap, not as a replacement for meals.
- Type | Concentrate costs less; isolate gives more protein per scoop.
:::

## Is whey protein safe?

For healthy people, yes. The ISSN found no controlled evidence that higher protein intakes harm healthy people who exercise [1]. In a meta-analysis of trials in healthy adults without kidney disease, high-protein diets did not change how well the kidneys filter blood [5].

If you have kidney disease, the picture is different. Talk to your doctor before you raise your protein, from shakes or from food. Whey comes from milk, so it is not suitable if you have a milk allergy.

## Common mistakes

- **Shakes instead of meals.** A shake gives you protein. A meal also gives you fibre and other nutrients a shake lacks. See [[hunger-and-satiety]] for what keeps you full.
- **Counting the shake but not the day.** Two shakes on top of a day with too little protein still leave you short.
- **Buying for the timing.** The total for the day matters more than the minutes after your workout [1].
