## What is overtraining?

Overtraining is what happens when training stress, and sometimes stress from the rest of your life, piles up faster than you recover. Sport science uses three labels for it: functional overreaching, nonfunctional overreaching, and overtraining syndrome. The reference here is a joint consensus statement of the European College of Sport Science and the American College of Sports Medicine. It is written about athletes. For lifters, see the section on lifting below [1][2].

:::takeaway
Training needs overload, but not **excessive overload plus too little recovery** [1]. A short dip in performance after hard training can lead to better performance after recovery (functional overreaching). If the balance between training and recovery stays disrupted, nonfunctional overreaching can occur, and then it takes **weeks or months** to recover [1][2]. Telling that apart from overtraining syndrome is very difficult [1].
:::

## What do you need for training to work?

Successful training must involve overload, so you have to push beyond what you are used to. It must also avoid the combination of excessive overload and inadequate recovery [1]. The problem is the combination, not hard training by itself.

## What is functional overreaching?

Athletes can have a short-term performance drop without severe psychological or other lasting negative symptoms. This is functional overreaching. The consensus says it will eventually lead to an improvement in performance after recovery [1]. The full text adds a caveat: the evidence for such a supercompensation effect after deliberate periods of intensified training is not abundant [2]. So treat it as something that can happen, not as a reason to train yourself flat on purpose.

The consensus notes that recovery from a short-term state of overreaching is possible within about two weeks [2]. For planning easier weeks, see [[deload]].

## What is nonfunctional overreaching?

When athletes do not sufficiently respect the balance between training and recovery, nonfunctional overreaching (NFOR) can occur [1]. At that stage the performance drop comes with psychological and hormonal disturbances, and the athletes will need weeks or months to recover [2]. An easier week is therefore not enough once you are there. For overtraining syndrome, the consensus says athletes may take months or possibly years to recover completely [2].

## What is overtraining syndrome?

Overtraining syndrome (OTS) is the third label. The consensus says it is generally thought that its symptoms (fatigue, performance decline, mood disturbances) are more severe than those of NFOR, and adds that there is no scientific evidence to confirm or refute this [1]. A keyword in recognising it might be "prolonged maladaptation": not only the athlete, but also several biological, neurochemical and hormonal regulation mechanisms adapt badly for a long time [1].

The full text calls the word "syndrome" a reminder that the causes are several and that training is not necessarily the only one. It also says the idea of a steady slide from one label to the next may be an oversimplification [2]. Do not read the three labels as a staircase every tired lifter is climbing.

## Why is overtraining so hard to diagnose?

The distinction between NFOR and OTS is very difficult. It depends on the clinical outcome, which means how things turn out: how long performance stays down despite rest. It also depends on excluding other causes. The athlete will often show the same signs and symptoms in both cases [1]. The consensus adds that OTS can often only be diagnosed in hindsight, when the time course can be overseen [2].

There is no reliable test either. Several markers are used, including hormones, performance tests, psychological tests and biochemical and immune markers, but none of them meets all the criteria for general acceptance [1].

## What should be ruled out first?

One approach to understanding OTS is to exclude other causes first: organic diseases or infections, dietary caloric restriction (a negative energy balance), too little carbohydrate or protein, iron deficiency, magnesium deficiency, allergies and others [1]. Dietary caloric restriction includes eating less on purpose. If you are dieting, a calorie deficit is on that list. See [[calorie-deficit]] and [[cutting]].

The definitive diagnosis also requires excluding an organic disease, such as a thyroid, adrenal or diabetes problem, iron deficiency with anaemia, or an infection [2]. That is work for a doctor.

The full text names more. One of the most certain triggers is a training error that leaves load and recovery out of balance. It also lists illness, psychosocial stressors (work, family) and sleep disorders as confounding factors that may be present [2]. Stress from outside the gym counts: the consensus says that exercise is not necessarily the only cause of the syndrome [2]. See [[stress-management]], [[sleep]], [[calories]], [[carbohydrates]] and [[protein]].

## What about lifting?

The consensus is about athletes, and most of the research it describes is on endurance activities [2]. For strength training, a scoping review of 47 articles found that short periods of high-volume or high-intensity training can produce functional overreaching, and that chronic high volume or intensity can lead to nonfunctional overreaching. It found minimal evidence that true overtraining syndrome has occurred in strength sports or resistance training [3].

The consensus text also says that when excessive volumes of maximal loads are used, maximal strength is one of the last performance measures to suffer, while speed and power are among the first [2]. So your one-rep max is not the first thing to show it.

## What should you do if you feel run down?

The summary of the consensus has no numbers for when to rest. The full text gives a few: it is generally recommended that athletes take at least one passive rest day each week, and it says to suspend or reduce training during an infection [2]. The rest is advice, not a finding:

- Do not train hard while you are ill. Take the time to recover after illness [2].
- Look at the basics next to your training: enough food, especially if you are dieting, and enough [[sleep]].
- Look at your training and your life: a recent jump in [[volume]] or [[intensity]], a stressful period at work.
- Expect that an easier week may not be enough if you have been flat for a long time [2].
- If you feel worn out and your performance stays down for weeks despite rest, see a doctor. A doctor can look for other causes.

:::checklist
- Balance | Hard training is fine. Hard training with too little recovery is the problem [1].
- Rule out | A calorie deficit, too little carbohydrate or protein, iron or magnesium deficiency, infection, stress outside training [1][2].
- Training too | Check [[sleep]] and [[calories]] as well as your training [2].
- Ill | Do not train hard while you have an infection [2].
- Doctor | Fatigue and a performance drop for weeks despite rest: get it checked.
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Calling every bad week overtraining.** Most bad weeks are just bad weeks. A dip after a hard block of training that then recovers is what the consensus calls functional overreaching [1][2].
- **Adding more training to a long dip.** The problem is the balance between training and recovery [1].
- **Training through an infection.** The consensus says to suspend training or reduce the intensity [2].
- **Skipping the other causes.** A calorie deficit, iron or magnesium deficiency and infections should be ruled out [1].
