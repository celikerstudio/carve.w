## What is a rep range?

A rep range is the number of repetitions you aim for in each set, such as 8 to 12. It goes hand in hand with the weight. A weight you can lift at most 10 times is called your 10-rep maximum, or 10RM. The heavier the weight, the fewer reps you can do.

So when a program says "3 sets of 8 to 12", it means a weight you can lift at least 8 times and no more than about 12. If you can do 20, the weight is too light for that range.

:::takeaway
As a beginner, use a weight you can lift **8 to 12 times** [1]. For muscle growth, light and heavy weights gave similar results when sets were taken **to failure** [2][3], and growth was larger the **closer to failure** sets ended [5]. For maximum strength, heavier sets do better [2][3][4]. For muscular endurance, the ACSM advises **light weights for many reps** with **short rests** [1].
:::

## How many reps should a beginner do?

The ACSM position stand advises people who have never trained, or have not trained for several years, to use loads they can lift for 8 to 12 reps [1]. In the full text, the advice for muscle growth adds 1 to 3 sets per exercise at about 70 to 85% of the most you can lift once. For strength, it names a lighter 60 to 70% for 8 to 12 reps for beginners and intermediate lifters [1].

That gives you several reps per set to practise the movement, with a weight that still challenges you. The ACSM pairs it with a rule for moving up: add 2 to 10% to the weight once you can do one or two reps more than you aimed for, on two sessions in a row [1]. See [[progressive-overload]].

## Which rep range builds the most muscle?

Several meta-analyses found similar muscle growth across a wide range of loads.

- A 2017 meta-analysis of 21 studies compared lighter loads (60% of your one-rep max or less) with heavier ones. With every set taken to failure, muscle growth was similar [2].
- A 2021 network meta-analysis of 28 studies split loads into low (more than 15 reps per set), moderate (9 to 15) and high (8 or fewer). Again with every set to failure, it found no difference in growth [3].
- A 2023 network meta-analysis of 119 studies on muscle size found that all the prescriptions it compared built muscle to a similar degree [4].

The condition in the first two is that sets went to failure. How close you get matters: a 2024 exploratory analysis found more growth when sets ended closer to failure [5]. With a light weight, that means pushing the set until the last reps are hard. See [[hypertrophy]].

For advanced lifters who want to build muscle, the ACSM's full text advises 70 to 100% of one-rep max for 1 to 12 reps, used over time, with most training in the 6 to 12 range [1]. Beginners and intermediate lifters stay at 8 to 12 [1].

## Are high reps less effective?

They work for growth, with two practical downsides. A set of 25 takes longer than a set of 8, and judging how close you are to failure is harder. In a meta-analysis of studies where people predicted how many reps they had left, predictions were less accurate in sets that went beyond 12 reps [6].

So high reps are a fine option for exercises where heavy weights feel awkward or hurt your joints. Just make sure you count honestly and take the set close to failure.

## Which rep range builds the most strength?

Heavier weights. The 2017 meta-analysis found bigger gains in one-rep max strength with heavier loads [2]. The 2021 analysis found bigger strength gains with high and moderate loads than with low loads, with a non-significant edge for high over moderate [3]. In the 2023 network meta-analysis, prescriptions with loads above 80% of one-rep max gave the most strength [4].

In the summary of its position stand, the ACSM advises intermediate and advanced lifters to use a wider range of 1 to 12 reps, used in a planned way over time, with the emphasis eventually on heavy loads of 1 to 6 reps and 3 to 5 minutes of rest between sets. The full text advises at least 2 to 3 minutes of rest for heavy core exercises such as the squat and bench press, at every level. In its full text, the heavy end, 80 to 100% of one-rep max, is for advanced lifters [1]. See [[periodization]].

## What are high reps for?

Muscular endurance: being able to keep going for many reps. In the summary of its position stand, the ACSM advises light to moderate weights (40 to 60% of your one-rep max) for more than 15 reps, with short rests of under 90 seconds. The detailed recommendations in the full text differ by level. Beginners and intermediate lifters use relatively light weights for 10 to 15 reps. Advanced lifters use several sets of 10 to 25 reps or more, varied over time. Rests stay short: 1 to 2 minutes after sets of 15 to 20 reps or more, and under a minute after sets of 10 to 15 [1]. That is useful if your sport or job asks you to keep going for many repetitions.

:::checklist
- Start | Pick a weight you can lift 8 to 12 times with good form [1].
- Effort | End most sets with only a few reps left in the tank [5].
- Strength | Want a bigger one-rep max once you have trained for a while? Include heavier sets of 1 to 6 reps over time [1].
- Endurance | Want to keep going longer? Use light weights for many reps with short rests [1].
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Stopping a light set far from failure.** Light weights build muscle when sets are hard [2][3][5]. Twelve reps with a weight you could lift 30 times leave 18 in the tank.
- **Believing only 8 to 12 builds muscle.** Growth was similar across a wide range of loads when sets went to failure [2][3].
- **Only training light if you want to get strong.** Heavier loads gave bigger strength gains [2][3][4].
- **Treating a rep range as fixed.** Once you pass the top of the range, add weight and work back up from the bottom. See [[progressive-overload]].
