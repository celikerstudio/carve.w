## What is melatonin?

Melatonin is a hormone from the pineal gland in your brain that helps regulate your body's rhythms [5]. Supplements are used to fall asleep faster and to cope with jet lag. This article covers what two European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) opinions say about both, and how the Netherlands treats melatonin.

:::takeaway
EFSA found that melatonin shortens the time to fall asleep, at **1 mg close to bedtime** [1]. It found that melatonin reduces the feeling of jet lag at **0.5 to 5 mg** close to bedtime on the day you travel and the first days after you arrive [2]. These are the conditions EFSA set for the effect, not a recommendation to take it. In the Netherlands, the health inspectorate (IGJ) in principle does not see a product under **0.3 mg a day** as a medicine [3].
:::

## Does melatonin help you fall asleep faster?

Yes, a bit. EFSA concluded that melatonin shortens the time it takes to fall asleep. To get that effect, it says, 1 mg should be taken close to bedtime. The target population is the general population [1].

How much faster? A meta-analysis of placebo-controlled trials in 1,683 people with sleep disorders found that they fell asleep about 7 minutes faster (95% CI 4 to 10) [4]. Those were people with sleep problems, not healthy sleepers, so your own gain may differ. The authors call the effect modest [4].

Melatonin is not a fix for a bad routine. If you sleep badly because of late caffeine, bright screens or an irregular schedule, start there. See [[sleep-hygiene]] and [[sleep]].

## Does melatonin help against jet lag?

EFSA concluded that melatonin reduces the feeling of jet lag. The dose it names is between 0.5 and 5 mg, taken close to bedtime on the first day of travel, on any later travel days, and on the first few days after arrival [2].

A Cochrane review of ten trials points the same way. Nine of the ten found that melatonin taken close to bedtime at the destination reduced jet lag after flights across five or more time zones. Doses between 0.5 and 5 mg were similarly effective, although people fell asleep faster and slept better on 5 mg than on 0.5 mg [5]. The review dates from 2002, so newer trials are not in it. It recommends melatonin for adult travellers only [5].

## What dose did EFSA look at?

It depends on the reason.

- To fall asleep: 1 mg, close to bedtime [1].
- Against jet lag: between 0.5 and 5 mg, close to bedtime, on the travel day and the first few days after arrival [2].

These are the conditions under which EFSA accepted that the effect exists. EFSA only says "close to bedtime", with no exact number of minutes [1][2]. The Cochrane review found that doses above 5 mg appear to be no more effective for jet lag [5]. It says occasional short-term use appears to be safe [5]. For taking it every night for weeks or months, ask a doctor.

## How does the Netherlands treat melatonin?

Whether a melatonin product counts as a medicine depends on the product, and the dose is one of the criteria. The IGJ in principle does not see a product with a daily dose below 0.3 mg as a medicine. From 0.3 mg a day, current science says melatonin has a meaningful pharmacological effect on the body, and a product can then be seen as a medicine if it also meets other criteria set by the courts [3]. So "above 0.3 mg is a medicine" is too simple. The dose is one of the criteria the IGJ uses, not the only one [3].

The EFSA claim "reduces the time needed to fall asleep" is allowed on a food supplement with at least 1 mg of melatonin. The IGJ says that does not settle whether a product is a medicine: the claims list only covers foods, so a product with such a claim can still be seen as a medicine, depending on the product [3].

Some melatonin products are registered medicines. They are used for insomnia in older people, for insomnia in children and adolescents with autism or Smith-Magenis syndrome, or for short-term treatment of jet lag in adults [3]. Registered products are sold on prescription or without prescription at a pharmacy or drugstore. Unregistered products are sold in drugstores, pharmacies and supermarkets [3].

## Who should be careful with melatonin?

Timing matters. The Cochrane review notes that melatonin taken at the wrong time, early in the day, is liable to cause sleepiness and delay adaptation to local time [5]. It also reports case reports suggesting that people with epilepsy, and people taking the blood thinner warfarin, may come to harm from it [5]. That review recommends melatonin for adult travellers only [5], and in the Netherlands the registered children's melatonin medicine is for insomnia in children with autism or Smith-Magenis syndrome [3]. The sources say nothing about pregnancy or about medicines other than warfarin, so the rest is advice:

- Ask a doctor or pharmacist first if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, if you take medication (certainly warfarin), if you have epilepsy, or if you want to give it to a child.
- Do not drive or use machinery until you know how it affects you.
- If you still sleep badly after a few weeks, see a doctor. Poor sleep can have a cause that melatonin does not touch.

- Falling asleep | EFSA's condition for the effect: **1 mg** close to bedtime [1].
- Jet lag | EFSA's condition for the effect: **0.5 to 5 mg** close to bedtime, on the travel day and the first days after arrival [2].
- First | Fix light, caffeine and your schedule. See [[sleep-hygiene]].

## Common mistakes

- **Taking more because it works a little.** EFSA's conditions for the effect are 1 mg for sleep and 0.5 to 5 mg for jet lag [1][2], and for jet lag doses above 5 mg appear to work no better [5].
- **Expecting a lot.** About 7 minutes faster, in people with sleep disorders [4].
- **Using it to cover up a bad routine.** Late caffeine and screens still cost you sleep. See [[caffeine]].
