## What does stretching do?

Stretching is the use of positions or movements that put a muscle and the tissue around it under stretch. The main thing it does well is increase your range of motion, how far a joint can move. A Delphi consensus of 20 international stretching experts, written about healthy people and not patients, says stretching improves range of motion, both right after a stretch and over weeks of regular stretching. It adds that alternatives exist [1]. That panel is the main source here, together with the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) [2].

:::takeaway
Stretching is for **range of motion**. It is not a primary way to build muscle, speed up recovery or prevent injuries. The expert panel recommends at least **2 bouts of 5 to 30 seconds** to gain range right away [1]. The ACSM recommends flexibility work on at least **2 days a week**, **60 seconds per exercise** in total, for each major muscle group [2]. Do not hold long static stretches right before a maximal or explosive effort of that muscle [1].
:::

## How long and how often should you stretch?

It depends on the goal, and two sources give different numbers because they answer different questions.

For range of motion right after stretching, the panel recommends at least 2 bouts of 5 to 30 seconds. It does not favour a particular technique, since all of them show similar effects [1].

To build flexibility over time, the panel advises static stretching or PNF (a method that alternates contracting and relaxing a muscle) over dynamic stretching. It recommends 2 to 3 sets daily, each held for 30 to 120 seconds per muscle, to reach the highest weekly volume [1].

The ACSM position stand of 2011 gives a base for apparently healthy adults. Its summary says that, crucial to maintaining joint range of movement, a series of flexibility exercises for each major muscle-tendon group (a total of 60 seconds per exercise) on at least 2 days a week is recommended [2]. The numbers differ from the panel's because they answer different questions: the ACSM gives an achievable weekly base for general fitness, the panel says what to do if you want to gain as much range as possible. The panel's dose is aimed at reaching the highest possible weekly volume [1]. This is from the summary of the stand only, so this article does not say what the full text advises for gaining range.

## Should you stretch before lifting?

Only briefly. The panel does not recommend prolonged static stretching (over 60 seconds per muscle) before maximal or explosive contractions in isolated muscle groups. Its example is that static calf stretching should be avoided before maximal strength efforts. It says that short static stretching inside a dynamic warm-up, and dynamic stretching, do not cause that problem [1].

The panel also says that stretching in a warm-up is not essential, but a viable option to raise range of motion in healthy people, and that short static and dynamic stretching in a warm-up is very unlikely to cause adverse effects on average [1]. For the rest of the warm-up, see [[warm-up]].

## Does stretching build muscle or strength?

Barely. The panel agreed that stretch training does not contribute substantively to muscle growth, and it does not recommend stretching as a primary strategy to enhance muscle mass or strength [1]. In its full text it adds that very long daily static stretching has shown small gains: it recommends more than 15 minutes per day per muscle for at least 6 weeks, only where resistance exercise is not feasible or undesired, and it says the effects are small compared with the time invested [1]. For strength it says the same for people unable or unwilling to do resistance exercise [1]. See [[hypertrophy]] for what does build muscle.

## Does stretching help recovery or prevent injury?

Not as a main strategy, according to the same panel. It does not recommend stretching as a post-exercise recovery routine, with low confidence in the evidence. That includes sore muscles: see [[doms]]. It also does not recommend stretching for injury prevention in general. It notes that initial evidence suggests static stretching may reduce muscle injuries, but possibly at the expense of other types of injury, such as bone and joint injuries [1]. See [[injury-prevention]].

## What about mobility?

Mobility is a looser word than range of motion, and this article only covers what the sources say. They say two things. Stretching increases range of motion [1]. And it is not the only way: the panel says alternatives can be used for chronic gains, and names foam rolling and eccentric resistance training as options. It also cites research in which resistance training through the full range of motion improved range at least as much, especially when the lowering phase was emphasised, and says that for healthy, young adults the choice of method can be left to individual preference [1]. For more on mobility work, see [[mobility]] and [[foam-rolling]].

## What can you do in the gym?

If you stretch, keep long holds away from your heaviest sets. Stretch after training or on a separate day, and keep the pre-lift version short and part of a dynamic warm-up [1]. If you want more range for a lift, the panel's numbers above are the thing to try.

:::checklist
- Range of motion | At least 2 bouts of 5 to 30 s for an acute gain [1].
- Over time | 2 to 3 sets daily of 30 to 120 s per muscle, static or PNF [1].
- Base | ACSM: 60 s per exercise in total, for each major muscle group, on 2 or more days a week [2].
- Before maximal or explosive efforts | Not over 60 s static per muscle. Short holds in a dynamic warm-up do not cause the problem [1].
- Not a main tool for | Muscle growth, recovery or injury prevention [1].
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Long static holds right before a maximal or explosive effort.** The panel advises against over 60 s per muscle [1].
- **Stretching to build muscle.** The panel finds no substantial contribution, and only very long daily stretching showed small gains [1].
- **Counting on stretching to keep you injury-free.** The panel does not recommend it for injury prevention in general [1].
- **Mixing up the numbers.** The ACSM's 60 s per exercise and the panel's doses answer different questions [1][2].
