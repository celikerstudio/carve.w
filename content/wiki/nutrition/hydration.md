## How much water do you need a day?

Water carries nutrients, cools you through sweat and keeps your blood volume up. How much you need depends on your size, the weather and how much you move, so there is no exact number for everyone.

:::takeaway
The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) sets an adequate intake of about **2.0 litres of water a day for women and 2.5 litres for men**, counting water from food as well as drinks [3]. You need more when you sweat. During training, aim not to lose more than **2% of your bodyweight** in sweat [1], and do not drink more than you lose during the session [2]. Coffee and tea count [5][6][7].
:::

Those amounts include water from food, and they apply to moderate temperatures and moderate activity [3]. Hot weather and hard training push your needs up.

A practical check is the colour of your urine. In three studies, urine colour tracked how concentrated the urine was closely enough to use as a field check, though not as a precise measurement [9]. Pale yellow most of the day is fine; if it is dark, drink more.

## Does dehydration hurt your training?

Yes, somewhat. The ACSM, the American College of Sports Medicine, says the goal of drinking during exercise is to avoid losing more than 2% of your bodyweight in water, because more than that can compromise performance [1]. For an 80 kg person, 2% is 1.6 kg.

For strength and power, a meta-analysis of 28 studies found that dehydration lowered muscle strength by about 5.5%, muscle endurance by 8.3% and anaerobic power by 5.8% [4]. The drop looked smaller in trained people, though that difference was not statistically clear [4]. The longer, hotter and sweatier your session, the more this matters.

## How do you know how much you sweat?

Weigh yourself before and after training. Sweat rates differ a lot from person to person, and the ACSM advises this simple test to estimate yours [1]. Every kilo you lose in a session is about a litre of sweat; add what you drank during it and subtract any urine you passed [2].

## What should you drink after training?

During training, drink no more than you lose [2]. After training it is the other way round: after a very sweaty session, drink a bit more than you lost, and have some salt with it. When you need to recover fast, within about four hours, the National Athletic Trainers' Association (NATA) says up to 150% of the fluid you lost may be needed, with enough sodium [2]. In a study of 12 men who lost 2% of their bodyweight in sweat, they needed to drink more than they had lost to get back to normal fluid balance. When the drink was low in sodium, much of the extra simply came out as urine [8]. Eating a salted meal with your drink is one way to get that sodium.

Some drinks stay in your body longer than water. In a study of 72 well-hydrated men at rest, without training beforehand, milk and an oral rehydration solution were retained better than water over four hours. Coffee, tea, cola, beer, orange juice and a sports drink were no different from water [7]. During exercise, the ACSM notes, drinks with electrolytes and carbohydrate can be better than water in some situations [1].

## Does coffee dehydrate you?

Not at normal amounts. The ISSN, a sports nutrition society, concludes that caffeine at the recommended doses does not noticeably affect your hydration [5]. It may cause a mild extra loss of fluid, but most research finds it does not impair hydration, even when exercising in the heat [5].

In a study of 50 men who drank coffee every day, four cups a day (4 mg of caffeine per kg of bodyweight in total) kept them as well hydrated over three days as the same amount of water [6]. In the study of 72 men, tea and coffee were no different from water either [7]. So your coffee and tea count towards your daily fluids. See [[caffeine]].

## Can you drink too much water?

Yes. Drinking more than you sweat out during exercise can dilute the sodium in your blood, a condition called hyponatremia, and the NATA warns about both too little and too much fluid [2]. Its advice is not to drink more than you lose during exercise [2]. The risk is mostly in long endurance events, especially those lasting more than 4 hours; it is uncommon in team sports [2].

The aim is to replace what you lose, not to drink as much as possible.

## Your checklist

:::checklist
- Daily | Around 2 litres (women) to 2.5 litres (men) from drinks and food, more in heat or with hard training.
- Check | Pale yellow urine most of the day.
- Training | Weigh before and after a long or sweaty session to learn your sweat rate.
- During | Drink no more than you lose in sweat.
- After | Drink a bit more than you lost, with some salt or a meal.
- Coffee | Counts towards your fluids.
:::
