## What is goal setting?

Goal setting means deciding in advance what you want to reach, such as training three times a week, losing 8 kg or squatting your bodyweight. A goal gives you something to plan around and something to measure. This article covers what research says about which goals help and how to work towards them.

:::takeaway
Setting a goal helps: in **45 studies**, programs built around physical activity goals got people moving more than control groups without goals [1]. Plan when, where and how you will act [6], and write down your progress [7]. Programs that built up activity step by step also worked, though always together with goals and planning [3]. Starting small is common advice, though the review that gives it did not test it [4].
:::

## Does setting a goal actually help?

Yes, on average. A meta-analysis of 45 articles with 5,912 people found that goal setting programs increased physical activity compared with control groups that set no goals, with a medium effect [1]. Those programs combined goals with other techniques, so the effect is not from the goal alone [1]. A broader meta-analysis looked at goal setting on its own, separate from other techniques, across many kinds of behaviour. It found a smaller but real effect [2].

So a goal is a useful tool, but it does not do the work for you. The techniques below make it more likely that the goal turns into action.

## How big should your goal be?

Big enough to stretch you, but start from where you are. That sounds like a contradiction, and the research does not fully settle it.

On one side, the broad meta-analysis found that goal setting worked best when the goal was difficult [2]. On the other, a meta-analysis of 39 trials on graded tasks, where people build up physical activity in steps, found that these programs made people more active than control groups, with a medium effect [3]. In all those trials, the steps came together with goal setting and planning, so the effect of the steps on their own is unknown, and the certainty of the evidence was rated low [3]. No trial here compared starting small with starting at the full goal. A review on habits adds the advice to begin with modest, attainable goals with an end date, but that advice comes from its introduction and the review did not test it [4].

One way to combine both, as advice: set a goal for a few months from now that is a real challenge, and make this week's step one you expect to manage. For how long habits and results take, see [[realistic-expectations]].

## Do goals need to be SMART?

Not necessarily. SMART stands for specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and time-bound, and it is the most common way to write a goal. A review of its use in physical activity concludes that the acronym is not based on scientific theory, is not consistent with the evidence, is applied inconsistently and may even do harm [5]. The review does not say goals are useless. It says this checklist is not the proven method it is often presented as [5].

What you can take from it: a clear, measurable goal makes it easy to see your progress, which helps (see below). Whether your goal ticks every letter matters less.

## How do you turn a goal into action?

Make a plan. A meta-analysis of 35 trials found that planning improved physical activity, with a small to medium effect [6]. Planning in those trials meant deciding when, where and how you will be active, sometimes combined with deciding in advance what you will do when something gets in the way [6]. Planning only the when, where and how worked at least as well as adding the obstacle plan [6]. Most trials measured activity with questionnaires; in the few that used step counters or activity trackers, the effect was small and not significant [6].

For example: "Monday, Wednesday and Friday at 18:00 I go to the gym on my way home from work. If I have to work late, I do a 20-minute session at home." Linking a habit to a fixed moment helps it stick, see [[consistency]].

## Why track your progress?

Because checking your progress is one of the things that makes goals work. A meta-analysis of 138 studies with 19,951 people found that interventions that got people to monitor their progress helped them reach their goals [7]. The effect was larger when people physically recorded their progress or shared it with others [7].

Write down your workouts, your weight or your food, in a notebook or an app. See [[progress-tracking]]. How often you review and adjust the goal itself has no fixed rule in these studies. Looking back every few weeks gives you enough data to see a trend without reacting to one bad day.

:::checklist
- Goal | Pick one or two goals that stretch you over a few months.
- Step | Make this week's step one you expect to manage.
- Plan | Decide when, where and how, and what you do when something gets in the way [6].
- Track | Record your progress, on paper or in an app [7].
- Share | Telling someone about your goal or progress can help [2][7].
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Setting a goal and stopping there** - Plans and tracking are what turn a goal into action [6][7].
- **Keeping your progress in your head** - Monitoring had a larger effect when progress was physically recorded [7].
- **Polishing the SMART form instead of acting** - The acronym itself has little scientific backing [5].
- **Too many goals at once** - Pick the one or two that matter most and plan those well.
