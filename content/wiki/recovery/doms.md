## What is DOMS?

DOMS stands for delayed onset muscle soreness: the stiff, tender feeling in a muscle that shows up after training, not during it. A 2025 umbrella review says it commonly follows intense and unaccustomed exercise, and that it comes with less muscle strength, more pain and inflammation [1]. What causes it is not settled. A 2003 review calls the mechanisms uncertain and lists six theories [2]. A 2018 meta-analysis points to muscle damage and inflammation [3]. The stretching panel says processes such as inflammation are not related to DOMS symptoms, and that DOMS seems to originate from the deep fascia, the connective tissue around the muscle [4]. That view rests on a newer line of research that is still a hypothesis. Eccentric work, where the muscle lengthens under load (the lowering part of a squat or curl), causes more micro-injury than other kinds of contraction [2].

:::takeaway
**Stretching does not fix DOMS.** An expert panel does not recommend stretching as a recovery routine after training, with low confidence in the evidence. For soreness it says stretching lacks evidence and a theoretical basis [4]. Several other methods, such as massage, have lowered soreness in trials [3], but most of the reviews that summarise this research are of low methodological quality [1].
:::

## When do you get it?

It is most common when you do something your muscles are not used to. A review from 2003 says DOMS is most common when athletes return after a break, and when they first try a new kind of activity [2]. Trials still measured differences in soreness four days (96 hours) after exercise, so it can last for days [1][3]. This article found no source that gives a reliable peak time, so it names none. The stretching panel calls DOMS a marker of unaccustomed training, more likely in less trained people, and not a common thing after regular training sessions [4].

The same 2003 review says that its authors would introduce eccentric exercise or a new activity gradually over 1 to 2 weeks [2]. That is the authors' advice from older research, not a tested rule, but it fits with building up slowly in [[progressive-overload]].

## Does stretching help against sore muscles?

No. The Delphi consensus of 20 stretching experts, written about healthy people, does not recommend stretching as a post-exercise recovery routine. It adds that confidence in the evidence is low [4]. Its remark on DOMS is stronger: stretching for soreness lacks evidence and also lacks a theoretical foundation. It grants that stretching may be of psychological value for some people [4]. If it feels good to you, that is a fair reason to do it, as long as you do not expect it to speed up recovery.

The reviews of trials agree. A meta-analysis of 99 studies found no change in soreness with stretching, and no change with electrostimulation [3]. The umbrella review calls the evidence for stretching weak [1]. The panel adds that existing reviews show no harm from stretching after exercise, so using it may be left to individual preference [4]. For stretching in general, see [[stretching]].

## What has been tested instead?

Many things, and the picture is messy. The umbrella review pooled 29 systematic reviews of 863 trials in healthy adults. Seventeen of the 29 reviews were of critically low quality. The authors speak of some strong evidence for cooling, cryostimulation, contrast therapy, massage, phototherapy and kinesiotaping, at different points in time after the session. Their own evidence map is more modest: none of the treatments reached Class I of the five classes, only contrast therapy right after exercise reached Class II, and the rest were Class III or IV [1]. The meta-analysis of 99 studies found that active recovery, massage, compression garments, immersion, contrast water therapy and cryotherapy lowered soreness, from small to large effects. It named massage as the most effective method, in one meta-analysis. Participants knew which treatment they got, and the authors say a placebo effect could not be ruled out [3]. See also [[massage]].

On light exercise the sources disagree. The 2003 review calls exercise the most effective way to ease the pain during DOMS, but only for a while [2], while the umbrella review calls the evidence for exercises weak [1].

Keep two limits in mind. These trials measured how sore people felt, not how fast they recovered or grew. And much of the evidence is low quality, so treat any single method as a comfort measure and not as a fix [1].

## Is being sore a sign of a good workout?

Not as far as these sources go. The stretching panel calls DOMS a marker of unaccustomed training: it is more consistent with unusual exercise in mode or dose, and it should not be common after regular training sessions [4]. So soreness mainly tells you the session was unfamiliar. A review on muscle growth also argues that muscle damage is not what drives growth: its authors say programs that cause little damage gave similar hypertrophy and strength gains as programs that cause damage at first [5].

:::check
Soreness is a poor yardstick for whether a session built muscle. No source here tests soreness as a predictor of growth. The growth review is about muscle damage, not about how sore people felt, it is a narrative review, and the causes of soreness are not settled (see above).
:::

For hypertrophy, see [[hypertrophy]]. A few sore days after a new session is also a different question from weeks of flat performance, which is what [[overtraining]] is about. For recovery in general, see [[sleep]].

## What should you do about it?

The second and third points are suggestions from the authors of a 2003 review, not tested rules.

- Do not count on stretching to get rid of soreness [4].
- Build up new exercises and eccentric work gradually over a week or two [2].
- If you must train again soon, the 2003 review suggests easing off for 1 to 2 days after an intense session, or training other muscles [2].
- If you want to try massage or cold or heat, expect some relief at best [1][3].
- Ordinary soreness is not the same as something wrong. If you have extreme pain, a swollen muscle or dark urine after a hard session, see a doctor.

:::checklist
- Stretching | Not recommended as recovery after training. Fine if you like it [4].
- Build up | Introduce new or eccentric exercise over 1 to 2 weeks [2].
- Massage | Lowered soreness most in one meta-analysis, where a placebo effect could not be ruled out [3]. The wider evidence is weak [1].
- Sore is not a goal | Soreness marks unaccustomed training [4]. No source here shows it means a better session.
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Stretching to get rid of soreness.** The consensus finds no evidence for it [4].
- **Chasing soreness as proof of a good session.** Soreness marks unfamiliar training [4], and damage is not what drives growth, according to the review above [5].
- **Jumping into a new exercise at full volume.** The 2003 review suggests introducing it gradually [2].
