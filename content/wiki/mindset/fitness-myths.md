## Why check fitness myths?

Because a myth costs you time. Hundreds of crunches for a flat stomach, six small meals to speed up your metabolism, or panic about a missed shake after training are all effort that research says does little. This article takes seven common beliefs and shows what the studies found. For each one, the useful part is what to do instead.

:::takeaway
Habits take **two to five months**, not 21 days [1]. You cannot burn fat in one spot by training that spot [2]. Eating more often does not appear to speed up your metabolism [4], and carbs do not stop fat loss when calories and protein are equal [5]. Your **daily protein total** matters more than the minutes after training [6], and higher protein has shown no harm in healthy people who train [7].
:::

## Do habits form in 21 days?

No. The 21-day figure is said to come from the self-help book Psycho-Cybernetics by Maxwell Maltz [1]. A systematic review of 20 studies on health habits says its results clearly refute the idea that habits form in about 21 days [1]. The review advises planning for at least two to five months before a new health habit becomes automatic [1]. People differ a lot: in one healthy-eating study in the review, individual estimates ran from 4 to 335 days, but only 23% reached the threshold for a habit [1][9]. That study lasted 84 days, so everything past day 84 was projected from a curve [9]. A 21-day challenge can be a good start. Just do not expect the habit to run on its own when it ends. See [[consistency]] and [[realistic-expectations]].

## Can you burn belly fat with ab exercises?

No. Training a muscle does not burn the fat on top of it. A meta-analysis of 13 studies with 1,158 people compared fat on a trained limb with fat on the untrained limb of the same person. There was no difference: no spot reduction [2]. In a trial with 24 sedentary adults, six weeks of abdominal exercises five days a week did not reduce belly fat, waist size or body fat compared with a group that did not train. Their abdominal endurance did improve [3].

To lose belly fat, lose fat overall with a [[calorie-deficit]]. Train your abs for strength and endurance.

## Does eating more often speed up your metabolism?

It does not appear to. The ISSN, a sports nutrition society, concludes in its position stand on meal frequency that eating more often does not appear to significantly raise the energy you burn digesting food, your total energy use or your resting metabolic rate [4]. In people who do not exercise, it also did not appear to improve body composition [4]. For athletes who are dieting and eat enough protein, the ISSN says more frequent meals may help preserve lean mass [4]. Eat as often as suits your hunger and your day. See [[meal-timing]] and [[metabolism]].

## Do carbs make you fat?

Not on their own. The ISSN position stand on diets says that no controlled feeding study, with equal calories and equal protein, has found a clinically meaningful fat loss advantage for low-carb or ketogenic diets [5]. A wide range of diets, from low-fat to low-carb, can work about equally well for body composition [5]. Low-carb can still help some people, because the ISSN notes that ketogenic diets can reduce appetite [5]. That works through eating less. See [[carbohydrates]] and [[flexible-dieting]].

## Do you need protein right after training?

No. A meta-analysis of 23 studies looked at protein taken within an hour before or after training. Once total protein intake was taken into account, that timing made no significant difference to muscle growth or strength [6]. How much protein you ate in total was the strongest predictor of growth [6]. Eat a protein-rich meal some time before or after training, and hit your daily total. See [[post-workout-nutrition]] and [[protein]].

## Is a high-protein diet bad for your kidneys?

There is no evidence of harm in healthy people who exercise. The ISSN position stand on protein says there is no controlled scientific evidence that higher protein intakes pose health risks in healthy people who exercise, and that reviews consistently find no sign of kidney or liver harm in athletes and active people [7]. It also says that people with kidney failure do benefit from eating less protein [7]. So if you have kidney disease, ask your doctor before raising your protein.

## Is breakfast the key to losing weight?

No. A meta-analysis of randomised trials in adults found that people assigned to eat breakfast did not lose more weight. They weighed 0.44 kg more than people who skipped it and ate about 260 kcal a day more [8]. The trials were short and mostly of low quality [8]. Eat breakfast if you like it and it keeps you full. Skipping it did not hurt weight loss in these trials [8].

## How do you spot the next myth?

There is no study on this, so take it as a habit of mind. Ask what the claim promises and how big the effect is. Ask whether it was tested in people like you, or in a lab, in mice or in one small group. Be wary when a claim offers a shortcut around the basics: a calorie deficit for fat loss, enough protein, and training that gets harder over time ([[progressive-overload]]).

:::checklist
- Habits | Plan on two to five months, not 21 days [1].
- Belly fat | Lose fat overall; ab training does not target it [2][3].
- Meals | Eat as often as suits you; it does not appear to change your metabolism [4].
- Carbs | Equal calories and protein, equal fat loss [5].
- Protein | Daily total first, timing second [6].
- Breakfast | Optional for weight loss [8].
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Giving up on a habit after three weeks** - Most habits take months [1].
- **Doing crunches to lose belly fat** - Six weeks of ab training did not reduce belly fat [3].
- **Cutting carbs as if they were the problem** - With calories and protein equal, low-carb gave no clear fat loss advantage [5].
- **Chugging a shake in the changing room while missing the daily total** - Total protein predicted growth, timing did not [6].
