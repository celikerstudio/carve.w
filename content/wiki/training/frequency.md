## What is training frequency?

Training frequency is how often you train. It can mean two different things, and it helps to keep them apart.

- **Days per week** - how many days you go to the gym or train at home.
- **Times per muscle per week** - how many of those days hit a given muscle. Three full-body sessions train your legs three times. Three days of a push, pull and legs split train them once.

:::takeaway
The ACSM advises **2 to 3 training days a week** as a beginner, **3 to 4** after about six months of steady training, and **4 to 5** as an advanced lifter [1]. How you spread the work matters less than the total: with the same weekly sets, training a muscle more often did not give significantly more growth [2].
:::

## How many days a week should you train?

The ACSM position stand on resistance training links the number of training days to your experience [1]:

- **Beginner** (no experience, or not trained for several years) - 2 to 3 days a week.
- **Intermediate** (about six months of steady training) - 3 to 4 days a week.
- **Advanced** (years of training) - 4 to 5 days a week. The detailed recommendations in the full text give 4 to 6 [1].

For health alone, the bar is lower. The WHO advises adults to do muscle-strengthening work that involves all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week [5]. See [[physical-activity]].

More days is not a goal in itself. The ACSM ties more days to more experience [1], and extra days mainly give you room to spread out more work. A beginner who trains three times a week is following the advice for their level.

## How often should you train each muscle?

For muscle growth, how often matters less than how much. A 2019 meta-analysis of 25 studies found no significant difference in growth between training a muscle more or less often when the weekly volume was the same. That held for people with training experience too [2]. A 2026 meta-regression, with mostly young men as participants, took weekly volume into account and found that any effect of frequency on growth was small and uncertain [4].

When volume was not equal, the 2019 analysis did find a small edge for training more often, and the authors call the difference between one and three or more days a week modest [2]. Their conclusion: for a given volume, you can choose how often to train a muscle based on personal preference [2]. So the question to ask is how many hard sets each muscle gets per week, see [[volume]], and then which split lets you do them well.

Training each muscle twice a week is a fine way to do that, but the studies above do not show that twice beats once at the same volume. Pick what fits your week.

## Does training more often make you stronger?

Here the picture is a bit different. In a 2018 meta-analysis of 22 studies, strength gains rose with frequency. When the weekly volume was equal, the effect was no longer significant, so the authors concluded the benefit seems to come mainly from the extra volume. Most of those studies used untrained people [3].

The 2026 meta-regression came to a different conclusion. After taking weekly volume into account, strength still went up with frequency, with smaller gains for each extra day. The authors note themselves that this contradicts earlier meta-analyses, including the 2018 one [4]. If getting stronger on a specific lift is your goal, doing it on more days of the week is a reasonable option.

## How do you choose a split?

Start from the number of days you can keep up for months. Then pick a split that trains every major muscle with enough sets over the week.

- **2 or 3 days** - full-body sessions work well. Each muscle gets trained on every training day. See [[full-body]].
- **4 days** - an upper and lower split trains each muscle twice. See [[upper-lower]].
- **5 or 6 days** - a push, pull and legs split, run once or twice through the week. See [[push-pull-legs]].

If a session gets so long that your last exercises suffer, that is a sign to spread the sets over more days. If you keep missing sessions, fewer days that you actually do beat a plan you cannot keep.

:::checklist
- Days | Pick the number of days you can keep up: 2 to 3 as a beginner [1].
- Sets | Count your hard sets per muscle per week. That total matters more than how you split it [2].
- Split | Choose a split that hits every major muscle group each week [5].
- Adjust | Sessions too long? Add a day. Missing sessions? Drop one.
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Training six days a week from the start.** The ACSM advises 2 to 3 days for beginners [1]. With the same weekly work, more days did not give more muscle [2].
- **Training a muscle once a week with only a few sets.** Frequency on its own matters little, but the total does. If one day means too few sets, add a second day [2].
- **Changing your split to fix a stall.** With the same volume, how often you trained a muscle made little difference to growth [2][4]. Look at your sets, your effort and your recovery first. See [[plateaus]].
