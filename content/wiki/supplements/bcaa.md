## What are BCAAs?

BCAAs are three of the nine essential amino acids: leucine, isoleucine and valine. Essential means your body cannot make them, so they have to come from food. Leucine gets most of the attention because it is the main trigger that switches on muscle protein building after you eat [1].

:::takeaway
In one study, 10 trained men who drank 5.6 g of BCAAs after lifting had muscle protein synthesis **22%** higher than on placebo [2]. The ISSN notes that this is about **half** of what other studies find with whey protein that holds a similar amount of BCAAs [1]. The ISSN rates the evidence for BCAAs as limited or mixed, and many authors advise whole protein sources over loose amino acids [1].
:::

## Do BCAAs build muscle as well as whey?

Not in the short-term measurements we have. In one study, 10 young, resistance-trained men drank 5.6 g of BCAAs or placebo right after lifting, and their muscle protein synthesis over the next 4 hours was 22% higher with BCAAs [2]. The ISSN adds that this rate was about 50% lower than what is commonly seen when whey protein with a similar amount of BCAAs is taken [1]. That is a comparison with other studies, because the BCAA study had no whey group. It also measured protein synthesis for a few hours, not muscle growth. Whey contains the BCAAs and also the other essential amino acids. Muscle protein synthesis needs all of them. One reason is that leucine can start the process, but the other amino acids are the material it is built from [3].

The ISSN protein review says the same in different words. A higher total dose of all the essential amino acids seems best for keeping muscle protein synthesis going, and leucine on its own is not the way to do it [3].

## Do BCAAs help you gain or keep muscle?

The idea makes sense on paper. BCAAs could limit muscle breakdown during hard training and so give you more lean mass, or lose less of it. The ISSN says only limited evidence supports that [1], and it lists BCAAs under limited or mixed evidence, for muscle building and for performance [1].

The studies it describes point in different directions [1]:

- Trekking at altitude for 21 days, about 10 g a day: fat-free mass rose 1.5%, with no change on placebo.
- Untrained people, 14 g a day for 30 days: muscle mass rose 1.3% and grip strength 8.1%.
- 8 weeks of resistance training, 9 g of BCAAs against placebo: no difference in body composition or muscle performance.

The ISSN's reading is that the mechanism looks plausible, but the studies do not consistently show that you need BCAA supplements [1].

## Do BCAAs help with soreness or fatigue?

The ISSN reports that several studies support BCAAs for recovery after muscle-damaging exercise, with less soreness and less loss of force [1]. Whether they improve endurance performance is unclear: the results are mixed [1]. Those are different goals from building muscle.

## What about free essential amino acids (EAAs)?

EAAs are all nine essential amino acids as a powder, not inside a food. In a single sitting they raise muscle protein synthesis. The ISSN says 6 to 12 g of essential amino acids stimulates muscle protein synthesis [1], and free-form essential amino acids can activate it, as can whey, soy and milk protein [3]. Several studies, but not all, found essential amino acids alone work about as well as a whole protein with the same essential amino acid content [3]. In one study of 15 older adults, 15 g of whey raised leg protein balance, an index of muscle protein gain, while an amino acid mix matching that whey did not [3].

The ISSN's own summary table lists essential amino acids among the supplements with strong evidence for muscle building. Its text is more careful. What is well supported is the short-term rise in muscle protein synthesis. On the long run it says that only limited evidence exists that non-intact sources of essential amino acids, such as free-form amino acids, add fat-free mass during resistance training [1]. That is a reading of its text, the table does not spell it out. For that reason, many authors and review articles encourage intact protein sources over free-form amino acids to gain fat-free mass [1]. The ISSN adds that the effect of this advice on performance has not been determined [1].

## What should you do instead?

Get your protein from food and, if you need it, a protein powder. See [[protein]] for how much, [[whey-protein]] for the powder and [[protein-sources]] for foods. The ISSN advice for athletes is to get enough leucine at each meal by choosing high-quality protein sources, about 1 to 3 g of leucine per meal [3]. A normal serving of those sources gives you the BCAAs with the rest of the amino acids.

## Common mistakes

- **Replacing protein with BCAAs.** BCAAs are 3 of the 9 essential amino acids. The rise in muscle protein synthesis after BCAAs was about half of what other studies find after whey with a similar amount of BCAAs [1].
- **Expecting muscle from them.** The ISSN calls the evidence limited [1].
- **Treating soreness research as muscle-building research.** The recovery findings are about muscle damage [1].
