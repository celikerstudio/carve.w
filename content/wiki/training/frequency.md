## What is training frequency?

Training frequency is how often you train. It can mean two different things, and it helps to keep them apart.

- **Days per week** - how many days you go to the gym or train at home.
- **Times per muscle per week** - how many of those days hit a given muscle. Three full-body sessions train your legs three times. Three days of a push, pull and legs split train them once.

:::takeaway
The ACSM advises training with high effort **at least twice a week**, working all major muscle groups [1]. How you spread the work matters less than the total: with the same weekly sets, training one day a week or more than five days a week made no difference to muscle growth [1].
:::

## How many days a week should you train?

The 2026 ACSM position stand advises healthy adults to do resistance training with high effort at least twice a week, with all major muscle groups being engaged [1]. This is meant for everyone from complete beginners to experienced lifters, and in most cases the reviews looked at people with little or no training experience [1]. It no longer sets a different number of days for each level.

The 2009 position stand did: 2 to 3 days a week for beginners, 3 to 4 for intermediate lifters (about six months of steady training) and 4 to 5 for advanced lifters [6]. The 2026 position stand drops that split by experience [1].

For strength, training on 2 or more days a week did better than less, and the ACSM could not determine from its analysis where the upper limit lies [1]. For health alone, the WHO also advises muscle-strengthening work that involves all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week [5]. See [[physical-activity]].

More days is not a goal in itself. For muscle growth, 1 day versus more than 5 days a week made no difference when total volume was equal [1], so extra days mainly give you room to spread out more work. A beginner who trains three times a week is above the minimum.

## How often should you train each muscle?

For muscle growth, how often matters less than how much. A 2019 meta-analysis of 25 studies found no significant difference in growth between training a muscle more or less often when the weekly volume was the same. That held for people with training experience too [2]. A later meta-regression (published online in 2025), with mostly young men as participants, took weekly volume into account and found that any effect of frequency on growth was small and uncertain [4].

When volume was not equal, the 2019 analysis did find a small edge for training more often, and the authors call the difference between one and three or more days a week modest [2]. Their conclusion: for a given volume, you can choose how often to train a muscle based on personal preference [2]. So the question to ask is how many hard sets each muscle gets per week, see [[volume]], and then which split lets you do them well.

Training each muscle twice a week is a fine way to do that, but the studies above do not show that twice beats once at the same volume. Pick what fits your week.

## Does training more often make you stronger?

Here the picture is a bit different. In a 2018 meta-analysis of 22 studies, strength gains rose with frequency. When the weekly volume was equal, the effect was no longer significant, so the authors concluded the benefit seems to come mainly from the extra volume. Most of those studies used untrained people [3].

The ACSM's 2026 position stand found more strength with training on 2 or more days a week than with fewer, and could not determine an upper limit [1]. It adds that there was too little evidence for a dose-response, and that the effect becomes smaller when weekly volume is equal [1]. The 2018 meta-analysis is one of the reviews it bases this on [3].

A later meta-regression, published online in 2025, came to a different conclusion. After taking weekly volume into account, strength still went up with frequency, with smaller gains for each extra day. The authors note themselves that this contradicts earlier meta-analyses, including the 2018 one [4]. If getting stronger on a specific lift is your goal, doing it on more days of the week is a reasonable option.

## How do you choose a split?

Start from the number of days you can keep up for months. Then pick a split that trains every major muscle with enough sets over the week.

- **2 or 3 days** - full-body sessions work well. Each muscle gets trained on every training day. See [[full-body]].
- **4 days** - an upper and lower split trains each muscle twice. See [[upper-lower]].
- **5 or 6 days** - a push, pull and legs split, run once or twice through the week. See [[push-pull-legs]].

If a session gets so long that your last exercises suffer, that is a sign to spread the sets over more days. If you keep missing sessions, fewer days that you actually do beat a plan you cannot keep.

:::checklist
- Days | Train at least two days a week, with all major muscle groups, and pick the number of days you can keep up [1].
- Sets | Count your hard sets per muscle per week. That total matters more than how you split it [2].
- Split | Choose a split that hits every major muscle group each week [1][5].
- Adjust | Sessions too long? Add a day. Missing sessions? Drop one.
:::

## Common mistakes

- **Thinking you need six days a week.** The ACSM minimum is two days a week [1]. With the same weekly work, more days did not give more muscle [1][2].
- **Training a muscle once a week with only a few sets.** Frequency on its own matters little, but the total does. If one day means too few sets, add a second day [2].
- **Changing your split to fix a stall.** With the same volume, how often you trained a muscle made little difference to growth [1][2][4]. Look at your sets, your effort and your recovery first. See [[plateaus]].
