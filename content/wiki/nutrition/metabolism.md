## What is metabolism?

Metabolism is the energy your body uses in a day. It has three parts: the energy you burn at rest, the energy it costs to digest food, and the energy you spend on activity [4]. Together they make your [[tdee]]. The resting part is your [[bmr]].

Activity is more than workouts. It includes fidgeting and holding your posture, along with the rest of everyday movement. Researchers call this non-exercise activity thermogenesis, or NEAT. It differs a lot between people: when 16 non-obese volunteers were overfed by 1,000 kcal a day for 8 weeks, the change in their NEAT was closely linked to how much fat they gained (r = 0.77) [5].

:::takeaway
After a diet you burn less. Part of that is simply a smaller body. On top of it, the International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) describes an extra drop of **10 to 15% of daily burn**, but most of the research behind that figure used very strict, low-protein diets without training, and better-designed studies find less [2][7]. Eating more often does not speed up your metabolism [1]. Lifting weights may help you keep your resting rate during a diet, but the evidence is thin [2][8][10].
:::

## How much does digesting food cost?

Digesting food costs energy too. On a mixed diet it comes to about 10% of the energy you eat (5 to 15% of daily expenditure in the review's summary). The cost depends on the nutrient. Reported values are 0 to 3% of its own calories for fat, 5 to 10% for carbohydrate and 20 to 30% for protein, with alcohol at 10 to 30% [4]. That is one reason protein is worth eating; see [[protein]].

## Does eating more often speed up your metabolism?

No. The ISSN reviewed the research on meal frequency and concluded that eating more often does not appear to raise the energy cost of digestion, total energy expenditure or resting metabolic rate [1].

The strongest evidence comes from studies where people stayed in a respiration chamber or a metabolic ward. They ate the same calories each day, split over anything from one to seven meals. Four of those studies used people with overweight or obesity and one used people of normal weight. Some lasted a few hours, others several weeks, and none found a higher resting rate or 24-hour energy expenditure from more meals [1]. Athletes were not studied separately [1]. See [[meal-timing]] for what meal spacing does change.

## Does dieting slow your metabolism?

Yes, and there are two reasons. First, a lighter body burns less. [[calories]] explains how that slows weight loss over time.

Second, there may be a slowdown that body size does not explain. The ISSN calls it adaptive thermogenesis. For lean and obese people who keep off at least 10% of their bodyweight it reports a drop of about 20 to 25% in daily energy expenditure. The part beyond what the lost fat and muscle predict is 10 to 15%, and most of it sits in energy spent outside of rest. The causes are unclear [2].

Look at where that comes from. The ISSN's own example is a classic experiment in obese people on an 800 kcal liquid diet with 15% protein, and it warns that such diets do not necessarily show what happens with better protein intake and training. It adds that most research showing the effect combined hard dieting, little protein and no strength training [2].

A systematic review of 33 studies with 2,528 adults found adaptive thermogenesis in 27 of them. The better-designed studies reported lower or non-significant values, and the effect seemed to fade or disappear once weight had been stable for a while [7].

## What happened to the Biggest Loser contestants?

This is the study people quote. Researchers followed 14 contestants of the TV show "The Biggest Loser". They lost 58 kg on average over 30 weeks and regained 41 kg of it over the next six years. At that point their resting metabolic rate was about 500 kcal a day lower than their new body size and age predicted [6].

Two details matter. The slowdown at the end of the show did not predict who regained weight (r = -0.1). And the people who kept more weight off at six years had more slowdown (r = 0.59) [6]. The authors read it as a proportional but incomplete response to the effort to reduce body weight. It is a small group after an extreme and fast weight loss, so do not treat 500 kcal as a typical number.

## Can you damage your metabolism?

The studies do not agree on how much slowdown remains, and they do not show a point where metabolism breaks. In the Biggest Loser follow-up it was still there after six years [6]. In the better-designed studies of the review it was small, or gone once weight was stable [7].

Before blaming your metabolism, check your counting. In a classic study, 10 obese people (nine women and one man) with a history of diet resistance said they ate under 1,200 kcal a day without losing weight. Their resting metabolic rate and total energy expenditure were within 5% of the predicted values. They had underreported their food intake by 47% on average and overreported their activity by 51% [9]. See [[plateaus]] for what to check when progress stops.

## What can you do about it?

Lift weights. The evidence is thin, and it comes from specific groups. The ISSN points to one 12-week trial in untrained people with obesity on an 800 kcal liquid diet. Of 20 people, the 10 who did resistance training three days a week kept their lean mass and resting metabolic rate. The 10 who walked, biked or climbed stairs lost both. Only resting metabolic rate was measured, and the effect of protein was not tested separately [2][10].

A second trial had 94 overweight premenopausal women (BMI 27 to 30) lose about 12 kg. The women who trained with weights kept their fat-free mass and resting energy expenditure. The women who did aerobic training or no exercise lost both [8].

Keep moving. Nothing here proves that steps protect your metabolism on a diet. But NEAT differs a lot between people [5], and keeping your daily steps from sliding costs nothing. See [[physical-activity]].

## Does metabolism slow down with age?

Not before about 60, going by one large dataset. A large database of people aged 8 days to 95 years, measured with the doubly labeled water method, showed that energy expenditure adjusted for fat-free mass stays stable from 20 to 60 years and then declines in older adults [3].

:::checklist
- Weigh | Judge your metabolism by weekly average weight, not by how you feel.
- Lift | Train with weights while you cut.
- Protein | Eat enough protein every day, see [[protein]].
- Move | Keep your daily steps steady.
- Recheck | After losing weight, work out your [[tdee]] again.
:::
