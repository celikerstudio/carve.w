## What is creatine?

Creatine is a compound your body makes itself and also gets from food, mostly red meat and seafood. About 95% of it is stored in your muscles [1]. There it helps rebuild ATP, the fuel for short, hard efforts like a heavy set or a sprint [1].

:::takeaway
Take **3 to 5 g of creatine monohydrate a day**. Your muscles are full after 3 to 4 weeks, or after 5 to 7 days if you start with about **0.3 g per kg a day** (about 20 g for 70 kg) [1]. Combined with strength training it adds a modest amount of strength and lean mass on average, in studies of adults under 50, mostly men [2][3]. Without training it does little for your muscles [4].
:::

Your body breaks down about 1 to 3 g of creatine a day, depending on how much muscle you have. About half of that comes back through food; your liver and kidneys make the rest [1]. A pound (450 g) of raw beef or salmon holds about 1 to 2 g [1].

On a normal diet your muscle stores are about 60 to 80% full. A supplement raises them by another 20 to 40% [1]. Vegetarians start with lower stores and may see a bigger rise [1].

## Does creatine work?

For strength and muscle, yes, with training. The ISSN, a sports nutrition society, calls creatine monohydrate the most effective supplement available for high-intensity exercise capacity and for building lean mass during training [1]. Fuller stores let you do a little more work across sets and sprints, and over weeks that better training adds up [1].

Two recent meta-analyses in adults under 50 put numbers on it. Compared with training alone, creatine added on average 1.14 kg of lean body mass and 0.73 kg less fat mass across 12 trials [2]. Across 23 trials, it added 4.4 kg to upper-body strength and 11.4 kg to lower-body strength compared with a placebo [3]. Older adults gained lean mass from creatine with strength training too [4]. Without exercise, there was no measurable change in lean mass [4].

Part of that lean mass is water. Creatine pulls some water into the muscle, and a loading week can add about 0.5 to 1 litre [1]. In one trial of 63 adults, without a placebo, a week of 5 g a day added 0.5 kg of lean mass before any training started, mostly in the women. Over the next 12 weeks of training, the creatine group gained no more than the control group [5]. The authors think the early gain was a short-term effect on the measurement, and that a maintenance dose above 5 g a day may be needed for extra muscle [5]. So part of what a scan or scale shows in the first week is likely water, not muscle.

Most studies were done in men. In the meta-analyses, men gained significantly in strength and lean mass, while women did not reach significance [3][4]. There were far fewer studies in women, so the honest conclusion is that the effect in women is less certain, not that it is absent [3]. The ISSN also notes that women may gain less [1].

## How much creatine should you take?

There are two ways to fill your muscles. Both end at the same place [1].

- **Without loading:** 3 to 5 g a day. Your stores are full after 3 to 4 weeks [1]. The ISSN notes that the early performance effects of this route are less well supported, which makes sense: it takes longer to get there [1].
- **With loading:** about 0.3 g per kg of bodyweight a day for 5 to 7 days, then 3 to 5 g a day [1]. The ISSN describes loading as 5 g four times a day, which is 20 g [1]. Worked out at 0.3 g per kg, 80 kg comes to 24 g a day.

Once your muscles are full, 3 to 5 g a day keeps them there. Some studies suggest that larger athletes may need 5 to 10 g [1].

Taking creatine with carbohydrate, or with carbohydrate and protein, gets a bit more of it into the muscle. Whether that improves performance is unclear [1]. In the lean-mass meta-analysis, adding a carbohydrate drink did not change the result [2].

:::checklist
- Daily dose | **3 to 5 g** of creatine monohydrate, every day.
- Faster start | Optional: about **0.3 g per kg** a day for 5 to 7 days, split into four doses, then back to 3 to 5 g.
- Mixing | Mix it just before you drink it. Creatine slowly breaks down in liquid.
- Training | Creatine helps you train harder. It does not replace the training.
:::

## Which type of creatine is best?

Creatine monohydrate. It is by far the most studied form and the one shown to work [1]. Creatine citrate, creatine serum, creatine ethyl ester, buffered creatine and creatine nitrate have not been shown to get more creatine into the muscle than monohydrate [1]. Your gut absorbs almost all of a normal monohydrate dose [1], so a more expensive form has nothing to fix.

Creatine is stable as a powder but breaks down in liquid. After 3 days at room temperature, 4 to 21% had turned into creatinine, depending on how acidic the liquid was [1]. That is why it is sold as powder, and why you should not mix a bottle days in advance.

## Is creatine safe?

For healthy people, the research says yes. Over 1,000 studies have been done since the early 1990s [1]. The ISSN found no compelling evidence that creatine monohydrate harms otherwise healthy people, at up to 30 g a day for up to 5 years [1]. Those highest doses over the longest time come from studies in patients with conditions like Parkinson's and Huntington's disease [1]. In healthy people, American football players took 5 to 10 g a day for 21 months with no clinically significant differences in markers of kidney and liver function, muscle enzymes, blood fats or urine. One study of up to 10 g a day, taken for between 10 months and 5 years, found no change in kidney filtration [1].

The only side effect that shows up consistently is weight gain [1]. Well-controlled studies found no increase in muscle injuries, dehydration, muscle cramps or stomach upset, and no support for kidney damage [1].

### Is creatine bad for your kidneys?

Not in healthy people, as far as the trials show. A meta-analysis of 20 randomised trials found that creatine raises creatinine in the blood a little, by 0.13 mg/dL on average. Urea and the estimated kidney filtration rate (eGFR) did not change [6]. Creatinine is the normal breakdown product of creatine [1], so more creatine in, more creatinine out.

That matters for blood tests. Doctors use creatinine to estimate kidney function, so a higher value can look like a kidney problem. Tell your doctor you take creatine before a blood test. The authors of the meta-analysis add that trials longer than a year are still needed [6].

### Does creatine cause hair loss?

There is no good evidence that it does. The idea comes from one study in 20 rugby players, in which DHT, the hormone behind the hair-loss worry [8], rose by 56% after a week of loading at 25 g a day and stayed 40% higher after two more weeks at 5 g [7]. That study did not measure hair. In a 12-week trial, 45 trained men were randomised to 5 g a day without loading or a placebo, and 38 finished. Creatine did not change DHT or any measure of hair growth [8].

## Who should be careful with creatine?

- **Kidney disease:** some researchers advise people with existing kidney disease to talk to their doctor before taking creatine, as a precaution [1]. Do that.
- **Under 18:** the ISSN sees no scientific reason to forbid it, but advises it only for young athletes in serious, supervised training who eat well, understand how to use it and stay within the normal dose [1].
- **Pregnancy:** the ISSN mentions creatine in pregnancy only as a research topic, not as a recommendation [1]. If you are pregnant or breastfeeding, ask your doctor or midwife first.
- **Medication or a health condition:** ask your doctor. The safety research is mostly in healthy people.

One thing to know when you read about creatine: the ISSN position stand was funded by the Council for Responsible Nutrition, a supplement industry group, and several authors work with companies that sell creatine [1]. Meta-analyses by other research groups, on lean mass and on kidneys, reach similar conclusions [2][6].

## What happens when you stop taking creatine?

Your muscle stores drop back to their normal level in 4 to 6 weeks [1]. They do not fall below where they started, so there is no sign that creatine shuts down your own production for good [1].

## Common mistakes

- **Panicking at the scale.** Loading can hold an extra 0.5 to 1 litre of water [1]. If you are cutting, judge your progress on the weeks after that.
- **Paying for a fancy form.** Monohydrate is the form that is proven to work [1].
- **Expecting it to build muscle on its own.** Without training, there was no measurable change in lean mass [4].
